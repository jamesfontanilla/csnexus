# Filing Systems

## Explanations

### Introduction

A filing system is the organized method by which records are arranged, stored, and retrieved in an office. While indexing determines how a record is labeled and classification determines what category it belongs to, the filing system determines the overall arrangement scheme — the logic that governs where every document sits in relation to every other document. Choosing the right filing system is a foundational decision that affects every subsequent retrieval attempt.

In the Philippine Civil Service Examination (CSE) Sub-Professional level, filing system questions test whether an examinee can identify the correct system for a given scenario, apply the rules of each system to arrange records properly, and recognize the advantages and limitations of each approach. Government offices handle diverse records — personnel files, financial transactions, correspondence, and legal documents — and different types of records call for different filing systems.

For government clerks at agencies like the CSC, COA, DILG, and DBM, understanding filing systems is not optional. A clerk who files correspondence alphabetically by sender but financial records numerically by voucher number is applying two different systems — and must know the rules of each. The CSE tests this practical knowledge because misapplied filing systems cause lost documents, failed audits, and delayed public services.

### Why Filing Systems Is Tested in the CSE

- Government offices use multiple filing systems simultaneously for different record types.
- Clerks must select the appropriate system based on the nature and volume of records.
- Retrieval speed depends entirely on consistent application of filing system rules.
- Misapplied filing rules cause misfiled documents that may never be recovered.
- The National Archives of the Philippines (NAP) prescribes filing standards for government agencies.
- Filing system knowledge is essential for both physical cabinets and digital records management.
- Audit compliance requires that records be retrievable through a documented, logical system.
- The skill demonstrates systematic thinking and organizational competence expected of civil servants.

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. Confusing alphabetic filing with simple ABC ordering — ignoring rules for prefixes, particles, and compound names.
2. Assuming numeric filing means filing by date — numeric filing uses assigned numbers, not chronological order.
3. Not recognizing that subject filing requires a pre-established list of subjects (a relative index).
4. Mixing filing system rules — applying alphabetic rules within a numeric system or vice versa.
5. Forgetting that geographic filing still requires alphabetic arrangement within each geographic division.
6. Believing that one filing system is universally "best" — each system suits different situations.
7. Ignoring the need for cross-references when a record could logically be filed under multiple headings.
8. Confusing the filing system (the arrangement method) with the filing equipment (cabinets, folders, shelves).

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define what a filing system is and distinguish it from indexing and classification.
- Describe the four major filing systems: alphabetic, numeric, subject, and geographic.
- Apply the rules of each filing system to arrange records correctly.
- Identify the advantages and disadvantages of each system.
- Determine which filing system is most appropriate for a given office scenario.
- Recognize when a combination of systems is needed.
- Handle cross-references within each filing system.
- Solve filing system problems quickly under CSE time constraints.

### 4.1 Overview of Filing Systems

A filing system is the plan or method used to arrange records so they can be stored systematically and retrieved quickly. Every filing system answers one question: **"In what order should these records be arranged?"**

The four major filing systems are:

| Filing System | Arrangement Basis | Example |
|---------------|------------------|---------|
| Alphabetic | Letters of the alphabet (names, subjects) | Santos before Torres; Accounting before Budget |
| Numeric | Assigned numbers | File 001, File 002, File 003... |
| Subject | Topic or subject heading | All "Leave" documents together |
| Geographic | Location or area | Luzon → Visayas → Mindanao |

Each system has a primary arrangement method and may use a secondary method within groups. For example, a geographic system arranges first by region, then alphabetically by name within each region.

**Key distinction:** The filing system is the *arrangement logic*. The filing method is the *physical process* of placing documents. Do not confuse the two.

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Filing systems work because they impose a single, predictable ordering rule on all records within a collection. When every clerk in an office follows the same rule, any clerk can retrieve any document without needing to know who filed it or when. The system eliminates personal memory as a retrieval requirement — the arrangement itself tells you where to look.

### 4.2 Alphabetic Filing System

The alphabetic filing system arranges records according to the letters of the alphabet. It is the most common and most intuitive filing system — most people can use it without extensive training because the alphabet is universally known.

#### How It Works

Records are arranged in strict alphabetical order based on a key filing unit. For personal names, the surname is the key unit. For business names, the first word of the business name (excluding articles like "The") is the key unit.

**Arrangement rules:**

1. Compare key units letter by letter from left to right.
2. If key units are identical, compare second units.
3. If second units are identical, compare third units.
4. "Nothing comes before something" — a shorter name files before a longer name that begins with the same letters (e.g., "Cruz" before "Cruz-Reyes").

**Example — Personal names in alphabetic order:**

| # | Name (Filed as) | Key Unit | Unit 2 | Unit 3 |
|---|----------------|----------|--------|--------|
| 1 | Bautista, Ana Marie | Bautista | Ana | Marie |
| 2 | Cruz, Jose P. | Cruz | Jose | P |
| 3 | Dela Cruz, Maria | DelaCruz | Maria | — |
| 4 | Garcia, Roberto S. | Garcia | Roberto | S |
| 5 | Santos, Juan | Santos | Juan | — |
| 6 | Santos, Maria Clara | Santos | Maria | Clara |
| 7 | Torres, Elena V. | Torres | Elena | V |

**Note:** "Dela Cruz" is treated as one unit (DelaCruz) — prefixes and particles are combined with the surname.

#### Advantages

- Simple and universally understood — minimal training required.
- Direct access — no index needed to locate a file (you go straight to the letter).
- Expandable — new files can be inserted without renumbering or reorganizing.
- Self-indexing — the arrangement itself serves as the index.

#### Disadvantages

- Congestion at common letters — in Philippine offices, many surnames start with "S" (Santos, Soriano, Salazar) or "D" (Dela Cruz, De Leon, Dizon), creating uneven distribution.
- Misspelling causes misfiling — if "Garcia" is misspelled as "Garsia," the file is lost.
- Requires consistent indexing rules — without agreed rules for prefixes, titles, and particles, different clerks may file the same name differently.
- Not suitable for confidential records — the file label reveals the person's name.

#### When to Use Alphabetic Filing

- Small to medium offices with fewer than 5,000 records.
- When records are most often requested by name.
- Correspondence files arranged by sender/recipient name.
- Personnel files (201 files) arranged by employee surname.

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Alphabetic filing leverages a skill everyone already has — knowledge of the alphabet. Because the ordering rule is external and universal (A before B before C...), it requires no lookup table, no code book, and no prior knowledge of the filing system's history. A new clerk on their first day can find "Santos" by going to the "S" section. This zero-training-required property makes it the default choice for most small offices.

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "Alphabetic filing means you just put names in ABC order — there are no special rules."
>
> **Why it fails:** Consider these names: "Dela Cruz, Maria" and "De Leon, Jose." Does "Dela" come before "De"? Is "Dela Cruz" one unit or two? Without standardized rules for prefixes, particles, titles, and suffixes, two clerks will file the same name in different places. Simple ABC ordering breaks down immediately with real Filipino names.
>
> **Correct model:** Alphabetic filing requires a complete set of indexing rules that govern how names are broken into units, how prefixes are handled, and how ties are broken. The alphabet provides the ordering; the rules provide consistency.

### 4.3 Numeric Filing System

The numeric filing system arranges records by assigned numbers rather than by name or subject. Each record (or each correspondent/client) receives a unique number, and files are arranged in numerical sequence.

#### How It Works

1. A new record or correspondent is assigned the next available number.
2. The file is placed in numerical order (001, 002, 003...).
3. A separate alphabetic index (card file or database) maps names to numbers.
4. To retrieve a file, look up the name in the alphabetic index → get the number → go to that number in the file.

**Example — Numeric filing in a government office:**

| File Number | Correspondent/Record |
|-------------|---------------------|
| 2024-001 | Santos, Maria — Personnel File |
| 2024-002 | Reyes, Juan — Personnel File |
| 2024-003 | Garcia, Ana — Personnel File |
| 2024-004 | Torres, Roberto — Personnel File |
| 2024-005 | Cruz, Elena — Personnel File |

**Types of numeric filing:**

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Consecutive/Serial | Numbers assigned in sequence | 001, 002, 003... |
| Terminal-digit | Filed by last digits first (reduces congestion) | File 23-45-**67** filed in section 67 |
| Middle-digit | Filed by middle digits first | File 23-**45**-67 filed in section 45 |
| Duplex-numeric | Numbers with subdivisions | 12-3 (main class 12, subdivision 3) |

#### Terminal-Digit Filing

In large offices with thousands of files, consecutive numeric filing creates congestion — all new files go to the end. Terminal-digit filing solves this by reading the number from right to left:

**File number: 34-56-78**

| Primary (last two) | Secondary (middle two) | Tertiary (first two) |
|--------------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| 78 | 56 | 34 |

The file goes in section **78**, subsection **56**, position **34**. This distributes new files evenly across all sections.

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Terminal-digit filing distributes files evenly because the last digits of sequentially assigned numbers cycle through all values (00-99) uniformly. In consecutive filing, files 5001-5099 all go in the same section, creating a bottleneck. In terminal-digit filing, file 5001 goes in section 01, file 5002 in section 02, file 5003 in section 03 — spreading the workload across 100 sections. This is why large hospitals and government agencies with 50,000+ records use terminal-digit filing.

#### Advantages

- Unlimited expansion — new numbers are always available.
- Confidentiality — file labels show numbers, not names (useful for personnel, medical, legal records).
- Accuracy — numbers are less prone to misspelling than names.
- Even distribution — especially with terminal-digit filing.
- Unique identification — no two files share a number, eliminating confusion between "Santos, Maria" and "Santos, Maria C."

#### Disadvantages

- Indirect access — requires an alphabetic index to find the number (two-step retrieval).
- Index maintenance — the alphabetic index must be kept current; if it's lost, the system is unusable.
- No logical grouping — related records (same family, same department) may have widely separated numbers.
- Training required — clerks must understand the numbering scheme and index system.

#### When to Use Numeric Filing

- Large offices with more than 5,000 active records.
- When confidentiality is important (medical records, legal cases, personnel investigations).
- When records are referenced by number (case numbers, voucher numbers, transaction IDs).
- When multiple people with the same name exist in the system.
- Government agencies that assign tracking numbers to transactions (e.g., COA audit findings).

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "Numeric filing means filing documents by their date — newest first or oldest first."
>
> **Why it fails:** Filing by date is *chronological* filing — a separate method. Numeric filing assigns an arbitrary number to each record as an identifier. File 2024-003 was not necessarily created on the third day of 2024; it was simply the third file opened that year. The number is an address, not a timestamp.
>
> **Correct model:** In numeric filing, the number is a unique identifier assigned to a record or correspondent. The number determines the file's physical position. The alphabetic index provides the bridge between the name you know and the number you need.

### 4.4 Subject Filing System

The subject filing system arranges records by their topic or subject matter rather than by name or number. All documents related to the same subject are grouped together regardless of who created them or when.

#### How It Works

1. A master list of subjects (called a **relative index**) is established in advance.
2. Each incoming document is read and assigned to the appropriate subject heading.
3. Documents are filed behind the subject guide in the cabinet.
4. Within each subject, documents may be arranged chronologically (newest on top) or alphabetically by correspondent.

**Example — Subject filing in a government HR office:**

| Main Subject | Sub-subject | Documents Filed Here |
|-------------|-------------|---------------------|
| Benefits | Health Insurance | PhilHealth enrollment forms, claims |
| Benefits | Leave | Leave applications, leave credits summary |
| Benefits | Retirement | GSIS forms, retirement applications |
| Recruitment | Examinations | CSE results, eligibility certificates |
| Recruitment | Hiring | Job postings, application letters, shortlists |
| Training | Local | Training orders, certificates (local seminars) |
| Training | Foreign | Travel authority, scholarship grants |

**The Relative Index:**

A relative index is an alphabetical list of all subjects, sub-subjects, and cross-references used in the system. It tells the clerk exactly where to file a document and prevents the creation of duplicate or overlapping categories.

```
RELATIVE INDEX (excerpt)
─────────────────────────
Benefits — see also: Compensation
  Health Insurance
  Leave
  Retirement
Compensation — see: Benefits
Examinations — see: Recruitment > Examinations
Hiring — see: Recruitment > Hiring
Leave — see: Benefits > Leave
Recruitment
  Examinations
  Hiring
Training
  Foreign
  Local
```

#### Advantages

- Groups related documents together — all "Training" records in one place regardless of who attended.
- Logical for offices that think in terms of topics (policy offices, research units).
- Flexible — new subjects can be added as needs arise.
- Supports decision-making — all information on a topic is consolidated.

#### Disadvantages

- Requires a pre-established subject list (relative index) — without it, clerks invent their own headings and chaos follows.
- Subjective — different clerks may assign the same document to different subjects.
- Difficult to maintain consistency as the system grows.
- Indirect access for name-based searches — if you want "all documents from Santos," you must search every subject.
- Requires training — clerks must understand the subject hierarchy and use the relative index.

#### When to Use Subject Filing

- When records are requested by topic rather than by name or number.
- Policy and research offices where information is organized by issue.
- Project-based offices where all project documents must be together.
- Offices that handle diverse correspondence on recurring topics.
- When the same subject involves multiple correspondents over time.

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Subject filing works because it mirrors how people actually think about and request information in topic-oriented offices. A division chief asking "Where are our training records?" is thinking by subject, not by the name of each trainee. By pre-defining subjects in a relative index, the system ensures that everyone uses the same categories — preventing the situation where one clerk files under "Seminars," another under "Training," and a third under "Capacity Building" for the same type of document.

### 4.5 Geographic Filing System

The geographic filing system arranges records by location — region, province, city, municipality, or barangay. It is used when the physical location of a correspondent, client, or transaction is the primary way records are requested.

#### How It Works

1. Determine the geographic divisions (e.g., Region → Province → City/Municipality).
2. Create guide tabs for each geographic level.
3. File records behind the appropriate geographic guide.
4. Within each geographic division, arrange records alphabetically by name.

**Example — Geographic filing for a national agency with regional offices:**

| Region | Province | City/Municipality | Correspondent |
|--------|----------|-------------------|---------------|
| Region I — Ilocos | Pangasinan | Dagupan City | Aquino, Pedro |
| Region I — Ilocos | Pangasinan | Dagupan City | Santos, Maria |
| Region I — Ilocos | Pangasinan | Lingayen | Cruz, Ana |
| Region III — Central Luzon | Bulacan | Malolos | Garcia, Jose |
| Region III — Central Luzon | Pampanga | San Fernando | Reyes, Elena |
| NCR | Metro Manila | Quezon City | Torres, Juan |
| NCR | Metro Manila | Manila | Villanueva, Rosa |

**Filing order within the system:**

1. **Primary guide:** Region (arranged in standard Philippine regional order or alphabetically)
2. **Secondary guide:** Province (alphabetical within region)
3. **Tertiary guide:** City/Municipality (alphabetical within province)
4. **Individual folders:** Correspondent name (alphabetical within city)

#### The Lettered Guide Plan vs. Location Name Plan

| Plan | Primary Arrangement | Example |
|------|-------------------|---------|
| Lettered Guide | Alphabetical by location name | Bulacan → Cavite → Laguna → Pampanga |
| Location Name | By geographic/administrative hierarchy | Region I → Region II → Region III → NCR |

Philippine government offices typically use the **Location Name Plan** following the standard regional numbering (Region I through BARMM), since this matches how agencies are administratively organized.

#### Advantages

- Natural for offices that serve geographic areas (regional offices, field units, delivery services).
- Groups all records from one area together — useful for field visits, regional reports, and area-based analysis.
- Supports geographic reporting requirements (e.g., "How many transactions from Region IV-A?").
- Intuitive for clerks who think in terms of service areas.

#### Disadvantages

- Requires knowledge of Philippine geography — clerks must know which province belongs to which region.
- Indirect access for name-based searches — finding "Santos, Maria" requires knowing her location first.
- Boundary changes cause reorganization — when municipalities are reclassified or new regions are created, files must be moved.
- Uneven distribution — NCR may have far more records than CAR or BARMM.
- Requires a supplementary alphabetic index for name-based retrieval.

#### When to Use Geographic Filing

- Regional and field offices that serve specific geographic areas.
- Agencies that track services by location (DSWD, DPWH, DepEd regional offices).
- Sales or service organizations with territory-based operations.
- When reports are generated by geographic area.
- When field personnel are assigned by region and need quick access to their area's records.

**Philippine geographic hierarchy for filing:**

```
Region (e.g., Region IV-A — CALABARZON)
  └── Province (e.g., Laguna)
       └── City/Municipality (e.g., Santa Rosa City)
            └── Barangay (if needed)
                 └── Individual name (alphabetical)
```

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "Geographic filing is just alphabetic filing by city name."
>
> **Why it fails:** If you file purely alphabetically by city, then "Dagupan" (Pangasinan) files next to "Dasmariñas" (Cavite) — two cities in completely different regions. When a regional director asks for "all files from Region I," you would need to search the entire cabinet for scattered cities. Geographic filing groups by administrative hierarchy first, then alphabetizes within each level.
>
> **Correct model:** Geographic filing uses a hierarchical arrangement (Region → Province → City) with alphabetic arrangement as the secondary method within each geographic level. The geographic grouping is primary; the alphabetic arrangement is secondary.

### 4.6 Comparing the Four Filing Systems

Understanding when to use each system is a key CSE skill. The exam often presents a scenario and asks which filing system is most appropriate.

**Comparison Matrix:**

| Arrangement basis | Name/letter | Assigned number | Topic | Location |
| Access type | Direct | Indirect (needs index) | Indirect (needs relative index) | Indirect (needs location knowledge) |
| Best for | Small offices, name-based retrieval | Large offices, confidential records | Topic-based offices, policy work | Regional offices, area-based services |
| Expansion | Easy (insert alphabetically) | Easy (add next number) | Moderate (must fit subject list) | Moderate (geographic boundaries fixed) |

### 4.7 Combination Systems

In practice, most government offices use a combination of filing systems. Different record types within the same office may use different systems.

**Common combinations in Philippine government offices:**

| Record Type | Filing System Used | Reason |
|-------------|-------------------|--------|
| Personnel files (201) | Alphabetic by surname | Requested by employee name |
| Financial vouchers | Numeric by voucher number | Requested by transaction number, audit trail |
| Policy files | Subject | Requested by topic |
| Field office correspondence | Geographic then alphabetic | Requested by region/area |
| Case files (legal, admin) | Numeric by case number | Confidentiality, sequential tracking |
| General correspondence | Alphabetic by sender | Requested by correspondent name |

**Rule for the CSE:** When a question asks "Which filing system is used?", focus on the PRIMARY arrangement method. If files are arranged first by region and then alphabetically within each region, the system is **geographic** (not alphabetic).

### Check Your Understanding

**1.** What is the key difference between alphabetic and numeric filing? → **Alphabetic provides direct access (go straight to the name); numeric requires indirect access (look up the name in an index to get the number, then find the number).** (Direct vs. indirect retrieval)

**2.** Why does subject filing require a relative index? → **Without a pre-established list of subjects, different clerks will create different headings for the same topic, causing documents to scatter across inconsistent categories.** (Consistency requires a controlled vocabulary)

**3.** In geographic filing, what is the secondary arrangement within each location? → **Alphabetical by name.** (Geographic grouping is primary; alphabetic is secondary)

**4.** Which filing system provides the most confidentiality? → **Numeric** — file labels show only numbers, revealing nothing about the content or person.

**5.** A government office has 50,000 personnel records and many employees share common surnames. Which system is most appropriate? → **Numeric** — handles large volume, eliminates name confusion, and provides confidentiality for personnel records.

### Exam Strategies

- When asked "Which filing system is best for...?", identify the PRIMARY way records are requested (by name, number, topic, or location).
- If the scenario mentions confidentiality or large volume, lean toward numeric.
- If the scenario mentions a regional office or area-based service, lean toward geographic.
- If the scenario mentions policy work, projects, or topic-based retrieval, lean toward subject.
- If the scenario mentions a small office with name-based requests, lean toward alphabetic.
- For "arrange these in correct filing order" questions, identify which system is being used first, then apply that system's rules.
- Remember: geographic filing uses alphabetic as a SECONDARY method — don't confuse the primary system.
- Watch for trick questions that describe a combination system — identify the PRIMARY arrangement.
- Time tip: filing system identification questions should take 15-20 seconds. If you're overthinking, go with the most obvious match.

### Memory Aids

#### The ANSG Mnemonic for Four Filing Systems

**A** — Alphabetic (by name/letter)
**N** — Numeric (by number)
**S** — Subject (by topic)
**G** — Geographic (by location)

Remember: "**A** **N**eat **S**ystem **G**ives results"

#### Direct vs. Indirect Access

- **Direct** = go straight to the file (Alphabetic only)
- **Indirect** = need a separate index first (Numeric, Subject, Geographic)

Memory trick: "**A**lphabetic is the only **A**utomatic access — all others need an index."

#### When to Choose Each System

| If they ask for records by... | Use... |
|-------------------------------|--------|
| Name | Alphabetic |
| Number/Case ID | Numeric |
| Topic/Issue | Subject |
| Place/Region | Geographic |

#### The Confidentiality Test

"Can an unauthorized person learn something from the file label?"
- Alphabetic label "Santos, Maria" → reveals identity → LOW confidentiality
- Numeric label "2024-0347" → reveals nothing → HIGH confidentiality

### Guided Practice

#### Problem 1

A small municipal office (200 employees) needs to organize personnel files. Records are always requested by employee name. Which filing system should they use?

**Step 1:** How are records requested? → By name
**Step 2:** What is the volume? → Small (200 records)
**Step 3:** Is confidentiality a major concern? → Not mentioned
**Step 4:** Apply the decision guide: name-based + small volume = _____

**Answer:** **Alphabetic filing system.** Small volume, name-based retrieval, no special confidentiality needs — alphabetic is the simplest and most efficient choice.

#### Problem 2

The Commission on Audit (COA) needs to file 80,000 audit findings. Each finding has a case number. Findings must be confidential until resolved. Which filing system?

**Step 1:** How are records requested? → By case number
**Step 2:** What is the volume? → Very large (80,000)
**Step 3:** Is confidentiality needed? → Yes
**Step 4:** Apply the decision guide: number-based + large volume + confidentiality = _____

**Answer:** **Numeric filing system.** The case numbers provide natural numeric identifiers, the volume is too large for alphabetic, and confidentiality requires that labels not reveal content.

#### Problem 3

A policy research office at NEDA organizes documents about economic planning, infrastructure, social development, and governance. Staff always ask for documents by topic: "Where are the infrastructure files?" Which system?

**Step 1:** How are records requested? → By topic/subject
**Step 2:** What is the nature of the office? → Policy/research, topic-oriented
**Step 3:** Apply the decision guide: topic-based retrieval = _____

**Answer:** **Subject filing system.** The office thinks and works in terms of subjects. A relative index would list: Economic Planning, Governance, Infrastructure, Social Development (and sub-topics under each).

#### Problem 4

The DSWD Regional Office for Region VII (Central Visayas) manages case files for beneficiaries across Cebu, Bohol, Siquijor, and Negros Oriental. Field workers request files by province and municipality. Which system?

### Which Method?

### Example 1: Identifying the Correct Filing System

**Problem:** The Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) assigns each taxpayer a Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN). All taxpayer records are filed by TIN. **Solution:**

Step 2: How are records retrieved? → Look up name → get number → find file (indirect access)
Step 3: Is there a separate index? → Yes (the computer system maps names to TINs)
Step 4: Match to filing system characteristics → Numeric filing system ✓

**Answer:** Numeric filing system. The TIN serves as the unique numeric identifier, files are arranged in numeric order, and an alphabetic index (computerized) bridges the gap between the name and the number.

### Example 3: Choosing Between Two Systems

**Problem:** A government hospital has 30,000 patient records. The records clerk argues for alphabetic filing because "everyone knows the alphabet." The records officer argues for numeric filing. Who is correct and why?

**Solution:**

Step 1: Evaluate volume → 30,000 records (large)
Step 2: Evaluate confidentiality → Medical records are highly confidential
Step 3: Evaluate name duplication → With 30,000 patients, many will share surnames (Santos, Cruz, Garcia)
Step 4: Evaluate retrieval pattern → Patients have medical record numbers assigned at admission

**Analysis:**
- Alphabetic: Would create massive congestion at common letters. Multiple "Santos, Maria" entries would cause confusion. File labels would reveal patient names (privacy violation).
- Numeric: Handles large volume, provides confidentiality, eliminates name confusion, and patients already have assigned numbers.

**Answer:** The records officer is correct — numeric filing (specifically terminal-digit for even distribution) is appropriate. The clerk's argument fails because "knowing the alphabet" doesn't solve the problems of volume, confidentiality, and name duplication.

## Key Takeaways

- A filing system is the method used to arrange records for storage and retrieval — it answers "In what order?"
- The four major filing systems are: alphabetic (by name), numeric (by number), subject (by topic), and geographic (by location).
- Alphabetic filing is the only system with DIRECT access — all others require a separate index (indirect access).
- Numeric filing provides the highest confidentiality because file labels reveal no content.
- Subject filing requires a relative index (pre-established list of subjects) to maintain consistency.
- Geographic filing arranges by location hierarchy (Region → Province → City) with alphabetic arrangement as the secondary method within each level.
- The "best" system depends on: how records are requested, volume, confidentiality needs, and office function.
- Most government offices use COMBINATION systems — different record types use different filing systems.
- Terminal-digit filing solves the congestion problem in large numeric systems by distributing files evenly.
- For the CSE, identify the PRIMARY arrangement method — that determines the filing system name, even if a secondary method (like alphabetic within geographic) is also used.

## Summary

Filing systems are the backbone of records management in any government office. The four major systems — alphabetic, numeric, subject, and geographic — each serve different organizational needs. Alphabetic filing arranges records by name and provides the simplest, most intuitive access; it works best in small offices where records are requested by name. Numeric filing assigns unique numbers to records and arranges them in numerical sequence; it excels in large offices, provides confidentiality, and eliminates confusion when multiple records share the same name.

Subject filing groups records by topic using a pre-established relative index, making it ideal for policy offices and research units where staff think in terms of issues rather than names. Geographic filing arranges records by location — following the Philippine administrative hierarchy of Region, Province, and City/Municipality — and serves regional offices that deliver area-based services. Each system has clear advantages and limitations, and the choice depends on how records are most frequently requested, the volume of records, confidentiality requirements, and the nature of the office's work.

In practice, Philippine government offices use combination systems: personnel files filed alphabetically, financial vouchers filed numerically, policy documents filed by subject, and field reports filed geographically. The CSE tests your ability to identify which system fits which scenario, apply each system's rules correctly, and understand the trade-offs involved. Mastering these four systems and their decision criteria gives you the foundation to handle any filing question the exam presents.
