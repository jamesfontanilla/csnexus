# Homophones

## Explanations

### Introduction

Homophones are words that sound identical when spoken but differ in spelling and meaning. The word comes from Greek: *homos* (same) + *phone* (sound). Examples include *their*, *there*, and *they're* — three distinct words that are pronounced exactly the same yet carry completely different meanings and spellings.

In the Philippine Civil Service Examination (CSE) Sub-Professional level, homophones are tested because government employees must choose the correct word in official documents. A memorandum that reads "the principle reason for the delay" instead of "the principal reason" is not just a spelling error — it is a meaning error that can misrepresent the intent of an official communication. The CSE tests whether candidates can distinguish homophones in context, selecting the word whose meaning fits the sentence.

This lesson covers the most commonly tested homophone pairs and groups, with emphasis on the government and professional vocabulary that appears in Philippine civil service documents. Mastery of homophones requires both spelling knowledge and contextual reading — you must know what each word means, not just how it sounds.

### Why Homophones Are Tested in the CSE

- Government documents are legal records — using the wrong homophone changes the meaning of official text.
- Clerical staff transcribe dictated correspondence where homophones are indistinguishable by ear.
- Proofreading requires recognizing that a word is spelled correctly but used incorrectly.
- Homophone errors are invisible to spell-checkers, making human recognition essential.
- Philippine government forms (SALN, CS Form 212) require precise word choices.
- Confusing homophones in contracts, resolutions, or ordinances can create legal ambiguity.
- The CSE tests whether candidates can apply vocabulary knowledge under time pressure.
- Homophone mastery signals the reading comprehension level expected of civil servants.

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. Relying on sound alone — since homophones sound identical, examinees guess without checking meaning.
2. Confusing *affect* and *effect* — both appear in government writing but serve different grammatical roles.
3. Using *principal* when *principle* is required, or vice versa — a very common error in policy documents.
4. Writing *their* when *there* or *they're* is correct — the most frequent homophone error in English.
5. Confusing *complement* and *compliment* — both appear in HR and administrative contexts.
6. Mixing up *stationary* and *stationery* — both are relevant to office work.
7. Choosing *precede* vs. *proceed* incorrectly — both appear in procedural documents.
8. Failing to distinguish *ensure*, *insure*, and *assure* — all three appear in government directives.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define homophones and explain why they cause errors in written documents.
- Identify the correct homophone in a sentence based on grammatical role and meaning.
- Distinguish between the 30+ most commonly tested homophone pairs in government writing.
- Apply context clues to select the appropriate word from a homophone pair or group.
- Recognize homophones that are specific to Philippine government and office vocabulary.
- Use memory aids and semantic anchors to reliably distinguish confusable pairs.
- Avoid the most common homophone errors found in official correspondence.
- Complete homophone recognition tasks within the time constraints of the CSE.


### 4.1 What Makes Homophones Dangerous in Official Writing

Homophones are uniquely dangerous because standard spell-checkers cannot catch them. If you type *their* when you mean *there*, the word is spelled correctly — the error is semantic, not orthographic. This means the only defense is human knowledge of what each word means.

In government writing, the stakes are high. Consider these examples:

| Wrong | Right | Why It Matters |
|-------|-------|---------------|
| "The principle office is in Manila." | "The principal office is in Manila." | *Principle* = rule; *principal* = main/chief |
| "Please altar the document." | "Please alter the document." | *Altar* = raised platform; *alter* = to change |
| "The board past the resolution." | "The board passed the resolution." | *Past* = previous time; *passed* = past tense of pass |
| "The employee's moral is low." | "The employee's morale is low." | *Moral* = ethical; *morale* = spirit/attitude |
| "The cite was approved." | "The site was approved." | *Cite* = to reference; *site* = location |

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Homophones exist because English spelling was standardized at different times for different words, while pronunciation continued to evolve. Words that once sounded different (like *flour* and *flower*, which had distinct vowel sounds in Middle English) converged in pronunciation while retaining their separate spellings. Understanding that spelling preserves historical distinctions helps you remember that each homophone has its own identity — they are not interchangeable variants of the same word.

### 4.2 The Their / There / They're Group

This is the most frequently tested homophone group on any English exam.

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| their | possessive pronoun | belonging to them | *Their* reports are due Friday. |
| there | adverb / pronoun | in that place; introduces a clause | The files are *there*. *There* are three copies. |
| they're | contraction | they are | *They're* attending the seminar. |

**Quick test:**
- Can you replace it with "they are"? → Use *they're*
- Does it show ownership? → Use *their*
- Does it indicate a place or introduce a sentence? → Use *there*

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "I can use *their*, *there*, and *they're* interchangeably in informal writing."
>
> **Why it fails:** Government documents are never informal. A memorandum that reads "Their is no basis for the claim" is grammatically wrong and reflects poorly on the issuing office.
>
> **Correct model:** Always apply the substitution test. If "they are" fits, use *they're*. If ownership is implied, use *their*. Otherwise, use *there*.

### 4.3 The To / Too / Two Group

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| to | preposition / infinitive marker | direction; before a verb | Submit *to* the office. *To* proceed with the plan... |
| too | adverb | also; excessively | The clerk *too* must sign. The fee is *too* high. |
| two | number | the number 2 | *Two* copies are required. |

**Quick test:**
- Is it a number? → *two*
- Does it mean "also" or "excessively"? → *too*
- Everything else → *to*


### 4.4 Principal vs. Principle

This pair is extremely common in government and policy documents.

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Memory Aid |
|------|---------------|---------|-----------|
| principal | noun / adjective | main, chief; head of a school or organization | The principAL is your pAL |
| principle | noun | a fundamental rule, standard, or belief | A principLE is a ruLE |

**Government examples:**
- "The *principal* reason for the suspension is insubordination." (main reason)
- "The *principal* of the school submitted the SALN." (head of school)
- "The *principle* of due process must be observed." (fundamental rule)
- "The agency operates on the *principle* of transparency." (standard)

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The memory aid works because both *principal* and *pal* end in -al, and both *principle* and *rule* end in -le. Anchoring the spelling to a meaning-related word creates a dual memory trace — you remember both the spelling and the meaning simultaneously.

### 4.5 Affect vs. Effect

This pair causes errors even among experienced writers. Both appear constantly in government reports and policy documents.

| Word | Primary Role | Meaning | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|---------|
| affect | verb | to influence or have an impact on | The new policy will *affect* all employees. |
| effect | noun | a result or outcome | The *effect* of the policy was immediate. |
| effect | verb (rare) | to bring about, to cause | The committee will *effect* the necessary changes. |
| affect | noun (psychology) | emotional state (rare in government writing) | The patient displayed flat *affect*. |

**The 95% rule:** In government writing, *affect* is almost always the verb and *effect* is almost always the noun.

**Quick test:** Can you replace it with "result" or "outcome"? → Use *effect* (noun). Can you replace it with "influence" or "impact"? → Use *affect* (verb).

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "Effect is always a noun and affect is always a verb."
>
> **Why it fails:** *Effect* can be a verb meaning "to bring about" (as in "effect change"), and *affect* can be a noun in psychology. However, these uses are rare in government writing.
>
> **Correct model:** In 95% of CSE questions, treat *affect* as the verb and *effect* as the noun. Only consider the rare uses if the context makes the standard interpretation impossible.

### 4.6 Complement vs. Compliment

Both words appear in HR, administrative, and correspondence contexts.

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| complement | noun / verb | something that completes; to complete | The staff *complement* is 45 employees. The new hire *complements* the team. |
| compliment | noun / verb | praise; to praise | The director gave a *compliment* to the team. He *complimented* her work. |

**Memory aid:** *complEment* = complEte (both have E). *complIment* = I like you (both have I).

**Government context:** "Staff complement" is a standard HR term meaning the total number of authorized positions. This is the most common government use of *complement*.


### 4.7 Stationary vs. Stationery

Both words are directly relevant to office and clerical work.

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Memory Aid |
|------|---------------|---------|-----------|
| stationary | adjective | not moving, fixed in place | stationARy = stAnding still (A for at rest) |
| stationery | noun | writing materials (paper, envelopes, pens) | stationERy = papER and lettERs (E for envelope) |

**Government examples:**
- "The vehicle remained *stationary* during the inspection." (not moving)
- "Order new *stationery* for the regional office." (office supplies)
- "The *stationery* budget is under MOOE." (writing materials)

### 4.8 Precede vs. Proceed

Both appear in procedural and administrative documents.

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| precede | verb | to come before in time or order | The orientation will *precede* the examination. |
| proceed | verb | to continue, to move forward | Please *proceed* to the next step. |

**Memory aid:** *preCEDE* = comes bEforE (CE sounds like "see" — you see what came before). *proCEED* = go forward (like "speed" — you speed ahead).

**Related words:** *precedent* (a prior case that guides future decisions), *procedure* (a set of steps to follow), *proceedings* (the record of what happened).

### 4.9 Ensure vs. Insure vs. Assure

These three near-homophones are frequently confused in government directives and memoranda.

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| ensure | to make certain that something happens | *Ensure* that all forms are complete before submission. |
| insure | to provide financial insurance coverage | *Insure* the government vehicle against damage. |
| assure | to give confidence to a person; to promise | I *assure* you that the report is accurate. |

**Quick test:**
- Is it about making something certain? → *ensure*
- Is it about an insurance policy? → *insure*
- Is it about reassuring a person? → *assure*

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The three words share the root *-sure* (from Latin *securus*, safe/certain) but differ in their prefixes. *En-* (to make), *in-* (into/against), and *as-* (toward a person) each signal a different relationship. Recognizing the prefix meaning helps you select the right word.

### 4.10 Cite vs. Site vs. Sight

All three appear in government and administrative contexts.

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| cite | verb / noun | to reference a source; a citation | *Cite* the relevant provision of the law. |
| site | noun | a location or place | The construction *site* was inspected. |
| sight | noun / verb | the ability to see; something seen | The document was signed on *sight*. |

**Government examples:**
- "Please *cite* the legal basis for the action." (reference)
- "The project *site* is in Quezon City." (location)
- "The officer approved the request on *sight*." (upon seeing)


### 4.11 Passed vs. Past

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| passed | verb | past tense of "pass" | The board *passed* the resolution. She *passed* the examination. |
| past | adjective / noun / preposition | previous; a former time; beyond | In the *past*, the rule was different. Walk *past* the office. |

**Quick test:** Can you replace it with "went by" or "moved beyond"? → *past* (preposition). Is it the action of passing? → *passed* (verb).

### 4.12 Moral vs. Morale

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| moral | adjective / noun | relating to ethics; a lesson | The *moral* obligation of civil servants. The *moral* of the story. |
| morale | noun | the confidence and spirit of a group | Employee *morale* improved after the announcement. |

**Memory aid:** *morALE* ends in -ale, like "ale" — something that lifts your spirits. *morAL* ends in -al, like "ethical."

### 4.13 Discrete vs. Discreet

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| discrete | adjective | separate, distinct, individually distinct | The project has three *discrete* phases. |
| discreet | adjective | careful, tactful, not drawing attention | Please be *discreet* about the personnel matter. |

**Memory aid:** *disCREEt* = CREEP quietly (careful, quiet). *disCRETE* = separate (the two E's are separated by a T — they are discrete from each other).

### 4.14 Formally vs. Formerly

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| formally | adverb | in a formal manner | The complaint was *formally* filed. |
| formerly | adverb | in the past, previously | She was *formerly* assigned to the DILG. |

**Memory aid:** *formERly* = ERlier (earlier, in the past). *formAlly* = in A formal way.

### 4.15 Counsel vs. Council

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|---------|
| counsel | noun / verb | advice; a lawyer; to advise | Legal *counsel* reviewed the contract. The supervisor *counseled* the employee. |
| council | noun | a governing or advisory body | The *council* approved the ordinance. The City *Council* convened. |

**Memory aid:** *counCIL* = CIty (a council is a city/governing body). *counSEL* = SELf-help (counsel gives advice to help yourself).


### 4.16 Additional High-Frequency Homophone Pairs

The following pairs appear regularly on the CSE and in government writing:

**Allowed vs. Aloud**
- *allowed* = permitted: "Employees are *allowed* to take leave."
- *aloud* = out loud, audibly: "Read the resolution *aloud*."

**Bare vs. Bear**
- *bare* = uncovered, minimal: "The *bare* minimum requirement."
- *bear* = to carry or endure; the animal: "The agency must *bear* the cost."

**Board vs. Bored**
- *board* = a governing body; a flat surface: "The *board* of directors convened."
- *bored* = uninterested: "The clerk was *bored* during the long meeting."

**Capital vs. Capitol**
- *capital* = the seat of government; money; uppercase letter; punishable by death: "Manila is the *capital*." "Working *capital* is insufficient."
- *capitol* = the building where a legislature meets: "The *Capitol* building in Washington, D.C."

**Coarse vs. Course**
- *coarse* = rough in texture; crude: "The *coarse* language in the report was inappropriate."
- *course* = a path; a subject of study; of course: "The training *course* lasts three days."

**Dual vs. Duel**
- *dual* = having two parts: "The employee has a *dual* role."
- *duel* = a formal fight between two parties: "The two agencies engaged in a legal *duel*."

**Flair vs. Flare**
- *flair* = a natural talent: "She has a *flair* for administration."
- *flare* = to burst out; a signal light: "Tensions *flare* during budget season."

**Forth vs. Fourth**
- *forth* = forward, onward: "The directive sets *forth* the guidelines."
- *fourth* = the ordinal number 4: "The *fourth* quarter report is due."

**Hear vs. Here**
- *hear* = to perceive sound: "Did you *hear* the announcement?"
- *here* = in this place: "Submit the form *here*."

**Hole vs. Whole**
- *hole* = an opening: "There is a *hole* in the argument."
- *whole* = complete, entire: "The *whole* department attended."

**Hour vs. Our**
- *hour* = 60 minutes: "The meeting lasts one *hour*."
- *our* = belonging to us: "*Our* office is on the third floor."

**Its vs. It's**
- *its* = possessive of "it": "The agency submitted *its* report."
- *it's* = it is / it has: "*It's* important to file on time."

**Know vs. No**
- *know* = to be aware of: "I *know* the procedure."
- *no* = negative: "There is *no* basis for the claim."

**Knew vs. New**
- *knew* = past tense of know: "She *knew* the regulation."
- *new* = recently made: "The *new* policy takes effect Monday."

**Lead vs. Led**
- *lead* (rhymes with "feed") = the metal; to guide: "She will *lead* the team."
- *led* = past tense of lead: "She *led* the investigation."
- *lead* (rhymes with "bed") = the metal element Pb

**Lessen vs. Lesson**
- *lessen* = to reduce: "The policy will *lessen* the workload."
- *lesson* = something learned: "The *lesson* on homophones is important."

**Loose vs. Lose**
- *loose* = not tight; free: "The *loose* pages were misfiled."
- *lose* = to fail to keep; to be defeated: "Do not *lose* the original document."

**Made vs. Maid**
- *made* = past tense of make: "The decision was *made* by the director."
- *maid* = a female domestic worker: "The *maid* of honor signed the register."

**Mail vs. Male**
- *mail* = postal correspondence: "Send the notice by *mail*."
- *male* = masculine gender: "The *male* applicants were interviewed first."

**Meat vs. Meet vs. Mete**
- *meat* = animal flesh: "The *meat* of the argument is in Section 3."
- *meet* = to encounter; to satisfy: "The proposal does not *meet* the requirements."
- *mete* = to distribute (usually "mete out"): "The penalty was *meted* out fairly."

**Pair vs. Pare vs. Pear**
- *pair* = two of something: "A *pair* of documents is required."
- *pare* = to peel or reduce: "We must *pare* down the budget."
- *pear* = the fruit: (rarely appears in government writing)

**Peace vs. Piece**
- *peace* = absence of conflict: "The *peace* and order situation improved."
- *piece* = a part of something: "A *piece* of evidence was submitted."

**Plain vs. Plane**
- *plain* = simple; a flat area: "The instructions are written in *plain* language."
- *plane* = an aircraft; a flat surface: "The official traveled by *plane*."

**Pray vs. Prey**
- *pray* = to address a deity; to request earnestly: "We *pray* for guidance."
- *prey* = a victim; to hunt: "Corrupt officials *prey* on the public."

**Right vs. Write vs. Rite**
- *right* = correct; a legal entitlement: "The employee has the *right* to appeal."
- *write* = to put words on paper: "*Write* the report in formal language."
- *rite* = a ceremony: "The *rite* of passage for new employees."

**Role vs. Roll**
- *role* = a function or part: "The supervisor's *role* is to evaluate."
- *roll* = a list; to rotate: "The attendance *roll* was called."

**Sole vs. Soul**
- *sole* = only; the bottom of a shoe: "The *sole* purpose of the audit."
- *soul* = the spiritual essence: "The *soul* of public service is integrity."

**Some vs. Sum**
- *some* = an unspecified amount: "*Some* employees were absent."
- *sum* = a total amount: "The *sum* of the expenditures exceeded the budget."

**Tail vs. Tale**
- *tail* = the rear end; to follow: "The audit will *tail* the procurement process."
- *tale* = a story: "The *tale* of the missing funds was investigated."

**Vain vs. Vane vs. Vein**
- *vain* = conceited; unsuccessful: "The appeal was made in *vain*."
- *vane* = a weather vane: (rarely in government writing)
- *vein* = a blood vessel; a streak: "In the same *vein*, the policy applies."

**Waist vs. Waste**
- *waist* = the middle of the body: (rarely in government writing)
- *waste* = to use carelessly; garbage: "Do not *waste* government resources."

**Wait vs. Weight**
- *wait* = to remain until: "*Wait* for the official announcement."
- *weight* = heaviness; importance: "Give *weight* to the evidence presented."

**Weak vs. Week**
- *weak* = lacking strength: "The *weak* argument was dismissed."
- *week* = seven days: "Submit the report within one *week*."

**Weather vs. Whether**
- *weather* = atmospheric conditions: "The *weather* delayed the field inspection."
- *whether* = if (introduces alternatives): "Determine *whether* the claim is valid."

**Whose vs. Who's**
- *whose* = possessive of who: "*Whose* signature is on the document?"
- *who's* = who is / who has: "*Who's* responsible for the filing?"

**Your vs. You're**
- *your* = belonging to you: "Submit *your* application by Friday."
- *you're* = you are: "*You're* required to attend the seminar."


### 4.17 Homophones in Philippine Government Context

These pairs appear specifically in Philippine government documents and CSE materials:

**Altar vs. Alter**
- *altar* = a raised platform for religious ceremonies: "The *altar* was decorated for the Mass."
- *alter* = to change: "Do not *alter* the official document."

**Bail vs. Bale**
- *bail* = security for release from custody; to scoop water: "The accused posted *bail*."
- *bale* = a bundle: "A *bale* of documents was submitted."

**Brake vs. Break**
- *brake* = a device to stop movement: "Apply the *brake* before parking."
- *break* = to separate; a pause: "Take a *break* after the examination."

**Clause vs. Claws**
- *clause* = a section of a legal document: "Refer to *clause* 5 of the contract."
- *claws* = sharp nails of an animal: (rarely in government writing)

**Foul vs. Fowl**
- *foul* = offensive; a violation: "The *foul* odor was reported to the sanitation office."
- *fowl* = a bird: (rarely in government writing)

**Grate vs. Great**
- *grate* = to shred; to irritate: "The noise began to *grate* on the staff."

**Groan vs. Grown**
- *groan* = a sound of pain: "The staff let out a *groan* at the announcement."

**Hail vs. Hale**
- *hail* = to greet; frozen rain: "The employees *hailed* the new director."

**Heal vs. Heel**

**Idle vs. Idol**

**Knot vs. Not**

**Loot vs. Lute**

**Miner vs. Minor**

**Naval vs. Navel**

**Pail vs. Pale**

**Peer vs. Pier**

**Pleas vs. Please**

**Profit vs. Prophet**
- *profit* = financial gain: "The agency does not operate for *profit*."
- *prophet* = one who predicts: (rarely in government writing)

**Rack vs. Wrack**
- *rack* = a frame for storage; to torment: "The filing *rack* was reorganized."
- *wrack* = destruction; seaweed: "The storm left *wrack* and ruin."

**Rain vs. Reign vs. Rein**
- *rain* = precipitation: "The *rain* delayed the outdoor ceremony."

**Stair vs. Stare**

**Straight vs. Strait**

**Wail vs. Whale**

- *ware* = goods for sale: "Software is a type of *ware*."

- *where* = in what place: "*Where* is the original document?"

**Wring vs. Ring**
- *wring* = to twist and squeeze: "Do not *wring* the wet documents."
- *ring* = a circular object; to call by phone: "The *ring* binder holds the records."

### Exam Strategies

- Read the entire sentence before selecting a homophone — context always determines the correct choice.
- Identify the grammatical role first: is the blank a noun, verb, adjective, or adverb? This eliminates half the options immediately.
- Use the substitution test: replace the blank with a definition of each homophone and see which makes sense.
- For *affect/effect*: try replacing with "influence" (verb → *affect*) or "result" (noun → *effect*).
- For *principal/principle*: try replacing with "main" (adjective → *principal*) or "rule" (noun → *principle*).
- For *their/there/they're*: try replacing with "they are" (*they're*), "belonging to them" (*their*), or "in that place" (*there*).
- Do not rely on sound — all homophones sound identical. Sound gives you zero information.
- Budget 10-15 seconds per homophone question. Context reading is fast once you know the pairs.
- If two choices look identical in meaning, re-read the sentence — one will fit the grammar better.

## Key Takeaways

- Homophones are words that sound identical but differ in spelling and meaning — they cannot be distinguished by ear, only by context.
- Spell-checkers cannot catch homophone errors because each word is spelled correctly — human knowledge is the only defense.
- The most tested pairs in government writing are: *affect/effect*, *principal/principle*, *their/there/they're*, *complement/compliment*, *stationary/stationery*, *ensure/insure/assure*, *counsel/council*, *precede/proceed*, *moral/morale*, and *discrete/discreet*.
- Always identify the grammatical role (noun, verb, adjective, adverb) before selecting a homophone — this eliminates half the options immediately.
- Use the substitution test: replace the blank with a definition of each homophone and see which makes the sentence meaningful.
- Memory anchors (principAL = your pAL; stationERy = papER) create dual memory traces linking spelling to meaning.
- In government writing, *affect* is almost always the verb and *effect* is almost always the noun — the rare exceptions (*effect* as a verb meaning "to bring about") appear in formal policy language.
- The PAIR method (Part of speech → Association → Interchange test → Rule) provides a systematic approach to any homophone question.
- Philippine government documents use American English conventions — be aware of pairs like *program/programme* where only the American form is correct on the CSE.
- Homophones overlap with vocabulary, grammar, and reading comprehension — mastering them strengthens performance across multiple CSE subtests.

## Summary

Homophones are among the most insidious errors in official writing because they are invisible to automated tools and require genuine vocabulary knowledge to detect. In the Philippine Civil Service Examination, homophone questions test whether candidates can select the word whose meaning fits the context — not just the word that sounds right. Since all homophones sound identical, the only reliable strategy is to understand what each word means and apply that knowledge to the sentence.

The most important homophone pairs for the CSE are those that appear in government and administrative writing: *affect/effect* (verb vs. noun for influence and result), *principal/principle* (main/chief vs. fundamental rule), *their/there/they're* (possession vs. place vs. contraction), *complement/compliment* (to complete vs. to praise), *stationary/stationery* (not moving vs. writing materials), *ensure/insure/assure* (make certain vs. provide insurance vs. reassure a person), *counsel/council* (advice/lawyer vs. governing body), *precede/proceed* (come before vs. continue), *moral/morale* (ethical vs. group spirit), and *discrete/discreet* (separate vs. tactful). Mastering these ten groups covers the vast majority of homophone questions on the exam.

The systematic approach to homophone questions is: identify the grammatical role, apply the substitution test, use memory anchors to confirm the spelling, and eliminate misspelled options. With practice, this process becomes automatic — you will recognize the correct homophone as quickly as you recognize a misspelled word, because you have built a mental library of what each word means and how it is spelled.
