# Common Spelling Errors

## Explanations

### Introduction

Common spelling errors are predictable mistakes that occur because English spelling does not consistently follow pronunciation. In the Philippine Civil Service Examination (CSE) Sub-Professional level, questions on spelling frequently exploit these predictable error patterns — presenting misspelled versions that look plausible because they reflect how words sound rather than how they are actually written.

Government employees produce official memoranda, endorsements, certifications, and legal documents daily. A single spelling error in a CSC resolution, a COA audit report, or a DILG directive can create ambiguity, delay processing, or undermine institutional credibility. The CSE tests whether candidates can detect and avoid the most common categories of spelling errors that plague professional writing.

This lesson catalogs the systematic error patterns that produce most spelling mistakes — not random misspellings, but recurring, classifiable errors rooted in English phonology, morphology, and etymology. By understanding WHY these errors happen, you build a diagnostic framework that works across thousands of words rather than memorizing individual spellings one at a time.

### Why Common Spelling Errors Are Tested in the CSE

- Government documents are permanent records — errors cannot be casually corrected after filing.
- Misspelled words in legal instruments (resolutions, orders) can be grounds for amendment or challenge.
- Clerical staff are the last line of defense before documents reach the public.
- Common errors cluster around specific patterns — testing these patterns efficiently measures spelling competence.
- The ability to detect error patterns indicates attention to detail required in records management.
- Spelling accuracy in correspondence reflects the professionalism of the issuing agency.
- Civil servants who recognize error patterns can proofread more efficiently under time pressure.
- The CSE uses plausible misspellings as distractors — understanding error types helps eliminate wrong choices.

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. Trusting pronunciation over spelling rules — writing "pronounciation" because that is how it sounds.
2. Applying a correct rule to the wrong word — using -ible where -able is required.
3. Omitting letters that are silent in speech — "Febuary" instead of "February."
4. Adding letters by analogy with similar words — "occurrance" by analogy with "appearance."
5. Swapping vowels in unstressed syllables — "definately" instead of "definitely."
6. Doubling consonants that should be single — "untill" instead of "until."
7. Failing to double consonants that require it — "refered" instead of "referred."
8. Confusing homophones and near-homophones — "their/there/they're" or "affect/effect."

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Classify common spelling errors into distinct pattern categories (vowel confusion, consonant doubling, silent letters, suffix errors, prefix errors).
- Identify the specific error type in a misspelled word and apply the correct rule to fix it.
- Recognize why certain words are systematically misspelled based on phonological or morphological factors.
- Apply consonant-doubling rules to determine when final consonants double before suffixes.
- Distinguish between words that require silent letters and those that do not.
- Detect unstressed vowel errors by identifying the root word or etymology.
- Use prefix and suffix attachment rules to predict correct spelling.
- Avoid common analogy traps where a correct pattern for one word does not apply to another.

### 4.1 The Anatomy of a Spelling Error

Spelling errors are not random. They fall into classifiable categories, each with a specific cause:

| Error Type | Cause | Example |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| Vowel substitution | Unstressed vowels sound alike | definite → "definate" |
| Consonant doubling | Unclear whether to double | occurred → "occured" |
| Consonant singling | Over-correction | until → "untill" |
| Silent letter omission | Letter not pronounced | government → "goverment" |
| Silent letter insertion | Adding letters by analogy | athlete → "athelete" |
| Suffix confusion | Similar-sounding endings | dependable → "dependible" |
| Transposition | Letters swapped | relevant → "relavant" |
| Homophone confusion | Same sound, different spelling | principal → "principle" |

Understanding which category an error belongs to tells you which rule to apply. This is faster than memorizing every word individually.

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Categorizing errors works because English spelling errors are systematic, not random. The same phonological ambiguities that cause one person to write "seperate" cause thousands of people to make the same mistake. By learning the 8-10 error categories, you can diagnose and correct errors in words you have never specifically studied — because the underlying cause is the same.

### 4.2 Vowel Errors in Unstressed Syllables

The most common spelling error category involves vowels in unstressed syllables. In English, unstressed vowels reduce to a neutral "schwa" sound (ə) — making a, e, i, o, and u sound identical in those positions. Your ear cannot help you; only knowledge of the word's structure can.

**Category A: The a/e confusion**

| Correct | Common Error | Memory Aid |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| separate | seperate | "There's A RAT in sepARAte" |
| calendar | calender | Ends in -ar (like "solar," "lunar") |
| grammar | grammer | Ends in -ar (like "similar") |
| relevant | relevent | Contains "relev-ANT" (think of an ant) |
| maintenance | maintainence | Main-TEN-ance (ten, not tain) |
| resistance | resistence | Resist + ance (not ence) |
| abundance | abundence | Abund + ance |
| tolerance | tolerence | Toler + ance |

**Category B: The e/i confusion**

| Correct | Common Error | Memory Aid |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| definite | definate | "There's FINITE in definite" |
| desperate | despirate | "There's an ERA in desperate" |
| repetition | repitition | "REPEAT → repetition" |
| competitive | competetive | "COMPETE → competitive" (i appears) |
| medicine | medecine | "MEDIC → medicine" |
| privilege | priviledge | Ends in -lege (not -ledge) |

**Category C: The i/e confusion in other positions**

| Correct | Common Error | Memory Aid |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| describe | discribe | "DE- prefix, not DI-" |
| despair | dispair | "DE- prefix" |
| cemetery | cemetary | All e's: "e-e-e" (three e's in a row) |
| category | catagory | "catEgory" — second vowel is e |

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "If I say the word slowly and carefully, I can hear which vowel belongs there."
>
> **Why it fails:** Unstressed vowels are phonetically reduced to schwa regardless of how slowly you speak. Saying "sep-uh-rate" slowly still produces "uh" in the middle — which could be spelled with any vowel.
>
> **Correct model:** For unstressed vowels, rely on the root word (maintain → maintenance), related forms (define → definite), or etymology — never pronunciation.

### 4.3 Consonant Doubling Errors

Consonant doubling errors come in two forms: failing to double when required, and doubling when not required. Both are extremely common on the CSE.

**Rule: When to double the final consonant before a suffix**

Double the final consonant when ALL three conditions are met:
1. The word ends in a single consonant
2. Preceded by a single vowel
3. The last syllable is stressed (or the word is one syllable)

| Word | Stressed? | + Suffix | Result | Explanation |
|------|-----------|----------|--------|-------------|
| occur | Yes (oc-CUR) | + ence | occurrence | Double r |
| refer | Yes (re-FER) | + ed | referred | Double r |
| submit | Yes (sub-MIT) | + ed | submitted | Double t |
| begin | Yes (be-GIN) | + ing | beginning | Double n |
| transfer | Yes (trans-FER) | + ed | transferred | Double r |
| benefit | No (BEN-e-fit) | + ed | benefited | Single t |
| budget | No (BUD-get) | + ed | budgeted | Single t |
| open | No (O-pen) | + ed | opened | Single n |
| travel | No (TRAV-el) | + ing | traveling | Single l (American) |

**Words commonly doubled incorrectly (should be SINGLE):**

| Correct | Common Error | Why Single |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| until | untill | "Until" is not "till" + prefix |
| fulfill | fullfill | Ful- + fill (one l in prefix) |
| welcome | wellcome | Wel- + come |
| already | allready | Al- + ready (one l) |
| altogether | alltogether | Al- + together |
| disappear | dissappear | Dis- + appear (one s, two p's) |
| recommend | reccommend | Re- + commend (one c in prefix) |

**Words commonly singled incorrectly (should be DOUBLE):**

| Correct | Common Error | Why Double |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| accommodate | accomodate | Ad + com → acc + comm |
| occurrence | occurence | Occur (double c) + rence (double r) |
| committee | comittee | Com + mit + tee (double m, double t) |
| embarrass | embarass | Double r, double s |
| harassment | harrassment | Single r, double s (harass) |
| possession | posession | Double s twice (pos-SESS-ion) |
| professional | proffesional | Single f, double s |
| misspell | mispell | Mis- + spell (keep both s's) |

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The doubling rule exists because English uses consonant doubling to signal that the preceding vowel is short. "Hoping" (long o) vs. "hopping" (short o). When you add a suffix to "occur" (short u, stressed), you double the r to preserve the short vowel sound. When the syllable is unstressed (like "benefit"), the vowel quality does not need protection, so no doubling occurs.

### 4.4 Silent Letter Errors

Silent letters are letters present in spelling but absent from pronunciation. Errors occur in two directions: omitting silent letters that exist, or inserting letters that do not exist.

| February | Febuary | Silent first r |

| handsome | hansome | Silent d |
| sandwich | sanwich | Silent d |
| environment | enviroment | Silent n before m |
| parliament | parliment | Silent ia (parlia-) |
| surprise | suprise | First r often dropped |

**Commonly inserted letters (letters that do NOT belong):**

| Correct | Common Error | Why Wrong |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| athlete | athelete | No extra e — only 2 syllables |
| mischievous | mischievious | No -ious ending — it's -ous |
| pronunciation | pronounciation | Not "pronounce" + iation |

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Silent letters persist in English because spelling was standardized during periods when these letters were still pronounced. "Knight" once had a pronounced k and gh. "Government" retains the n from "govern" even though modern speakers drop it. Knowing the root word or historical pronunciation reveals which "silent" letters must be written.

### 4.5 Suffix Confusion Errors

English has several pairs of suffixes that sound identical but are spelled differently. These are among the most tested patterns on the CSE.

**The -ible vs. -able error**

Quick rule:
- If the root is a complete English word → -able (depend → dependable)
- If the root is NOT a standalone word → -ible (aud- → audible)

| Correct | Common Error | Root Status |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| responsible | responsable | "respons-" is not a word → -ible |
| accessible | accessable | "access-" takes -ible (Latin pattern) |
| permissible | permissable | "permiss-" is not a word → -ible |
| feasible | feasable | "feas-" is not a word → -ible |
| flexible | flexable | "flex-" is not a standalone word → -ible |
| dependable | dependible | "depend" IS a word → -able |
| manageable | managible | "manage" IS a word → -able |
| noticeable | noticible | "notice" IS a word → -able |
| enforceable | enforcible | "enforce" IS a word → -able |
| transferable | transferible | "transfer" IS a word → -able |

**The -ence/-ance error**

Quick rule:
- If related verb ends in -ate → -ance (dominate → dominance)
- If related verb has stressed final syllable → -ence (refer → reference)

| Correct | Common Error | Rule Applied |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| occurrence | occurance | Occur (stressed) → -ence |
| preference | preferance | Prefer (stressed) → -ence |
| independence | independance | Depend → -ence pattern |
| correspondence | correspondance | Correspond → -ence |
| maintenance | maintainence | Maintain → -enance (irregular) |
| attendance | attendence | Attend + ance |
| performance | performence | Perform + ance |
| compliance | complience | Comply → compliance |
| surveillance | surveillence | French origin → -ance |
| allegiance | allegience | French origin → -ance |

**The -tion/-sion/-cian error**

| Correct | Common Error | Rule |
|---------|-------------|------|
| extension | extention | Extend (ends in -d) → -sion |
| admission | admittion | Admit (ends in -mit) → -ssion |
| permission | permittion | Permit (ends in -mit) → -ssion |
| suspension | suspencion | Suspend (ends in -d) → -sion |
| comprehension | comprehention | Comprehend → -sion |
| politician | politicion | Person/practitioner → -cian |
| technician | technision | Person/practitioner → -cian |

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "I can tell -ible from -able by how the word sounds."
>
> **Why it fails:** "Responsible" and "dependable" have endings that sound identical — both produce the same "uh-bull" sound. Pronunciation gives zero information about which suffix to use.
>
> **Correct model:** The distinction is structural, not phonetic. Check whether the root before the suffix is a complete English word. If yes → -able. If no → -ible. This works for approximately 90% of cases.

### 4.6 Prefix Attachment Errors

When a prefix ends with the same letter that the root begins with, both letters are kept. This creates double letters that writers often incorrectly reduce to one.

|--------|------|---------|-------------|
| mis- | spell | misspell | mispell |
| mis- | step | misstep | mistep |
| dis- | satisfied | dissatisfied | disatisfied |
| dis- | similar | dissimilar | disimilar |
| un- | necessary | unnecessary | unecessary |
| un- | natural | unnatural | unatural |
| ir- | regular | irregular | iregular |
| ir- | responsible | irresponsible | iresponsible |
| il- | legal | illegal | ilegal |
| il- | logical | illogical | ilogical |
| im- | mature | immature | imature |
| im- | moral | immoral | imoral |
| over- | rule | overrule | overule |
| over- | rate | overrate | overate |

| Prefix | Root | Correct | Common Error |

### 4.7 Transposition and Sequence Errors

Transposition errors occur when letters are written in the wrong order. These are particularly insidious because the resulting word often "looks right" at a glance.

**Common transposition errors:**

| Correct | Common Error | Letters Swapped |
|---------|-------------|----------------|
| relevant | relavant | e and a swapped |
| tragedy | tradegy | e and g swapped |
| cavalry | calvary | l and v swapped |
| jewelry | jewlery | e and l swapped |
| nuclear | nucular | le and u swapped |
| prescription | perscription | re and er swapped |
| perspective | prospective* | Different word entirely |
| specific | pacific* | Different word entirely |

*These last two are word confusions, not transpositions — but they appear on exams as distractors.

**Sequence errors in vowel clusters:**

| Correct | Common Error | Correct Sequence |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| bureaucracy | beauracracy | u-r-e-a-u (bureau) |
| lieutenant | leiutenant | i-e-u (lieu) |
| colleague | colleauge | e-a-g-u-e |
| guarantee | gaurantee | g-u-a-r |
| language | langauge | g-u-a-g-e |
| adequate | adeqaute | q-u-a-t-e |

**Strategy for transposition errors:** Break the word into its morphological parts (prefix + root + suffix). If each part is spelled correctly, the whole word is correct. "Bureaucracy" = bureau + cracy. "Prescription" = pre + script + ion.

### 4.8 Words Commonly Misspelled by Analogy

Some errors occur because writers apply a pattern from one word to a different word where it does not belong. This "false analogy" is a trap the CSE exploits.

**False analogy errors:**

| Correct Word | Error | False Analogy Source |
|-------------|-------|---------------------|
| supersede | supercede | Analogy with "precede," "intercede" |
| consensus | concensus | Analogy with "census" |
| inoculate | innoculate | Analogy with "innocent" |
| harass | harrass | Analogy with "embarrass" |
| desiccate | dessicate | Analogy with "dessert" |
| minuscule | miniscule | Analogy with "mini" |
| sacrilegious | sacreligious | Analogy with "religious" |
| memento | momento | Analogy with "moment" |
| cemetery | cemetary | Analogy with "secretary" |
| independent | independant | Analogy with "pendant" |

**Why "supersede" is unique:**
- "Supersede" is the ONLY English word ending in -sede
- All others end in -cede: precede, recede, concede, intercede, secede
- Or -ceed: proceed, succeed, exceed
- The word comes from Latin "supersedere" (to sit above), not "cedere" (to go)

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** False analogy errors reveal that spelling is not purely rule-based — it is also word-specific. "Harass" has one r because it comes from Old French "harer" (to set a dog on), while "embarrass" has two r's from Spanish "embarazar." Words that sound similar may have completely different etymologies and therefore different spellings. The CSE tests whether you know the specific word, not just the general pattern.

### 4.9 The ie/ei Error Pattern

The traditional rule "i before e, except after c, or when sounded as 'ay'" covers most cases but has important exceptions that the CSE tests.

**Standard ie words (i before e):**
- achieve, believe, chief, field, grief, piece, relief, retrieve, shield, thief, yield

**ei after c:**
- ceiling, conceive, deceive, perceive, receipt, receive

**ei sounded as "ay":**
- eight, freight, neighbor, reign, vein, weigh, sleigh

**Exceptions to memorize (ei without c, not "ay" sound):**
- either, neither, weird, seize, leisure, protein, caffeine, foreign, forfeit, species, science, sufficient, efficient

**Common ie/ei errors on the CSE:**

| Correct | Common Error | Rule/Exception |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| receive | recieve | ei after c |
| achieve | acheive | Standard ie |
| believe | beleive | Standard ie |
| foreign | foriegn | Exception — memorize |
| weird | wierd | Exception — memorize |
| seize | sieze | Exception — memorize |
| sufficient | sufficent | -ient ending |
| efficient | efficent | -ient ending |
| conscience | concience | sci cluster |
| science | sceince | sci cluster |

### 4.11 The Final-e Dropping Error

When adding a suffix to a word ending in silent e, the rule depends on whether the suffix starts with a vowel or consonant:

**Drop the e before vowel suffixes (-ing, -able, -ous, -ation):**

| Root | + Suffix | Correct | Common Error |
|------|----------|---------|-------------|
| manage | + able | manageable* | managable |
| notice | + able | noticeable* | noticable |
| achieve | + ment | achievement | achievment |
| argue | + ment | argument | arguement |
| judge | + ment | judgment | judgement** |
| acknowledge | + ment | acknowledgment | acknowledgement** |
| write | + ing | writing | writeing |
| come | + ing | coming | comeing |
| desire | + able | desirable | desireable |
| value | + able | valuable | valueable |

*Exception: Keep e after soft c or g to preserve the soft sound (manageable, noticeable, courageous).

**American English drops the e in judgment and acknowledgment; British keeps it. The CSE uses American spelling.

**Keep the e before consonant suffixes (-ment, -ness, -ful, -ly):**

| Root | + Suffix | Correct | Common Error |
|------|----------|---------|-------------|
| manage | + ment | management | managment |
| require | + ment | requirement | requirment |
| achieve | + ment | achievement | achievment |
| sincere | + ly | sincerely | sincerly |
| complete | + ly | completely | completly |
| immediate | + ly | immediately | immediatly |
| state | + ment | statement | statment |
| arrange | + ment | arrangement | arrangment |

### 4.12 The -ful and -fully Error

A common error involves the suffix -ful (one l) and the adverb form -fully (two l's):

| Correct | Common Error | Rule |
|---------|-------------|------|
| careful | carefull | -ful suffix always has ONE l |
| grateful | gratefull | -ful suffix always has ONE l |
| successful | successfull | -ful suffix always has ONE l |
| carefully | carefuly | -fully (adverb) always has TWO l's |
| gratefully | gratefuly | -fully always has TWO l's |
| successfully | successfuly | -fully always has TWO l's |
| hopeful | hopefull | ONE l |
| hopefully | hopefuly | TWO l's |
| beautiful | beautifull | ONE l |
| beautifully | beautifuly | TWO l's |

**The rule is simple:** The suffix -ful ALWAYS has one l. The adverb ending -fully ALWAYS has two l's (because it is -ful + -ly).

### Check Your Understanding

**1.** What type of error is "goverment"? → **Silent letter omission** (the n from "govern" is dropped)

**2.** Why is "untill" wrong? → **False doubling** — "until" is a single word, not "un" + "till"; it has always been spelled with one l

**3.** What is the correct spelling: "occured" or "occurred"? → **occurred** (stressed final syllable + single vowel + single consonant = double the r before -ed)

**4.** Why is "supercede" wrong? → **False analogy** — unlike "precede" and "intercede" (from Latin cedere), "supersede" comes from Latin "supersedere" (to sit above), making it the only -sede word

**5.** Is it "dependible" or "dependable"? → **dependable** ("depend" is a complete English word → use -able)

**6.** What error type is "Febuary"? → **Silent letter omission** (the first r is dropped because speakers do not pronounce it)

**7.** Is it "dissapear" or "disappear"? → **disappear** (dis- + appear; the prefix dis- has one s, and "appear" starts with a, so no doubling)

**8.** What is wrong with "arguement"? → **Failure to drop final e** — argue drops the e before -ment (exception to the "keep e before consonant suffix" rule)

### Exam Strategies

- Identify the error TYPE being tested before choosing an answer. Ask: "Is this testing double letters? Silent letters? A suffix?"
- When two choices look almost identical, focus on the single letter that differs — that is the error being tested.
- Use root-word decomposition: break the word into prefix + root + suffix and verify each part.
- For unstressed vowels, recall a related word where the vowel IS stressed (define → definite, maintain → maintenance).
- Trust visual memory for high-frequency words — if you have read widely, the correct spelling will "look right."
- Eliminate choices with obvious structural violations first (triple consonants, impossible letter combinations).
- Budget 10-15 seconds per spelling item. If stuck, mark and return — spelling recognition is faster on second pass.
- On the CSE, the correct answer position (A/B/C/D) is randomized — do not look for position patterns.

### Guided Practice

#### Practice 1 (Scaffolded)

Identify the error type and correct spelling:

Word presented: "occurence"

**Think:** Break it down — occur + ence. "Occur" ends in a stressed syllable with a single vowel before a single consonant. Before the suffix -ence (starts with vowel), double the final r. Also, "occur" has two c's.

**Error type:** Consonant doubling failure (missing second r)
**Correct spelling:** occurrence (cc + rr)

#### Practice 2 (Scaffolded)

Word presented: "independant"

**Think:** The suffix is -ent or -ant? The related verb "depend" has a stressed final syllable → use -ence/-ent pattern. Independent ends in -ent.

**Error type:** Suffix confusion (-ant instead of -ent)
**Correct spelling:** independent

#### Practice 3 (Less scaffolding)

Word presented: "dissappoint"

**Think:** Prefix dis- (one s) + appoint (two p's). The prefix does not change the root.

**Error type:** Prefix attachment error (extra s added)
**Correct spelling:** disappoint

#### Practice 4 (Minimal scaffolding)

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Identifying a Consonant Doubling Error

**Question:** Which of the following is spelled correctly?
(A) refered (B) referred (C) reffered (D) referrd

**Step 1:** Identify the root word — "refer."
**Step 2:** Check stress — re-FER (stressed final syllable).
**Step 3:** Check structure — ends in single consonant (r), preceded by single vowel (e).
**Step 4:** Apply rule — all three conditions met → double the final consonant before -ed.
**Step 5:** Result — referred (double r).

**Answer:** (B) referred

### Example 2: Identifying a Suffix Confusion Error

**Question:** Which of the following is spelled correctly?
(A) responsable (B) responseable (C) responsible (D) responsable

**Step 1:** Identify the suffix type — -ible or -able?
**Step 2:** Check the root — "respons-" is NOT a standalone English word (you cannot say "I respons").
**Step 3:** Apply rule — root is not a complete word → use -ible.
**Step 4:** Verify — related word "permission" ends in -ion (not -ation), confirming -ible.

**Answer:** (C) responsible

### Example 3: Identifying a Silent Letter Error

**Question:** Which of the following is spelled correctly?
(A) goverment (B) governmant (C) govermnent (D) government

**Step 1:** Identify the root — "govern."
**Step 2:** Check if root letters are preserved — "govern" contains an n before the final consonant.
**Step 3:** Apply rule — root letters are retained when adding suffix: govern + ment = government.
**Step 4:** Verify — the n is silent in casual speech but required in writing.

**Answer:** (D) government

### Example 4: Identifying a False Analogy Error

**Question:** Which of the following is spelled correctly?
(A) supercede (B) superseed (C) superceed (D) supersede

**Step 1:** Recognize the word family — words ending in the "seed" sound: precede, recede, proceed, succeed, supersede.
**Step 2:** Recall the unique rule — "supersede" is the ONLY word ending in -sede (from Latin supersedere = to sit above).
**Step 3:** All others use -cede (from Latin cedere = to go) or -ceed (proceed, succeed, exceed).
**Step 4:** Eliminate (A) supercede — wrong because it uses -cede with "super." Eliminate (B) and (C) — wrong endings.

**Answer:** (D) supersede

### Example 5: Identifying a Prefix Attachment Error

**Question:** Which of the following is spelled correctly?
(A) mispell (B) misspell (C) misspel (D) mispel

**Step 1:** Identify prefix and root — mis- + spell.
**Step 2:** Check if prefix ends with same letter root begins with — mis ends in s, spell starts with s.
**Step 3:** Apply rule — keep BOTH letters: mis + spell = misspell (double s).
**Step 4:** Verify the root is unchanged — "spell" retains both l's.

**Answer:** (B) misspell

## Key Takeaways

- Spelling errors are systematic, not random — they cluster into 8 predictable categories that you can learn to recognize.
- Unstressed vowels cannot be determined by pronunciation; use root words and related forms to identify the correct vowel (define → definite, maintain → maintenance).
- The consonant-doubling rule requires three conditions: single vowel + single consonant + stressed syllable. If any condition fails, do not double.
- Silent letters persist from root words (govern → government) and historical pronunciation (Wednesday, February). Know the root to know the spelling.
- The -ible/-able distinction is structural: complete root word → -able; incomplete root → -ible.
- When a prefix ends with the same letter the root begins with, keep both letters (mis + spell = misspell, un + necessary = unnecessary).
- False analogy is the most dangerous error type — a correct rule for one word may not apply to a similar-sounding word (harass ≠ embarrass, supersede ≠ precede).
- The suffix -ful always has one l; the adverb form -fully always has two l's.
- American English spelling is the CSE standard — use judgment (not judgement), fulfill (not fulfil), organize (not organise).
- When in doubt, decompose the word into prefix + root + suffix and verify each component independently.

## Summary

Common spelling errors on the Philippine Civil Service Examination follow predictable patterns rooted in the mismatch between English pronunciation and English orthography. The eight major error categories — vowel substitution in unstressed syllables, consonant doubling failures, silent letter omissions and insertions, suffix confusion (-ible/-able, -ence/-ance), prefix attachment errors, transposition errors, false analogy errors, and ie/ei violations — account for the vast majority of spelling mistakes that appear as distractors on the exam.

The key insight is that spelling competence is not about memorizing thousands of individual words but about understanding the structural rules that govern English orthography. When you know that "occur" has a stressed final syllable with a single vowel before a single consonant, you can predict that it doubles the r before any vowel suffix (occurred, occurrence, occurring) without memorizing each form separately. When you know that "depend" is a complete English word, you can predict "dependable" (not "dependible") without having seen the word before.

For the CSE specifically, focus on government and professional vocabulary where these error patterns concentrate: bureaucracy, liaison, questionnaire, personnel, reimbursement, surveillance, superintendent, and similar words that combine multiple difficulty factors (foreign origins, silent letters, double consonants, and unstressed vowels). Master the error categories, and you gain diagnostic power over any word the exam presents.
