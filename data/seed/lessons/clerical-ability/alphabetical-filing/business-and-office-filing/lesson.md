# Business and Office Filing

## Explanations

### Introduction

Business and office filing refers to the rules for arranging company names, organization names, government office names, and other institutional entries in alphabetical order. Unlike personal names — which follow a surname-first convention — business names are filed exactly as written, using the first word of the name as the primary filing unit. This distinction is critical because government clerks handle correspondence, contracts, and records from hundreds of businesses and agencies daily.

In the Philippine Civil Service Examination (CSE) Sub-Professional level, business filing questions test whether an examinee can correctly order entries that include numeric names (like "7-Eleven"), acronyms (like "CSC" or "NBI"), and government offices (like "Department of Education"). These entries follow specialized rules that differ from personal-name filing, and examinees who apply personal-name rules to business names will make errors.

Mastering business and office filing is essential for government employees who maintain vendor lists, supplier directories, agency correspondence files, and interoffice records. Offices such as the COA, DBM, and DILG regularly file documents from both private companies and other government agencies, making these rules part of everyday clerical operations.

### Why Business and Office Filing Is Tested in the CSE

- Government employees file correspondence from private businesses and other agencies.
- Vendor and supplier records must be retrievable by company name.
- Interagency communications require consistent filing of government office names.
- Clerks must distinguish between acronyms, full names, and numeric entries.
- Accurate business filing prevents misfiled contracts and procurement documents.
- Filing errors in government transactions can delay public services.
- The skill demonstrates attention to detail and knowledge of standardized systems.

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. Rearranging business names into surname-first format as if they were personal names.
2. Filing "The" as the first unit instead of moving it to the end.
3. Filing numbers by their digit value instead of spelling them out.
4. Expanding acronyms before filing instead of filing them letter by letter.
5. Filing government offices under "Department of" or "Bureau of" instead of the distinctive word.
6. Treating hyphenated business names as two separate units instead of one unit.
7. Ignoring articles and prepositions that are part of the official business name.
8. Confusing the filing order of acronyms with the filing order of spelled-out names.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- File company names using the "as written" rule correctly.
- Identify and relocate "The" to the end of a business name for filing purposes.
- Convert numeric business names to their spelled-out equivalents for filing.
- File acronyms letter by letter without expanding them.
- Rearrange government office names to place the distinctive word first.
- Distinguish between the two government filing conventions and apply the CSE-standard method.
- Arrange mixed lists containing companies, acronyms, and government offices.
- Apply these rules quickly under exam time pressure.

### 4.1 Company Names: The "As Written" Rule

The fundamental rule for business names is simple: file the name exactly as it is written. The first word of the business name becomes the primary filing unit, the second word becomes the second unit, and so on.

**Rule:** File business names in the order the words appear. Do not rearrange them.

| Business Name | Unit 1 | Unit 2 | Unit 3 |
|---------------|--------|--------|--------|
| Manila Water | Manila | Water | — |
| Globe Telecom | Globe | Telecom | — |
| San Miguel Corporation | San | Miguel | Corporation |
| Jollibee Foods Corporation | Jollibee | Foods | Corporation |

**Comparison with personal names:**

| Type | Original | Filed As |
|------|----------|----------|
| Personal name | Juan Santos | Santos, Juan |
| Business name | Santos Trucking | Santos Trucking |

Notice that "Santos Trucking" is NOT rearranged. It files under "S" because "Santos" is the first word of the business name — not because it resembles a surname.

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Business names are filed as written because companies choose their names as complete units — "Globe Telecom" is a single identity, not a person with a surname. Rearranging would create ambiguity: should "San Miguel Corporation" file under "Miguel" or "Corporation"? The as-written rule eliminates this confusion by treating the first word as the natural access point, which is how people naturally search for a company.

#### The Exception: "The" at the Beginning

When a business name begins with "The," move it to the end in parentheses. It is not considered a filing unit.

| Original Name | Filed As | Unit 1 | Unit 2 |
|---------------|----------|--------|--------|
| The Manila Hotel | Manila Hotel (The) | Manila | Hotel |
| The Philippine Star | Philippine Star (The) | Philippine | Star |
| The Freeman | Freeman (The) | Freeman | — |

**Important:** Only "The" at the beginning is moved. Articles appearing elsewhere in the name stay in place.

Example: "Steak of the Art" files as written — "Steak" is Unit 1.

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "All articles (a, an, the) should be ignored when filing business names."
>
> **Why it fails:** Only "The" at the *beginning* of a name is moved to the end. If a business name is "A-1 Delivery Services," the "A" is part of the alphanumeric name and is filed as the first unit. Similarly, "An's Bakeshop" uses "An" as a proper name, not an article.
>
> **Correct model:** Move only the initial "The" to the end. All other words — including "A" or "An" that are integral parts of the business name — remain in place.

### 4.2 Numeric Business Names

When a business name contains a number, file it AS IF the number were spelled out in English.

| Original Name | Filed As If Spelled | Unit 1 | Unit 2 |
|---------------|-------------------|--------|--------|
| 7-Eleven | Seven-Eleven | Seven | Eleven |
| 3M Philippines | Three M Philippines | Three | M |
| 8th Wonder Café | Eighth Wonder Café | Eighth | Wonder |
| 1st Choice Printing | First Choice Printing | First | Choice |
| 24/7 Express | Twentyfour Seven Express | Twentyfour | Seven |
| 100 Islands Resort | One Hundred Islands Resort | One | Hundred |

**Key points:**

- Ordinal numbers are spelled as ordinals: 1st = First, 2nd = Second, 3rd = Third.
- Cardinal numbers are spelled as cardinals: 7 = Seven, 100 = One Hundred.
- Hyphenated number-names remain hyphenated when spelled: 7-Eleven = Seven-Eleven.
- The spelled-out form determines alphabetical position.

**Filing order example:**

Which comes first: "7-Eleven" or "Seventeen Magazine"?

- 7-Eleven files as "Seven-Eleven"
- Seventeen Magazine files as "Seventeen Magazine"

Compare: Seven vs. Seventeen
- S-e-v-e-n vs. S-e-v-e-n-t-e-e-n
- "Seven" is shorter and all letters match → Seven comes first.

Correct order:
1. 7-Eleven (Seven-Eleven)
2. Seventeen Magazine

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Numbers are spelled out because a purely numeric filing system would place all numbered businesses at the very beginning of the file (before "A"), creating an inconsistent hybrid system. By converting numbers to their word equivalents, every entry — whether it starts with a letter or a digit — participates in the same alphabetical sequence. This ensures that "7-Eleven" is findable by someone thinking "Seven" and browsing the S section.

### 4.3 Acronyms and Abbreviations

Acronyms and initialisms are filed letter by letter, exactly as written. Do NOT expand them to their full form before filing.

| Entry | Filed As | Unit 1 |
|-------|----------|--------|
| CSC | C-S-C | CSC |
| NBI | N-B-I | NBI |
| BIR | B-I-R | BIR |
| SSS | S-S-S | SSS |
| GSIS | G-S-I-S | GSIS |
| PhilHealth | PhilHealth | PhilHealth |
| Pag-IBIG | Pag-IBIG | PagIBIG |

**Rules for acronyms:**

1. File each letter of the acronym in sequence.
2. Do not insert spaces between letters — treat the acronym as one continuous unit.
3. Do not expand the acronym (CSC is filed under C, not under "Civil").
4. Acronyms with no spaces are treated as single filing units.

**Distinguishing acronyms from abbreviations:**

| Type | Example | How to File |
|------|---------|-------------|
| Acronym (all caps, no periods) | NBI | As one unit: NBI |
| Abbreviation with periods | N.B.I. | Ignore periods, file as: NBI |
| Mixed case acronym | PhilHealth | As one unit: PhilHealth |
| Hyphenated acronym | Pag-IBIG | Ignore hyphen, file as: PagIBIG |

**Filing order example:**

Arrange: CSC, COA, DBM, DENR, DILG

Compare letter by letter:
- COA: C-O-A
- CSC: C-S-C
- DBM: D-B-M
- DENR: D-E-N-R
- DILG: D-I-L-G

First letters: C, C, D, D, D

Among C entries: COA vs. CSC
- Second letters: O vs. S → O comes before S

Among D entries: DBM vs. DENR vs. DILG
- Second letters: B vs. E vs. I → B before E before I

Correct order:
1. COA
2. CSC
3. DBM
4. DENR
5. DILG

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Acronyms are filed as written because they function as the organization's *name* in everyday use. Government employees search for "CSC" — not "Civil Service Commission" — when looking through files. Filing by the acronym matches how people actually think about and search for these organizations, making retrieval faster and more intuitive.

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "Acronyms should be expanded to their full name before filing because that's the 'real' name."
>
> **Why it fails:** If you expanded CSC to "Civil Service Commission," it would file under C-I, far from where anyone would look for it. Similarly, NBI expanded to "National Bureau of Investigation" would file under N-A, not N-B where clerks expect it.
>
> **Correct model:** File acronyms exactly as they appear. The acronym IS the filing name. If a cross-reference is needed, create a separate entry under the full name that says "See [acronym]."

### 4.4 Government Office Names

Government offices present a special filing challenge because many begin with generic words like "Department of," "Bureau of," "Office of," or "Commission on." If filed as written, dozens of agencies would cluster under "D" for "Department" or "B" for "Bureau," making retrieval slow and impractical.

**Primary rule (CSE standard):** File government offices by the DISTINCTIVE word — the word that identifies the specific agency — not by the generic organizational term.

| Original Name | Filed As | Unit 1 | Unit 2 | Unit 3 |
|---------------|----------|--------|--------|--------|
| Department of Education | Education, Department of | Education | Department | of |
| Department of Health | Health, Department of | Health | Department | of |
| Bureau of Internal Revenue | Internal Revenue, Bureau of | Internal | Revenue | Bureau |
| Office of the President | President, Office of the | President | Office | of the |
| Commission on Audit | Audit, Commission on | Audit | Commission | on |
| Department of the Interior and Local Government | Interior and Local Government, Department of the | Interior | and | Local |

**How to identify the distinctive word:**

The distinctive word is the word that makes the agency unique. Remove "Department of," "Bureau of," "Office of," "Commission on," or similar generic prefixes — what remains is the distinctive element.

- Department of **Education** → distinctive word: Education
- Bureau of **Internal Revenue** → distinctive word: Internal Revenue
- Department of **Budget** and Management → distinctive word: Budget
- Office of the **Ombudsman** → distinctive word: Ombudsman

**Filing order example:**

Arrange these government offices:
- Department of Education (DepEd)
- Department of Health (DOH)
- Department of Budget and Management (DBM)
- Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG)
- Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE)

Step 1: Identify distinctive words.
- Education
- Health
- Budget and Management
- Interior and Local Government
- Labor and Employment

Step 2: Arrange by distinctive word alphabetically.
1. Budget and Management, Department of (DBM)
2. Education, Department of (DepEd)
3. Health, Department of (DOH)
4. Interior and Local Government, Department of the (DILG)
5. Labor and Employment, Department of (DOLE)

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Filing by the distinctive word distributes government agencies across the entire alphabet instead of clustering them all under "D" for Department or "B" for Bureau. If 20 departments all filed under "D," a clerk would need to read deep into each entry to find the right one. By leading with the distinctive word, each agency occupies a unique alphabetical position — "Education" under E, "Health" under H — making retrieval as fast as looking up a single word.

#### Alternative Convention: Filing As Written

Some filing systems — particularly those used in libraries and some private organizations — file government names exactly as written, without rearrangement:

| Method | "Department of Education" Files Under |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| Distinctive word first (CSE standard) | E (Education, Department of) |
| As written | D (Department of Education) |

**For the CSE:** Use the distinctive-word-first method unless the question explicitly states otherwise. This is the standard taught in Philippine civil service training materials and is the method that produces the most efficient retrieval in government filing systems.

**When you might see the as-written method:** If a question provides a filing rule that says "file all entries exactly as written," follow that instruction — even for government offices. Always read the question's instructions first.

### 4.5 Mixed Filing: Putting It All Together

In the CSE, you will encounter lists that mix company names, acronyms, numeric names, and government offices. Apply each rule to its appropriate entry type:

| Company with "The" | Move "The" to end | The Freeman → F |
| Numeric name | Spell out number | 7-Eleven → S (Seven) |

### 4.6 Special Cases and Edge Rules

#### Hyphenated Business Names

Hyphenated words in business names are treated as one filing unit (ignore the hyphen, no space).

| Name | Filing Unit |
|------|-------------|
| Coca-Cola | CocaCola (one unit) |
| Pag-IBIG | PagIBIG (one unit) |
| Seven-Eleven | SevenEleven (one unit) |

#### Possessives

Ignore the apostrophe. File the word as if the apostrophe were not there.

| Name | Filed As |
|------|----------|
| McDonald's | McDonalds |
| Wendy's | Wendys |
| Andok's | Andoks |

#### Single Letters as Business Names

A single letter used as a business name is filed as a single unit before full words beginning with that letter.

Example: "A-1 Delivery" files before "Ace Hardware" because the single unit "A" (with "1" spelled as "One") comes before "Ace."

#### Prepositions and Conjunctions Within Names

Words like "of," "and," "the," "for," and "in" that appear WITHIN a business name (not at the beginning) are filed as written — they count as filing units.

| Name | Unit 1 | Unit 2 | Unit 3 |
|------|--------|--------|--------|
| Bank of the Philippine Islands | Bank | of | the |
| Bread and Butter Bakery | Bread | and | Butter |
| Department of Trade and Industry | Trade | and | Industry |

### Exam Strategies

- Read the question instructions first — some questions specify "file as written" for all entries.
- Identify the entry type immediately: company, acronym, number, or government office.
- Convert numeric names to spelled-out form mentally before comparing.
- For government offices, cross out "Department of" or "Bureau of" mentally and focus on the distinctive word.
- When comparing acronyms, go letter by letter — do not try to recall what the acronym stands for.
- Watch for "The" at the beginning — it is the most common trap in business filing questions.
- If unsure whether an entry is an acronym or a word, check if it is all capitals with no vowel pattern (CSC, DBM, NBI = acronyms; TESDA, PAGASA = words/acronyms treated the same way).
- Use elimination: identify the entry that clearly comes first or last, then work inward.

### Memory Aids

#### The TANG Rule for Business Filing

**T** — "The" moves to the end
**A** — Acronyms file as written (letter by letter)
**N** — Numbers are spelled out
**G** — Government offices use the distinctive word first

#### Government Filing Mnemonic

"**Drop the Department, Keep the Key word.**"

Think of it as answering the question: "Department of WHAT?" The answer to "what" is your filing word.

- Department of what? → Education → File under E
- Bureau of what? → Internal Revenue → File under I
- Office of what? → The President → File under P

### Worked Example 1

**Question:** Arrange in correct filing order:
- Department of Tourism (DOT)
- The Manila Hotel
- NBI
- 3M Philippines
- Globe Telecom

**Step 1:** Identify entry types.
- Department of Tourism → government office
- The Manila Hotel → company with "The"
- NBI → acronym

## Key Takeaways

- Business names are filed exactly as written — the first word is the primary filing unit.
- "The" at the beginning of a business name is moved to the end and is not a filing unit.
- Numbers in business names are spelled out: "7-Eleven" becomes "Seven-Eleven" for filing purposes.
- Acronyms are filed letter by letter as written — never expand them to the full name before filing.
- Government offices are filed by the distinctive word, not by "Department of" or "Bureau of."
- The distinctive word is what makes the agency unique — answer "Department of WHAT?" to find it.
- Hyphenated business names are treated as one unit (ignore the hyphen).
- Prepositions and conjunctions within a name are filed as regular units.
- The CSE uses the distinctive-word-first method for government offices as its standard.
- Use the TANG mnemonic: The → end, Acronyms → as written, Numbers → spelled out, Government → distinctive word first.

## Summary

Business and office filing covers the rules for alphabetically arranging company names, numeric business names, acronyms, and government office names. Unlike personal names, business names are filed in the order they are written — the first word serves as the primary filing unit. The key exception is "The" at the beginning of a name, which is moved to the end. Numbers appearing in business names are converted to their spelled-out English equivalents so they can participate in the standard alphabetical sequence. Acronyms like CSC, NBI, and BIR are filed letter by letter exactly as written, without expanding them to their full organizational names.

Government office names require special treatment because filing them as written would cluster dozens of agencies under "Department" or "Bureau." The CSE-standard method is to file by the distinctive word — the word that uniquely identifies the agency. "Department of Education" files under "Education," "Bureau of Internal Revenue" files under "Internal Revenue," and "Office of the President" files under "President." This distributes government entries across the alphabet and makes retrieval efficient.

Mastering these rules requires practice with mixed lists that combine all entry types. The TANG mnemonic (The → end, Acronyms → as written, Numbers → spelled out, Government → distinctive word first) provides a quick decision framework during the exam. Since clerical ability questions are time-pressured, the ability to quickly identify entry types and apply the correct rule without hesitation is the difference between a passing and failing score on this section.
