# Shape Patterns

## Explanations

### Introduction

Abstract reasoning is the ability to identify patterns, logical rules, and relationships between visual elements without relying on language or prior knowledge. Unlike verbal or numerical reasoning, abstract reasoning tests your capacity to think logically using shapes, symbols, and spatial relationships alone.

Shape patterns are sequences of geometric figures that follow specific rules. Your task is to identify the rule governing the sequence and predict what comes next — or identify which figure does not belong. The three fundamental transformations tested in the Philippine Civil Service Examination are **rotation**, **reflection**, and **transformation**.

**Why abstract reasoning matters in the Civil Service Exam:**

- It measures raw analytical intelligence independent of educational background
- It evaluates problem-solving ability under time pressure
- It tests pattern recognition — a skill critical for data analysis, process improvement, and policy interpretation in government work
- It is language-neutral, making it a fair assessment across diverse examinees

**Why memorization fails here:**

Abstract reasoning questions are generated from infinite combinations of shapes and rules. You cannot memorize answers. Instead, you must develop the skill of observing relationships between figures, detecting the rule, and applying it consistently. Speed comes from practiced pattern recognition, not from recall.

**Common mistakes examinees make:**

- Focusing on only one element while ignoring others
- Confusing rotation with reflection
- Assuming patterns are more complex than they actually are
- Not checking all answer choices before selecting one
- Rushing without establishing the rule first

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, learners should be able to:

- Define abstract reasoning and explain its role in the CSE
- Identify rotation patterns (clockwise and counterclockwise at 90°, 180°, 270°)
- Identify reflection patterns (horizontal, vertical, and diagonal mirror images)
- Recognize transformation patterns (size changes, shading, element addition/removal)
- Analyze multi-rule sequences where two or more changes occur simultaneously
- Apply systematic elimination to narrow answer choices efficiently
- Distinguish between rotation and reflection when they appear similar
- Solve CSE-style abstract reasoning questions within time constraints

### 4.1 What Is Abstract Reasoning?
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The principle behind this operation follows from the fundamental properties of arithmetic. Understanding the "why" — not just the "how" — lets you recognize when to apply this method in unfamiliar problem contexts on the CSE.


Abstract reasoning is the process of identifying logical relationships between visual elements. In a typical CSE question, you are shown a sequence of figures that follow a hidden rule. Your job is to determine that rule and select the answer choice that continues the pattern.

**How abstract reasoning questions work:**

1. You are given a sequence of 3–5 figures arranged in order
2. Each figure changes from the previous one according to a consistent rule
3. You must identify the rule by comparing adjacent figures
4. You select the answer choice that correctly applies the same rule to continue the sequence

**The key insight:** The first two or three figures establish the rule. Every subsequent figure — including the correct answer — must follow that same rule exactly.

**What to observe in each figure:**

- Shape type (triangle, square, circle, arrow, star, polygon)
- Orientation (which direction it points)
- Position (where it sits within the frame)
- Size (small, medium, large)
- Shading (empty, half-filled, fully filled, striped)
- Count (how many elements appear)
- Internal details (dots, lines, smaller shapes inside)

**Real-life analogy:** Think of abstract reasoning like watching a traffic light cycle. Green → Yellow → Red → Green. Once you observe the pattern, you can predict what comes next without anyone telling you the rule. Shape patterns work the same way — observe, detect the rule, predict.

### 4.2 Rotation Patterns

Rotation means turning a figure around a fixed center point by a specific angle. The shape itself does not change — only its orientation changes. Think of it like turning a steering wheel: the wheel stays the same shape, but its position rotates.

**Key rotation angles:**

| Rotation | Description | Visual Effect |
|----------|-------------|---------------|
| 90° clockwise | Quarter turn right | Top moves to right side |
| 90° counterclockwise | Quarter turn left | Top moves to left side |
| 180° | Half turn | Figure flips upside down |
| 270° clockwise | Three-quarter turn right | Same as 90° counterclockwise |
| 45° | Eighth turn | Diagonal shift |

**How to identify rotation:**

1. Pick a distinctive feature of the shape (a pointed end, a marked corner, a dot)
2. Track where that feature moves from one figure to the next
3. Determine the angle and direction of movement
4. Verify the same rotation applies to ALL transitions in the sequence

#### Alternating Rotation Patterns

Some sequences alternate between two rotation amounts. For example:

- Step 1 → Step 2: rotate 90° clockwise
- Step 2 → Step 3: rotate 45° clockwise
- Step 3 → Step 4: rotate 90° clockwise
- Step 4 → Step 5: rotate 45° clockwise

**CSE Tip:** When the rotation amount seems inconsistent, check if it alternates between two values. This is a common medium-difficulty pattern.

#### How to Distinguish Rotation from Other Changes

| If you see... | It is likely... |
|---------------|-----------------|
| Same shape, different orientation | Rotation |
| Shape appears flipped/mirrored | Reflection (not rotation) |
| Shape changes size or gains elements | Transformation |
| Shape moves position but keeps orientation | Translation (movement) |

**Critical rule:** In pure rotation, the shape is identical — same size, same internal details, same shading. Only the orientation changes. If anything else changes, you are dealing with a combined pattern.

### 4.3 Reflection Patterns
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** When you follow this procedure, you're exploiting a mathematical invariant — something that stays constant regardless of how you manipulate the numbers. Identifying that invariant is the key to solving problems efficiently rather than memorizing steps.


Reflection means creating a mirror image of a figure across an axis (a mirror line). Unlike rotation, reflection reverses the figure — left becomes right, or top becomes bottom.

**Key concept:** If you place a mirror along the axis of reflection, the reflected figure is what you would see in that mirror.

**Types of reflection:**

| Type | Mirror Line | Effect |
|------|-------------|--------|
| Horizontal reflection | Horizontal axis (left-right) | Top and bottom swap |
| Vertical reflection | Vertical axis (up-down) | Left and right swap |
| Diagonal reflection | 45° diagonal line | Corners swap diagonally |

#### Rotation vs. Reflection — The Critical Difference

This is the single most common source of errors on the CSE. Here is how to tell them apart:

| Feature | Rotation | Reflection |
|---------|----------|------------|
| Handedness | Preserved (same "chirality") | Reversed (mirror image) |
| Internal details | Stay on same relative side | Swap sides |
| Test method | Can you spin the original to match? | Do you need a mirror? |
| Asymmetric marker | Stays on same side | Jumps to opposite side |

**The asymmetric marker test:** If a figure has a dot, notch, or marking on one side, track it:
- If the marker stays on the same relative side after the change → **rotation**
- If the marker jumps to the opposite side → **reflection**

### 4.4 Transformation Patterns

Transformation is a broader category that includes any systematic change to a figure beyond rotation and reflection. Transformations modify the figure itself — its size, shading, number of elements, or internal structure.

**Common transformation types:**

| Transformation | What Changes | Example |
|----------------|--------------|---------|
| Resizing | Shape gets larger or smaller | Small circle → medium → large |
| Shading progression | Fill changes systematically | Empty → half-filled → fully filled |
| Element addition | New parts are added | 1 dot → 2 dots → 3 dots |
| Element removal | Parts are removed | 4 lines → 3 lines → 2 lines |
| Shape replacement | One shape becomes another | Circle → square → triangle |
| Position shift | Element moves within frame | Dot moves from corner to corner |

### 4.5 Pattern Analysis Techniques
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** This shortcut works because it's a special case of the more general rule. By understanding the underlying principle, you can verify your answer logically even if you forget the exact formula under exam pressure.


When facing an abstract reasoning question, use this systematic approach:

**Step 1: Scan all figures quickly (5 seconds)**

Look at the entire sequence at once. Do not fixate on the first figure. Get a general impression of what is changing.

**Step 2: Compare adjacent pairs**

Compare Figure 1 to Figure 2. What changed? Then compare Figure 2 to Figure 3. Is the same change repeated?

**Step 3: Identify the rule**

State the rule in words: "The arrow rotates 90° clockwise each step" or "One dot is added each step."

**Step 4: Verify the rule**

Apply your rule to ALL transitions in the sequence. If it works for every pair, you have the correct rule.

**Step 5: Apply the rule to find the answer**

Apply the rule one more time to predict the next figure. Match your prediction against the answer choices.

**Step 6: Eliminate wrong answers**

If your predicted answer matches one choice exactly, select it. If not, eliminate choices that clearly violate the rule.

### 4.6 Common Rotation Relationships

Here are the most frequently tested rotation patterns on the CSE:

**Clockwise rotation sequences:**

| Starting Direction | After 90° CW | After 180° | After 270° CW |
|-------------------|--------------|------------|----------------|
| Up (↑) | Right (→) | Down (↓) | Left (←) |
| Right (→) | Down (↓) | Left (←) | Up (↑) |

### 4.9 Practical Applications of Abstract Reasoning

Abstract reasoning is not just an exam skill — it directly maps to workplace competencies valued in the Philippine Civil Service:

**Process analysis:** Identifying patterns in workflows, recognizing inefficiencies, and predicting outcomes based on observed trends.

**Data interpretation:** Spotting visual patterns in charts, graphs, and dashboards without needing explicit labels.

**Problem decomposition:** Breaking complex problems into component rules, just as you break a shape pattern into individual transformations.

**Decision-making under uncertainty:** When you cannot memorize every possible scenario, you rely on pattern recognition to make informed decisions quickly.

**Systems thinking:** Understanding how multiple rules interact simultaneously — the same skill tested by multi-rule transformation questions.

**Quality control:** Detecting anomalies (the "odd one out") in processes, documents, or data — directly tested by odd-one-out abstract reasoning questions.

### 4.10 Step-by-Step Abstract Reasoning Strategies

#### The SCAN Method

Use this systematic approach for every abstract reasoning question:

**S — Spot the change:** What is different between Figure 1 and Figure 2?

**C — Confirm the pattern:** Does the same change happen between Figure 2 and Figure 3? And Figure 3 and Figure 4?

**A — Apply the rule:** What should the next figure look like if the rule continues?

**N — Narrow the choices:** Which answer choice matches your prediction? Eliminate those that do not.

#### Solving Under Time Pressure

The CSE gives approximately 30-45 seconds per abstract reasoning question. Here is how to use that time:

- **0-5 seconds:** Scan all figures for the most obvious change
- **5-15 seconds:** Confirm the rule by checking at least two transitions
- **15-25 seconds:** Predict the answer mentally
- **25-35 seconds:** Match your prediction to the choices and select
- **35-45 seconds:** If stuck, eliminate obviously wrong choices and make your best guess

**When to guess and move on:**

If after 30 seconds you cannot identify the rule, eliminate any choices that are clearly wrong (wrong shape, wrong size, wrong orientation) and select from the remaining options. Never spend more than 60 seconds on a single question — the time is better spent on questions you can solve.

#### Elimination Strategies

When you cannot determine the exact answer, eliminate choices that violate basic rules:

1. **Wrong shape type:** If the sequence uses only triangles, eliminate any choice with a different shape
2. **Wrong orientation:** If figures rotate clockwise, eliminate any choice that shows counterclockwise rotation
3. **Wrong count:** If elements increase by one each step, eliminate choices with the wrong number
4. **Wrong shading:** If shading progresses from empty to full, eliminate choices that go backward
5. **Too complex:** If the sequence is simple, eliminate choices that introduce new elements not present in the sequence

### 4.11 Common Errors in Abstract Reasoning

#### Error 1: Focusing on Only One Element

Many figures contain multiple elements (an outer shape, inner dots, shading, orientation). Examinees often identify one rule and ignore others.

**Fix:** After identifying one rule, ask yourself: "Is anything else changing?" Check shape, orientation, size, shading, count, and position independently.

#### Error 2: Confusing Rotation with Reflection

A triangle pointing right could be either a 90° clockwise rotation of an upward triangle OR a vertical reflection. The difference matters because it predicts different next steps.

**Fix:** Use the asymmetric marker test. Track a distinctive feature — if it stays on the same relative side, it is rotation. If it swaps sides, it is reflection.

#### Error 3: Assuming Linear Progression

Not all patterns increase linearly. Some alternate (A-B-A-B), some cycle (A-B-C-A-B-C), and some follow non-linear rules (1-2-4-8).

**Fix:** Always check at least three transitions before committing to a rule. Two data points can suggest a linear pattern that is actually cyclic.

#### Error 4: Choosing Visually Similar but Logically Wrong Answers

The CSE deliberately includes distractor choices that look similar to the correct answer but violate the rule in a subtle way (e.g., correct rotation angle but wrong shading).

**Fix:** After selecting an answer, verify it satisfies ALL identified rules, not just the most obvious one.

#### Error 5: Overthinking Simple Patterns

Some questions have straightforward rules (rotate 90° each step). Examinees sometimes invent complex explanations when the simple one works.

**Fix:** Start with the simplest possible explanation. Only add complexity if the simple rule fails to explain all transitions.

### 4.12 Estimation and Elimination Techniques

#### Rapid Elimination Checklist

When time is short, use this checklist to quickly eliminate wrong answers:

- **Shape mismatch:** Does the choice use the same shape type as the sequence? If not, eliminate.
- **Size mismatch:** Is the choice the expected size based on the progression? If not, eliminate.
- **Orientation mismatch:** Does the choice point in the direction predicted by the rotation rule? If not, eliminate.
- **Element count mismatch:** Does the choice have the correct number of internal elements? If not, eliminate.
- **Shading mismatch:** Does the choice have the expected fill level? If not, eliminate.

#### The "Odd One Out" Strategy

For odd-one-out questions (which figure does NOT belong?), flip the approach:

1. Find the rule that 3 or 4 figures share
2. The figure that violates that rule is the answer
3. Common shared rules: same rotation direction, same shading pattern, same element count, same shape family

### Mini Practice Set

**1.** An arrow points UP in Step 1, RIGHT in Step 2, DOWN in Step 3. What direction does it point in Step 4?

**Answer:** Left

**Explanation:** The arrow rotates 90° clockwise each step. Up → Right → Down → Left follows the clockwise cycle.

**2.** A triangle points up in Step 1 and down in Step 2. What is the transformation?

**Answer:** 180° rotation (or horizontal reflection)

**Explanation:** The triangle flips completely. This is a 180° rotation. Note: for an equilateral triangle, 180° rotation and horizontal reflection produce the same result.

**3.** A sequence shows: 1 dot, 2 dots, 3 dots, 4 dots. How many dots appear in Step 5?

**Answer:** 5 dots

**Explanation:** The count increases by 1 each step. This is a linear progression: n + 1.

**4.** A square has a dot in the top-left corner. After transformation, the dot is in the top-right corner. What happened?

**Answer:** Vertical reflection (left-right mirror)

**Explanation:** The dot moved from left to right while staying at the top. This is a reflection across the vertical axis.

**5.** A figure rotates 90° clockwise AND gains one dot per step. Step 1 shows an upward arrow with 1 dot. What does Step 3 look like?

**Answer:** A downward arrow with 3 dots

**Explanation:** After two 90° clockwise rotations: Up → Right → Down. Dots: 1 → 2 → 3.

**6.** In a sequence, shapes alternate: circle, square, circle, square. What comes next?

**Answer:** Circle

**Explanation:** The pattern is a two-element cycle (A-B-A-B-A). Position 5 follows the same rule as positions 1 and 3.

**7.** A pentagon has shading that fills one-fifth more each step. Step 1 is empty. What does Step 4 look like?

**Answer:** Three-fifths filled

**Explanation:** Step 1: 0/5, Step 2: 1/5, Step 3: 2/5, Step 4: 3/5 filled.

**8.** An L-shaped figure has the short arm pointing right in Step 1 and pointing up in Step 2. What rotation occurred?

**Answer:** 90° counterclockwise

**Explanation:** Right → Up is a counterclockwise rotation. Clockwise would go Right → Down.

**9.** A sequence shows shapes with 3, 4, 5, 6 sides. What is the rule?

**Answer:** The number of sides increases by 1 each step

**Explanation:** Triangle (3) → Square (4) → Pentagon (5) → Hexagon (6). Linear side-count progression.

**10.** A figure has a dot on its left side. After transformation, the dot is still on the left side but the figure is upside down. What happened?

**Answer:** 180° rotation

**Explanation:** In rotation, the dot maintains its relative position to the shape. The figure flipped upside down (180°) but the dot stayed on the same relative side — confirming rotation, not reflection.

**11.** A grid shows: Row 1 has empty shapes, Row 2 has half-filled shapes, Row 3 has fully filled shapes. Column 1 has circles, Column 2 has squares. What goes in Row 3, Column 2?

**Answer:** A fully filled square

**Explanation:** Row 3 rule = fully filled. Column 2 rule = square. Combine both: fully filled square.

**12.** An arrow rotates 45° clockwise each step. It starts pointing up (0°). After 4 steps, where does it point?

**Answer:** 180° (pointing down)

**Explanation:** 4 steps × 45° = 180° total rotation. Up + 180° = Down.

**13.** A sequence alternates between a shape and its reflection: normal F, mirrored F, normal F, mirrored F. What comes next?

**Answer:** Normal F

**Explanation:** Odd positions (1, 3, 5) show normal. Even positions (2, 4) show mirrored. Position 5 is odd = normal.

**14.** A figure grows by adding one line segment per step. Step 1 has 2 lines, Step 2 has 3 lines, Step 3 has 4 lines. How many lines in Step 6?

**Answer:** 7 lines

**Explanation:** Starting at 2, adding 1 per step: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7.

**15.** Two rules operate: the outer shape rotates 90° CW and the inner dot moves to the next corner clockwise. If Step 1 has a square with dot in top-left, what does Step 2 look like?

**Answer:** Square rotated 90° CW with dot in top-right corner

**Explanation:** The square rotates 90° CW. The dot independently moves one corner clockwise (top-left → top-right).

**16.** A sequence shows: small circle, medium circle, large circle, medium circle, small circle. What comes next?

**Answer:** Medium circle

**Explanation:** The size oscillates: small → medium → large → medium → small → medium. It bounces back and forth.

**17.** A triangle has a stripe pattern. In Step 1, stripes are horizontal. In Step 2, stripes are vertical. In Step 3, stripes are horizontal. What is the pattern?

**Answer:** Stripe orientation alternates between horizontal and vertical

**Explanation:** H-V-H-V is a two-state alternation. Step 4 would have vertical stripes.

**18.** In an odd-one-out question, four figures show arrows pointing right and one shows an arrow pointing left. Which is the odd one out?

**Answer:** The arrow pointing left

**Explanation:** Four figures share the same orientation (right). The one that differs (left) is the odd one out.

**19.** A shape sequence shows: empty triangle, empty square, filled triangle, filled square. What are the two rules?

**Answer:** Rule 1: shapes alternate triangle-square. Rule 2: shading alternates every two steps (empty-empty-filled-filled).

**Explanation:** Shape cycles every 1 step (T-S-T-S). Shading cycles every 2 steps (E-E-F-F). Next would be: empty triangle.

**20.** A complex figure has three elements: an outer hexagon that rotates 60° per step, an inner star that alternates between 5 and 6 points, and a dot that moves clockwise through the hexagon's vertices. After 3 steps from the starting position, describe the figure.

**Answer:** Hexagon rotated 180°, 6-pointed star inside, dot at the vertex 3 positions clockwise from start.

**Explanation:** Hexagon: 3 × 60° = 180° rotation. Star: alternates 5-6-5-6, so after 3 steps from 5-point start = 6-point. Dot: moves 3 vertices clockwise.

### Quick Recap

**Rotation patterns:**
- Shapes turn around a center point by a fixed angle
- Clockwise cycle: Up → Right → Down → Left
- Counterclockwise cycle: Up → Left → Down → Right
- Common angles: 45°, 90°, 180°, 270°
- The shape itself does not change — only orientation changes

**Reflection patterns:**
- Shapes are mirrored across an axis
- Vertical reflection: left and right swap
- Horizontal reflection: top and bottom swap
- The asymmetric marker test distinguishes reflection from rotation
- Reflected figures reverse handedness; rotated figures preserve it

**Transformation patterns:**
- Shapes change in size, shading, element count, or type
- Multiple rules can operate simultaneously
- Track each changing element independently
- Common progressions: linear increase, alternation, cycling

**Key strategies:**
- Use the SCAN method: Spot, Confirm, Apply, Narrow
- Compare adjacent figures to find the rule
- Verify the rule works for ALL transitions
- Eliminate choices that violate any identified rule
- Start simple — only add complexity if needed

**Common traps:**
- Confusing rotation with reflection (use the marker test)
- Ignoring secondary rules in multi-rule patterns
- Overthinking simple patterns
- Choosing visually similar but logically wrong distractors

### Memory Aids

- **Clockwise memory:** Think of a clock — hands move Right from the top. "ClocK-Right" = Up → Right → Down → Left.
- **Reflection test:** "Did the dot jump sides? Then it is a mirror." Dots that jump = reflection. Dots that stay = rotation.
- **Multi-rule check:** After finding one rule, always ask "What ELSE is changing?" Count the changing elements on your fingers.
- **Grid solving:** "Rows share one rule, columns share another." Solve the row rule first, then verify with the column rule.
- **Elimination shortcut:** "Shape wrong? Shade wrong? Count wrong? Cross it out." Eliminate on the first mismatch you find.
- **Time management:** "30 seconds to solve, 10 seconds to guess." If you cannot find the rule in 30 seconds, eliminate and guess.
- **Alternation detector:** "Same-different-same-different = alternation." If the pattern flips back and forth, it is a two-state cycle.
- **Progression detector:** "Getting bigger? Getting more? Getting darker? = progression." Steady increase or decrease in one direction.

The pattern-detection strategy works by systematically testing each transformation type until one explains all observed changes.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Rotation Pattern

**Problem:** An arrow points UP in Step 1, RIGHT in Step 2, DOWN in Step 3. What direction does it point in Step 4?

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Identify the change between steps: UP → RIGHT = 90° clockwise
2. Verify: RIGHT → DOWN = 90° clockwise ✅ (consistent)
3. Apply the rule to Step 3: DOWN + 90° clockwise = LEFT
4. **Answer: LEFT**

---

### Example 2: Reflection Pattern

**Problem:** A triangle with a dot on its LEFT side appears in Step 1. In Step 2, the triangle is the same orientation but the dot is on the RIGHT side. What type of transformation is this?

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. The triangle orientation is unchanged — so it is not a rotation of the whole figure
2. The dot moved from LEFT to RIGHT — it "jumped sides"
3. Apply the asymmetric marker test: dot jumping sides = reflection
4. The axis of reflection is vertical (left-right mirror)
5. **Answer: Vertical reflection**

---

### Example 3: Combined Transformation

**Problem:** Step 1 shows a small white circle. Step 2 shows a medium gray circle. Step 3 shows a large black circle. What are the rules?

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Track size: small → medium → large = size increases each step
2. Track shading: white → gray → black = shading darkens each step
3. Track shape: circle → circle → circle = shape stays constant
4. Two simultaneous rules: size progression + shading progression
5. **Answer: Size increases AND shading darkens each step**

---

### Example 4: Grid-Based Reasoning

**Problem:** A 3×3 grid shows: Row 1 has circle, square, triangle. Row 2 has square, triangle, circle. Row 3 has triangle, circle, ?. What fills the missing cell?

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Check row rule: each row contains one circle, one square, one triangle
2. Row 3 has triangle and circle — missing shape is SQUARE
3. Verify with column rule: Column 3 has triangle, circle, ? — needs square ✅
4. Both row and column rules confirm the same answer
5. **Answer: Square**

---

### Example 5: Multi-Rule Elimination

**Problem:** A sequence shows shapes rotating 90° clockwise each step AND gaining one dot inside. Step 3 shows a right-pointing arrow with 3 dots. The answer choices are: (a) down-arrow with 4 dots, (b) down-arrow with 3 dots, (c) left-arrow with 4 dots, (d) up-arrow with 4 dots.

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Rotation rule: right + 90° clockwise = DOWN
2. Eliminate choices that violate rotation: (c) left ❌, (d) up ❌
3. Dot rule: 3 dots + 1 = 4 dots
4. Eliminate choices that violate dot count: (b) 3 dots ❌
5. Only (a) satisfies BOTH rules: down-arrow with 4 dots
6. **Answer: (a)**

## Key Takeaways

- Abstract reasoning tests your ability to identify visual rules without relying on language or prior knowledge
- The three core pattern types are rotation (turning), reflection (mirroring), and transformation (changing properties)
- Use the asymmetric marker test to distinguish rotation from reflection: if a distinctive feature stays on the same relative side, it is rotation; if it jumps sides, it is reflection
- Always check for multiple simultaneous rules — harder questions combine rotation with shading changes, element count changes, or size changes
- The SCAN method (Scan → Compare → Apply → Narrow) provides a systematic approach for every question
- For grid-based questions, identify the row rule and column rule independently, then find the answer that satisfies both
- Eliminate answer choices on the first mismatch: wrong shape, wrong shading, wrong orientation, or wrong count
- Time management is critical: spend no more than 30–45 seconds per question; if stuck, eliminate and guess
- Start with the simplest explanation — only add complexity if the simple rule fails to explain all transitions
- Common traps include rotation-reflection confusion, focusing on only one element, and choosing visually similar but logically wrong distractors

## Summary

Shape patterns form the core of abstract reasoning on the Philippine Civil Service Examination. Questions present sequences of visual figures that follow hidden rules involving rotation, reflection, transformation, or combinations of these. The key to solving them efficiently is a systematic approach: scan the sequence for changes, compare consecutive steps to identify the rule, apply the rule to predict the next figure, and narrow the choices by elimination. The most common errors are confusing rotation with reflection (use the asymmetric marker test), focusing on only one element when multiple rules are active, and overthinking simple patterns. Master the SCAN method, practice rapid elimination, and manage your time — these three habits will handle the vast majority of abstract reasoning questions on exam day.
