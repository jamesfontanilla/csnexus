# Figure Series

## Introduction

Abstract reasoning is the ability to identify patterns, logical rules, and relationships between visual elements without relying on language or prior knowledge. **Figure series** are sequences of visual figures that follow specific transformation rules — your task is to identify the rule governing the sequence and either predict the next figure or identify a missing figure within the sequence.

Figure series questions are a staple of the Philippine Civil Service Examination because they measure raw analytical intelligence independent of educational background, language proficiency, or cultural context. They evaluate your capacity to:

- Observe visual relationships between sequential figures
- Detect transformation rules (rotation, size change, shading, movement)
- Predict logical continuations of visual patterns
- Reconstruct missing steps in a progression
- Think flexibly under time pressure

**Why figure series matter in the workplace:**

Government work requires pattern recognition in data analysis, process improvement, policy interpretation, and operational planning. Figure series reasoning directly measures the mental flexibility needed for these tasks — the ability to observe a system, identify its rules, and predict outcomes.

**Why memorization does not work:**

Figure series questions are generated from infinite combinations of shapes, transformations, and rules. You cannot memorize answers. Instead, you must develop the skill of systematic observation — comparing adjacent figures, detecting what changes and what stays constant, and applying the discovered rule to predict the answer.

**Common mistakes examinees make:**

- Focusing on only one change while ignoring secondary transformations
- Confusing rotation direction (clockwise vs. counterclockwise)
- Assuming patterns are more complex than they actually are
- Not checking all answer choices before selecting one
- Rushing without establishing the rule first
- Overlooking alternating patterns (A-B-A-B vs. progressive A-B-C-D)
- Failing to track position changes of elements within frames

## Learning Objectives

After this lesson, learners should be able to:

- Define figure series and explain their role in the CSE
- Identify visual sequence relationships between consecutive figures
- Predict the next figure in a series logically
- Identify missing figures in incomplete sequences accurately
- Recognize repeated transformation patterns (rotation, reflection, growth, shading)
- Solve CSE-style figure series questions efficiently under time pressure
- Eliminate incorrect answer choices strategically
- Analyze complex multi-rule visual sequences
- Detect subtle visual changes quickly and accurately


## 4.1 What Is a Figure Series?

A figure series is a sequence of visual figures arranged in a specific order, where each figure relates to the previous one through a consistent transformation rule. The figures progress logically — once you identify the rule, you can predict what comes next or determine what belongs in a gap.

**Key concepts:**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Series | An ordered sequence following a rule |
| Transformation | The change applied from one figure to the next |
| Progression | The direction and magnitude of change |
| Cycle | A repeating pattern that loops back to the start |
| Continuation | The next logical figure in the sequence |

**How figure series questions work:**

1. You are shown 3–5 figures arranged in order
2. Each figure changes from the previous one according to a consistent rule
3. You must identify the rule by comparing adjacent figures
4. You select the answer that correctly applies the same rule

**The fundamental insight:** The first two or three figures establish the rule. Every subsequent figure — including the correct answer — must follow that same rule exactly. The rule never changes mid-sequence.

**What to observe in each figure:**

- **Shape type** — triangle, square, circle, arrow, star, polygon
- **Orientation** — which direction it points or faces
- **Position** — where it sits within the frame
- **Size** — small, medium, large (is it growing or shrinking?)
- **Shading** — empty, partially filled, fully filled
- **Count** — how many elements appear
- **Internal details** — dots, lines, smaller shapes inside
- **Movement** — does an element shift position between frames?

**Real-life analogy:** Think of a traffic light cycle: Green → Yellow → Red → Green. Once you observe the pattern, you predict what comes next without anyone telling you the rule. Figure series work identically — observe, detect the rule, predict.

## 4.2 Next Figure Prediction

Next figure prediction is the most common figure series question type. You are shown a sequence and must determine what logically follows.

**Step-by-step approach:**

1. **Compare figures 1 and 2** — What changed? What stayed the same?
2. **Compare figures 2 and 3** — Does the same change repeat?
3. **Confirm the rule** — Is the transformation consistent?
4. **Apply the rule** to predict the next figure
5. **Match your prediction** against the answer choices

**Types of progression:**

| Progression Type | Example | What Changes |
|-----------------|---------|--------------|
| Rotational | Arrow turns 90° each step | Orientation |
| Additive | One more dot each step | Count |
| Size growth | Shape gets larger each step | Dimensions |
| Shading | Empty → quarter → half → full | Fill level |
| Alternating | Circle-Square-Circle-Square | Shape type |
| Cyclic | A-B-C-A-B-C | Repeating sequence |
| Positional | Dot moves clockwise through corners | Location |

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Figure series exploit the fact that visual transformations are constrained — a shape can only change in a finite number of ways (rotate, resize, recolor, move, add/remove elements). By systematically checking each transformation dimension between consecutive frames, you reduce an open-ended "what comes next?" question to a closed set of hypotheses. Once you find the dimension that changes consistently, the prediction becomes deterministic rather than creative.

## 4.3 Missing Figure Identification

Missing figure identification presents a sequence with a gap — typically position 2 or 3 is removed. You must infer what belongs in the gap by analyzing the figures before and after it.

**Key difference from next-figure prediction:** You have information on BOTH sides of the gap. Use the surrounding figures to constrain your answer.

**Strategy:**

1. **Identify the overall rule** using the figures you CAN see
2. **Determine what the missing position requires** based on the rule
3. **Verify** that your answer creates a consistent sequence when inserted

**Common traps in missing-figure questions:**

- The gap is placed where the transformation is hardest to infer
- Distractors often show the correct shape but wrong orientation
- Some distractors show what would come AFTER the gap, not IN the gap

### Rotational Sequences

The most frequent pattern type. A figure rotates by a fixed angle each step.

| Rotation Amount | Steps to Complete Full Circle | Difficulty |
|----------------|------------------------------|-----------|
| 90° | 4 steps | Easy |
| 180° | 2 steps (alternating) | Easy |
| 45° | 8 steps | Medium |
| 60° | 6 steps | Medium |
| 72° | 5 steps | Medium |
| 120° | 3 steps | Medium |
| Non-standard | Varies | Hard |

**How to identify rotation:**
1. Pick a distinctive feature (pointed end, marked corner, dot)
2. Track where that feature moves from one figure to the next
3. Measure the angle of movement
4. Confirm it is consistent across all steps

### Count Progression

Elements are added (or removed) at a fixed rate:

| Pattern | Example |
|---------|---------|
| +1 each step | 1, 2, 3, 4 |
| +2 each step | 2, 4, 6, 8 |
| ×2 each step | 1, 2, 4, 8 |
| Alternating add | 1, 3, 2, 4 |

### Alternating Patterns

Two states alternate: A-B-A-B or three states cycle: A-B-C-A-B-C.

**Key insight:** If you see the same figure repeat every 2 steps, it is alternating. If every 3 steps, it is a 3-cycle.

### Size Progression

Figures grow or shrink by a consistent amount each step. Track the radius, width, or height to confirm linearity.

### Positional Movement

An element (dot, shape, symbol) moves through fixed positions within a frame — typically clockwise through corners or along edges.


## 4.5 Visual Pattern Analysis Techniques

Systematic analysis beats intuition. Use these techniques to decode any figure series:

### Technique 1: Sequential Comparison

Compare each pair of adjacent figures and list what changed:

| Comparison | What Changed | What Stayed |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Fig 1 → Fig 2 | Arrow rotated 90° CW | Color, size, shape |
| Fig 2 → Fig 3 | Arrow rotated 90° CW | Color, size, shape |
| **Rule:** | 90° CW rotation per step | |

### Technique 2: Element Tracking

Pick ONE element and track it across all figures:
- Where is the dot in figure 1? Top-left.
- Where in figure 2? Top-right.
- Where in figure 3? Bottom-right.
- Pattern: clockwise movement through corners.

### Technique 3: Elimination Analysis

When you cannot immediately identify the rule:
1. Look at the answer choices
2. Eliminate any choice that contradicts what you DO know
3. The remaining choice is likely correct

**Example:** If figures show increasing complexity, eliminate any choice that is simpler than figure 3.

### Technique 4: Counting Changes

Count the number of distinct changes between steps:
- **1 change** = Easy question (single rule)
- **2 changes** = Medium question (dual rule)
- **3+ changes** = Hard question (multi-rule)

For multi-rule questions, identify each rule independently, then combine them for the answer.

### Polygon Rotation

When a polygon rotates, track a marked vertex (often shown as a red dot). The polygon's shape does not change — only its orientation shifts.

**Common polygon rotations:**
- Square: 90° rotation looks identical (symmetry trap!) — always look for a marker
- Triangle: 120° rotation returns to similar appearance
- Pentagon: 72° rotation (360° ÷ 5) creates apparent repetition

Each step adds or removes an element. Track the count carefully — the increment may not always be +1.

Shading fills in stages. Common patterns:

**Row rule:** Each row uses the same shape (row 1 = circles, row 2 = squares, row 3 = triangles).

**Column rule:** Each column uses the same shading (col 1 = empty, col 2 = half, col 3 = full).

**Answer:** The missing cell (row 3, col 3) must be a **fully-filled triangle**.

### Grid Analysis Strategy

1. **Analyze rows first** — What property is shared within each row?
2. **Analyze columns** — What property is shared within each column?
3. **Combine both rules** — The answer must satisfy BOTH the row rule AND the column rule
4. **Verify** — Check that your answer does not violate either rule

### Common Grid Rules

| Row Rule | Column Rule | Difficulty |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Same shape | Same shading | Medium |
| Same size | Same orientation | Hard |
| Same count | Same color | Hard |
| Progressive size | Progressive shading | Hard |

## 4.10 Step-by-Step Figure Series Strategies

### The SCORE Method

| Step | Action | Purpose |
|------|--------|---------|
| **S**can | Look at all figures quickly | Get overall impression |
| **C**ompare | Compare adjacent pairs | Identify changes |
| **O**bserve | Note what stays constant | Narrow the rule |
| **R**ule | State the rule explicitly | Confirm understanding |
| **E**valuate | Check all answer choices | Select the match |

### Time Management

For CSE figure series questions, aim for **30–45 seconds per question**:
- 10 seconds: Scan and compare
- 10 seconds: Identify the rule
- 10 seconds: Apply and select
- 5 seconds: Verify (if time permits)

### Shortcut Techniques

1. **Start with the answer choices** — If three choices point right and one points left, the odd one is likely wrong (or the answer, depending on the pattern)
2. **Count the changes** — If only ONE thing changes between steps, the question is testing that single transformation
3. **Look for markers** — Red dots, filled corners, or asymmetric features are placed specifically to help you track rotation
4. **Check for symmetry traps** — A square rotated 90° looks identical without a marker. Always look for distinguishing features
5. **Use the "odd step" test** — If the pattern seems to break at one step, you may have the wrong rule. Try a different interpretation

### When You Are Stuck

1. Try the OPPOSITE direction (if you assumed CW, try CCW)
2. Look for TWO rules instead of one
3. Check if it is alternating rather than progressive
4. Consider that the "change" might be in a property you have not examined yet (position, not rotation)


## 4.11 Common Errors in Figure Series

### Error 1: Focusing on Only One Change

**Problem:** A question has TWO simultaneous rules (rotation + shading), but you only notice the rotation.

**Fix:** After identifying one rule, ask: "Is there ANYTHING else that changes?" Check size, shading, count, and position.

### Error 2: Misreading Rotation Direction

**Problem:** You assume clockwise when the actual rotation is counterclockwise.

**Fix:** Use a reference point. If the arrow moves from up to LEFT, it is counterclockwise. If up to RIGHT, it is clockwise.

### Error 3: Overlooking Alternating Patterns

**Problem:** You assume progressive change (A → B → C → D) when the actual pattern alternates (A → B → A → B).

**Fix:** Check if figure 3 matches figure 1. If yes, it is alternating, not progressive.

### Error 4: Choosing Visually Similar but Logically Incorrect Figures

**Problem:** A distractor looks "close enough" but violates one of the rules.

**Fix:** Always verify your answer against ALL identified rules. A correct answer must satisfy every rule simultaneously.

### Error 5: Ignoring Position Changes

**Problem:** You focus on shape/color changes but miss that an element is also moving position within the frame.

**Fix:** Track the (x, y) position of each element across figures. Movement is a common secondary rule.

### Error 6: Assuming Complexity

**Problem:** You overthink a simple pattern, looking for hidden rules that do not exist.

**Fix:** Start with the simplest possible explanation. If "it rotates 90° each step" explains everything, that IS the answer. Do not add complexity.

## 4.12 Estimation and Elimination Techniques

### Rapid Elimination Framework

When time is short, eliminate impossible answers first:

1. **Direction check** — If the series shows consistent clockwise rotation, eliminate any choice pointing in a direction that breaks the pattern
2. **Size check** — If figures are growing, eliminate any choice smaller than the last figure
3. **Shading check** — If shading is increasing, eliminate any choice with less shading than the last figure
4. **Count check** — If elements are being added, eliminate any choice with fewer elements

### The "Two Survivors" Technique

After elimination, you often have 2 remaining choices. To decide between them:
- Check which one satisfies ALL rules (not just the primary one)
- The distractor typically gets the primary rule right but violates a secondary rule

### Visual Consistency Check

A correct answer should "feel" like it belongs in the sequence. If inserting your choice creates a visual disruption, reconsider. The correct answer maintains the visual rhythm of the series.

## 4.13 Advanced Figure Series Analysis

### Multi-Layer Transformations

Advanced questions combine 3+ simultaneous rules:

**Example:** Arrow rotates 90° CW + dot count increases by 1 + arrow size grows

To solve:
1. Identify each rule independently
2. Determine what each rule predicts for the next step
3. Find the answer choice that satisfies ALL predictions simultaneously

### Nested Pattern Logic

Some figures contain an outer shape and an inner shape, each following independent rules:
- Outer shape: rotates 30° each step
- Inner shape: cycles through circle → square → triangle

You must track both layers independently and combine them for the answer.

### Grid-Based Progression

3×3 matrices where:
- Rows follow one rule (e.g., same shape)
- Columns follow another rule (e.g., same shading)
- The missing cell must satisfy BOTH rules

### Alternating Rotation Direction

The most deceptive advanced pattern:
- Step 1: Rotate +90° (clockwise)
- Step 2: Rotate -90° (counterclockwise)
- Step 3: Rotate +90° (clockwise)
- Step 4: Rotate -90° (counterclockwise)

This creates a "wobbling" effect that looks random until you identify the alternation.

### Non-Standard Rotation Angles

Instead of clean 90° or 45° rotations, advanced questions may use:
- 60° (hexagonal symmetry)
- 72° (pentagonal symmetry)
- 120° (triangular symmetry)
- Combined angles (90° + skip = 180° apparent jump)

## Quick Recap

| Concept | Key Takeaway |
|---------|-------------|
| Next figure prediction | Identify the rule from shown figures, apply it forward |
| Missing figure identification | Use figures on BOTH sides of the gap to constrain the answer |
| Rotation patterns | Track a marker point; determine angle and direction |
| Transformation patterns | Check size, shading, count, and position changes |
| Multi-rule sequences | Identify each rule independently, then combine |
| Grid reasoning | Rows and columns follow separate rules; answer satisfies both |
| Elimination | Remove impossible choices first, then verify the survivor |
| Common traps | Symmetry illusions, secondary rules, alternating vs. progressive |

## Memory Aids

**ROTATION direction:** "ClocKwise = Keep going Right" (up → right → down → left)

**Counting rules:** "If it grew before, it grows again" — consistent increment

**Alternating detection:** "Does figure 3 look like figure 1? Then it alternates."

**Multi-rule check:** "After finding one rule, ask: what ELSE changed?"

**Grid solving:** "Rows share shape, columns share style" (most common grid pattern)

**Elimination shortcut:** "If 3 choices share a property the series doesn't have, eliminate all 3"

**Time pressure:** "Rule first, answer second. Never guess without a rule."
