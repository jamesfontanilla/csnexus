# Syllogisms

## Explanations

### Introduction

**Syllogisms** are the backbone of deductive reasoning — structured logical arguments where a conclusion is drawn from two or more premises. In the Philippine Civil Service Examination (CSE), syllogism questions test your ability to evaluate whether a conclusion necessarily follows from given statements, identify relationships between categories of things, and distinguish valid deductions from logical traps.

This subtopic covers the following critical areas:
- **Categorical Statements** — understanding universal and particular claims about groups
- **Deductive Reasoning** — drawing guaranteed conclusions from premises
- **Valid and Invalid Conclusions** — distinguishing what must be true from what merely could be true
- **Universal and Particular Statements** — mastering "All," "No," "Some," and "Some...not" logic
- **Logical Relationships Between Groups** — analyzing subset, superset, and exclusion relationships
- **Common Syllogism Fallacies** — recognizing reasoning errors that appear convincing but fail logically
- **Multi-Premise Chains** — linking multiple statements to derive complex conclusions

Mastering syllogisms is essential for civil servants. Government employees interpret regulations that use categorical language daily — "All applicants who meet criteria X are eligible for benefit Y," "No employee below Grade 5 may authorize expenditures above ₱50,000." The CSE tests syllogistic reasoning because it directly measures the structured thinking required for policy interpretation, eligibility determination, and administrative decision-making.

### Why Syllogisms Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination tests syllogisms because:

- Government employees must interpret categorical rules in laws, memoranda, and administrative orders — determining who falls within or outside a category
- Public servants evaluate eligibility conditions that require precise group-membership reasoning — a single misclassification can deny services to qualified citizens
- Administrative decision-making requires distinguishing between what is necessarily true versus what is merely assumed
- Policy implementation demands understanding of universal versus particular scope — knowing whether a rule applies to all members of a group or only some
- Legal and regulatory interpretation requires recognizing when a conclusion follows from stated premises and when it does not
- Both Professional and Sub-Professional levels include syllogism items, making this a high-value preparation area
- The ability to detect invalid reasoning protects against manipulation in proposals, negotiations, and administrative communications
- Syllogistic thinking supports classification tasks: categorizing documents, personnel, budget items, and cases according to established criteria

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Reversing the relationship** — concluding "All B are A" from "All A are B" (if all cats are animals, it does NOT follow that all animals are cats)
2. **Affirming the consequent** — concluding that someone belongs to a group just because they share a property with that group (all doctors are educated, but not all educated people are doctors)
3. **Confusing "some" with "all"** — treating a particular statement as if it were universal
4. **Ignoring negative statements** — misreading "No A are B" as "Some A are B" or vice versa
5. **Assuming the converse of a universal** — believing that "All A are B" automatically means "All B are A"
6. **Selecting partially correct answers** — choosing conclusions that sound reasonable but are not logically guaranteed by the premises alone
7. **Adding outside knowledge** — importing real-world assumptions instead of reasoning strictly from the given premises
8. **Confusing possibility with certainty** — selecting "could be true" answers when the question asks for what "must be true"

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define syllogisms and explain their role in deductive reasoning
- Identify and classify the four types of categorical statements (A, E, I, O)
- Analyze relationships between groups using subset, superset, and exclusion logic
- Distinguish valid conclusions from invalid ones given a set of premises
- Interpret universal affirmative, universal negative, particular affirmative, and particular negative statements correctly
- Recognize common syllogism fallacies including undistributed middle and illicit conversion
- Apply transitive reasoning to derive conclusions from multi-premise chains
- Solve CSE-style syllogism questions efficiently under time pressure
- Eliminate incorrect answer choices using logical consistency testing
- Visualize category relationships using set diagrams for verification

### 4.1 What Are Syllogisms?
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The principle behind this operation follows from the fundamental properties of arithmetic. Understanding the "why" — not just the "how" — lets you recognize when to apply this method in unfamiliar problem contexts on the CSE.


A **syllogism** is a form of deductive reasoning consisting of two or more **premises** (statements assumed to be true) and a **conclusion** that follows logically from those premises. The conclusion is valid if and only if it is impossible for the premises to be true while the conclusion is false.

#### Structure of a Syllogism

Every syllogism has three components:

| Component | Role | Example |
|-----------|------|---------|
| Major Premise | A general statement about a category | "All government employees pay taxes." |
| Minor Premise | A specific statement connecting to the major premise | "Maria is a government employee." |
| Conclusion | What necessarily follows from both premises | "Therefore, Maria pays taxes." |

#### How Syllogisms Work

The power of a syllogism lies in **category membership**. If you establish that:
1. All members of Group A have Property X (major premise)
2. Individual Y is a member of Group A (minor premise)

Then you can guarantee that Individual Y has Property X (conclusion).

**Workplace Example:**
- All Division Chiefs must submit quarterly reports.
- Mr. Reyes is a Division Chief.
- Therefore, Mr. Reyes must submit quarterly reports.

**Key Principle:** The conclusion must follow *necessarily* from the premises — not probably, not usually, but with absolute certainty given that the premises are true. If there is any scenario where the premises could be true but the conclusion false, the syllogism is invalid.

#### What Syllogisms Are NOT

- They are NOT about whether premises are factually true in the real world
- They are NOT about what is likely or probable
- They are NOT about common sense or general knowledge
- They ARE about logical structure: does the conclusion follow from the premises as given?

**CSE Trap:** A question might present premises you know are factually false ("All fish can fly"). You must still evaluate whether the conclusion follows logically from those premises, ignoring real-world knowledge.


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "The formula always works the same way regardless of the problem context."

> **Why it fails:** CSE problems often present variations where the standard formula must be adapted. Blindly applying a memorized formula without checking the context leads to systematic errors.

> **Correct model:** Always read the problem to identify what type of relationship exists (direct, inverse, part-whole, etc.), then apply the appropriate formula. Verify your answer makes sense in the problem's context before selecting it.

### 4.2 Types of Categorical Statements

Categorical statements make claims about the relationship between two categories (groups). There are exactly four types, traditionally labeled A, E, I, and O:

| Type | Label | Form | Example | Meaning |
|------|-------|------|---------|---------|
| Universal Affirmative | A | All A are B | "All teachers are professionals." | Every member of A is also a member of B |
| Universal Negative | E | No A are B | "No reptiles are mammals." | No member of A is a member of B |
| Particular Affirmative | I | Some A are B | "Some employees work remotely." | At least one member of A is also a member of B |
| Particular Negative | O | Some A are not B | "Some students are not athletes." | At least one member of A is not a member of B |

#### Understanding Scope

The critical difference between universal and particular statements is **scope**:

- **Universal** (All/No) — makes a claim about *every single member* of the subject category, with zero exceptions
- **Particular** (Some/Some...not) — makes a claim about *at least one* member, possibly more, but not necessarily all

**Important:** In logic, "some" means "at least one." It does NOT mean "a few" or "not many." If someone says "Some government employees are lawyers," this is true even if only one government employee is a lawyer — and it's also true if ALL of them are lawyers.

#### Recognizing Categorical Statements in Natural Language

CSE questions don't always use the exact words "All," "No," or "Some." Here are common phrasings:

| Natural Language | Logical Form | Type |
|-----------------|--------------|------|
| "All managers attend meetings." | All A are B | A |
| "Every citizen has rights." | All A are B | A |
| "Each applicant must submit Form 6." | All A are B | A |
| "No intern may sign contracts." | No A are B | E |
| "None of the trainees are permanent." | No A are B | E |
| "Birds never have gills." | No A are B | E |
| "Some reports contain errors." | Some A are B | I |
| "A few employees were promoted." | Some A are B | I |
| "There are teachers who are also lawyers." | Some A are B | I |
| "Some policies are not effective." | Some A are not B | O |
| "Not all applicants are qualified." | Some A are not B | O |
| "Certain employees did not pass." | Some A are not B | O |

**CSE Tip:** When you see "Not all A are B," translate it to "Some A are not B" (Type O). It does NOT mean "No A are B."


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "If my computed answer is close to one of the choices, it must be right."

> **Why it fails:** The CSE deliberately includes distractors that result from common errors — using the wrong operation, misidentifying the proportion type, or reversing the ratio. A "close" answer could be the result of a systematic mistake that the test writers anticipated.

> **Correct model:** Verify your setup before computing. Check that you've identified the correct proportion type, set up the equation properly, and solved accurately. A wrong setup with correct arithmetic still produces a wrong answer — and the CSE will include that wrong answer among the choices.

### 4.3 Universal Affirmative Statements (All A are B)
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** When you follow this procedure, you're exploiting a mathematical invariant — something that stays constant regardless of how you manipulate the numbers. Identifying that invariant is the key to solving problems efficiently rather than memorizing steps.


The universal affirmative statement "All A are B" means that **every single member** of category A is also a member of category B. In set terms, A is a **subset** of B.

#### What "All A are B" Guarantees

- Every A is a B (no exceptions)
- If something is an A, it must be a B
- Being an A is *sufficient* for being a B

#### What "All A are B" Does NOT Guarantee

- It does NOT mean all B are A (the reverse)
- It does NOT mean A and B are the same group
- It does NOT mean there are no B's outside of A

#### Visual Representation

Think of it as a smaller circle (A) completely inside a larger circle (B):

```
┌─────────────────────────┐
│         B               │
│    ┌──────────┐         │
│    │    A     │         │
│    │          │         │
│    └──────────┘         │
│                         │
└─────────────────────────┘
```

All of A is inside B, but B extends beyond A.

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- "All roses are flowers." → Every rose is a flower, but not every flower is a rose.
- "All senators are government officials." → Every senator is a government official, but not every government official is a senator.

**Medium:**
- "All Civil Service passers are eligible for permanent positions." → Every passer is eligible, but there may be other paths to eligibility.
- "All memoranda from the Secretary are binding." → Every such memorandum is binding, but other documents may also be binding.

**Hard:**
- "All items exceeding the procurement threshold require public bidding." → Every item above the threshold requires bidding. An item below the threshold may or may not require bidding — the statement says nothing about items outside category A.
- "All employees with at least 15 years of service are eligible for early retirement." → 15+ years guarantees eligibility. But someone with 14 years? The statement is silent — they might be eligible through another provision.

#### The Conversion Trap

The most common error with universal affirmatives is **illicit conversion** — reversing the statement:

| Original (Valid) | Converse (INVALID) |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| All cats are animals. | All animals are cats. ❌ |
| All engineers are problem-solvers. | All problem-solvers are engineers. ❌ |
| All Division Chiefs submit reports. | All who submit reports are Division Chiefs. ❌ |

**Rule:** "All A are B" does NOT imply "All B are A." The only valid conversion is the contrapositive: "If not B, then not A."

### 4.4 Universal Negative Statements (No A are B)

The universal negative statement "No A are B" means that **not a single member** of category A is a member of category B. The two groups are completely separate — they have zero overlap.

#### What "No A are B" Guarantees

- No member of A is a member of B
- No member of B is a member of A (this one IS reversible!)
- If something is an A, it cannot be a B
- If something is a B, it cannot be an A

#### Key Difference from Universal Affirmative

Unlike "All A are B," the universal negative IS validly convertible:
- "No A are B" ↔ "No B are A" (both say the same thing)

This is because mutual exclusion works in both directions. If cats and reptiles have no overlap, then reptiles and cats have no overlap.

#### Visual Representation

Two completely separate circles with no overlap:

```
┌──────────┐     ┌──────────┐
│    A     │     │    B     │
│          │     │          │
└──────────┘     └──────────┘
```

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- "No birds are mammals." → Birds and mammals are completely separate groups.
- "No triangles are circles." → A shape cannot be both a triangle and a circle.

**Medium:**
- "No casual employee may approve leave applications." → The categories "casual employees" and "those who may approve leave" have zero overlap.
- "No document classified as confidential may be released to the public." → Confidential documents and publicly releasable documents are mutually exclusive.

**Hard:**
- "No applicant who failed the background check is eligible for security clearance." → Failed-background-check applicants and security-clearance-eligible persons are completely separate groups. Note: this does NOT mean all who passed the background check ARE eligible — other requirements may exist.
- "No expenditure exceeding ₱1,000,000 may be approved without COA pre-audit." → The categories "expenditures over ₱1M" and "expenditures approvable without COA pre-audit" have zero overlap.

#### Common Errors with Universal Negatives

| Error | Example | Why It's Wrong |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Weakening to particular | "No A are B" → "Some A are not B" | Technically true but loses information — the universal is stronger |
| Negating incorrectly | "No A are B" → "All A are not-B" | This is actually equivalent (valid), but students confuse it with "Not all A are B" |
| Assuming exhaustive categories | "No A are B" → "Everything is either A or B" | Wrong — things can be neither A nor B |

### 4.5 Particular Statements (Some A are B / Some A are not B)
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** This shortcut works because it's a special case of the more general rule. By understanding the underlying principle, you can verify your answer logically even if you forget the exact formula under exam pressure.


Particular statements make claims about **at least one** member of a category, without committing to all members.

#### Particular Affirmative: "Some A are B" (Type I)

This means: **At least one member of A is also a member of B.** The two groups overlap, but neither necessarily contains the other.

What it guarantees:
- There exists at least one thing that is both A and B
- The intersection of A and B is not empty

What it does NOT guarantee:
- It does NOT tell you how many A's are B's (could be one, could be all)
- It does NOT mean most A's are B's
- It does NOT mean some A's are NOT B's (that's a separate claim)

**Important logical point:** "Some A are B" is compatible with "All A are B." If all teachers are professionals, then it's also true that some teachers are professionals. "Some" means "at least one" — it doesn't exclude "all."

#### Particular Negative: "Some A are not B" (Type O)

This means: **At least one member of A is NOT a member of B.** Not every A belongs to B.

What it guarantees:
- There exists at least one A that is not a B
- A is NOT a complete subset of B

What it does NOT guarantee:
- It does NOT mean no A's are B's (some might still be)
- It does NOT tell you how many A's are outside B
- It does NOT mean most A's are not B's

#### Visual Representations

**Some A are B** (partial overlap):
```
┌──────────┐
│    A   ┌─┼──────────┐
│        │ │    B      │
│        └─┼──────────┘
└──────────┘
```

**Some A are not B** (A extends beyond B):
```
┌──────────────┐
│    A       ┌─┼────────┐
│  (outside) │ │   B    │
│            └─┼────────┘
└──────────────┘
```

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- "Some students are musicians." → At least one student plays music. Others might not.
- "Some vehicles are electric." → At least one vehicle is electric. Many are not.

**Medium:**
- "Some government employees hold multiple positions." → At least one does, but not necessarily all or even most.
- "Some approved projects were not funded." → At least one approved project didn't receive funding.

**Hard:**
- "Some applicants who passed the written exam did not pass the interview." → Passing the written exam doesn't guarantee passing the interview for everyone, but some did fail the interview despite passing the written.
- "Some policies implemented during the previous administration are not being continued." → At least one old policy was discontinued, but others may still be active.

#### The "Some Does Not Mean Not All" Trap

Many examinees assume that "Some A are B" implies "Some A are not B." This is a common logical error:

- "Some employees are managers." Does this mean some employees are NOT managers? **In everyday language, yes. In formal logic, not necessarily.** The statement is compatible with ALL employees being managers.

**CSE Strategy:** In exam questions, treat "some" strictly as "at least one" without assuming anything about the rest of the group.

### 4.6 Valid and Invalid Conclusions

A conclusion is **valid** if it is impossible for the premises to be true while the conclusion is false. A conclusion is **invalid** if there exists even one scenario where the premises are true but the conclusion is false.

#### Testing Validity: The Counterexample Method

To test whether a conclusion is valid:
1. Assume the premises are true
2. Try to imagine a scenario where the conclusion is false
3. If you can find such a scenario → the conclusion is **invalid**
4. If no such scenario is possible → the conclusion is **valid**

#### Valid Syllogism Patterns

**Pattern 1: Barbara (AAA-1) — The Classic Chain**
- All A are B.
- All B are C.
- ∴ All A are C. ✅

Example:
- All managers are employees.
- All employees receive salaries.
- Therefore, all managers receive salaries. ✅

**Pattern 2: Darii (AII-1) — Universal + Particular**
- All A are B.
- Some C are A.
- ∴ Some C are B. ✅

Example:
- All lawyers passed the bar exam.
- Some government employees are lawyers.
- Therefore, some government employees passed the bar exam. ✅

**Pattern 3: Celarent (EAE-1) — Negative Chain**
- No A are B.
- All C are A.
- ∴ No C are B. ✅

Example:
- No casual employee may authorize purchases.
- All interns are casual employees.
- Therefore, no intern may authorize purchases. ✅

**Pattern 4: Ferio (EIO-1) — Negative + Particular**
- No A are B.
- Some C are A.
- ∴ Some C are not B. ✅

Example:
- No contractual worker receives retirement benefits.
- Some office staff are contractual workers.
- Therefore, some office staff do not receive retirement benefits. ✅

#### Invalid Syllogism Patterns (Fallacies)

**Fallacy 1: Undistributed Middle**
- All A are B.
- All C are B.
- ∴ All A are C. ❌ (or any connection between A and C)

Example:
- All dogs are animals.
- All cats are animals.
- Therefore, all dogs are cats. ❌

Why invalid: A and C both belong to B, but they could be completely different subsets of B.

**Fallacy 2: Illicit Conversion (Affirming the Consequent)**
- All A are B.
- X is a B.
- ∴ X is an A. ❌

Example:
- All teachers are professionals.
- Dr. Santos is a professional.
- Therefore, Dr. Santos is a teacher. ❌

Why invalid: Being a professional doesn't require being a teacher. Doctors, engineers, and lawyers are also professionals.

**Fallacy 3: Drawing Universal from Particular**
- Some A are B.
- ∴ All A are B. ❌

Example:
- Some employees work overtime.
- Therefore, all employees work overtime. ❌

Why invalid: "Some" only guarantees at least one — it says nothing about the rest.

### 4.7 Syllogism Diagrams and Visualization

Diagrams are the most reliable method for verifying syllogism validity. By drawing the relationships described in the premises, you can visually confirm whether a conclusion must be true.

#### The Circle Method (Euler/Venn Diagrams)

Represent each category as a circle. The spatial relationship between circles shows the logical relationship:

| Relationship | Diagram Description |
|-------------|-------------------|
| All A are B | Circle A is completely inside circle B |
| No A are B | Circles A and B do not touch at all |
| Some A are B | Circles A and B partially overlap |
| Some A are not B | Part of circle A is outside circle B |

#### Step-by-Step Diagram Method

**Step 1:** Draw the major premise relationship
**Step 2:** Add the minor premise relationship, consistent with Step 1
**Step 3:** Check if the conclusion is forced by the diagram
**Step 4:** If you can draw the diagram in a way that makes the premises true but the conclusion false, the syllogism is invalid

#### Worked Diagram Example

**Premises:**
- All managers are employees. (A inside B)
- Some employees are union members. (B overlaps C)

**Question:** Does it follow that some managers are union members?

**Diagram Analysis:**
```
┌─────────────────────────────┐
│         Employees (B)       │
│   ┌─────────┐    ┌────────┐│
│   │Managers │    │ Union  ││
│   │  (A)    │    │Members ││
│   │         │    │  (C)   ││
│   └─────────┘    └────────┘│
└─────────────────────────────┘
```

In this valid diagram, managers and union members don't overlap even though both are inside employees. The conclusion "Some managers are union members" is NOT guaranteed — it's possible but not necessary. **The conclusion is INVALID.**

#### When Diagrams Confirm Validity

**Premises:**
- All pharmacists are licensed professionals.
- Maria is a pharmacist.

**Diagram:**
```
┌─────────────────────────────┐
│  Licensed Professionals     │
│   ┌─────────────────┐      │
│   │  Pharmacists    │      │
│   │    • Maria      │      │
│   └─────────────────┘      │
└─────────────────────────────┘
```

No matter how you draw this, Maria must be inside "Licensed Professionals." The conclusion "Maria is a licensed professional" is **VALID** — there's no alternative arrangement.

#### CSE Strategy: The Two-Diagram Test

If you're unsure whether a conclusion is valid:
1. Draw one diagram where the conclusion IS true (consistent with premises)
2. Try to draw another diagram where the conclusion is FALSE (still consistent with premises)
3. If you succeed in step 2 → conclusion is INVALID
4. If step 2 is impossible → conclusion is VALID

### 4.8 Common Syllogism Fallacies

Fallacies are reasoning errors that produce invalid conclusions despite appearing logical. CSE questions frequently use these as distractors.

#### Fallacy 1: The Undistributed Middle

**Structure:** All A are C. All B are C. Therefore, All A are B (or some connection between A and B).

**Why it fails:** Two groups can both be subsets of a larger group without overlapping each other.

**Example:**
- All doctors are educated.
- All lawyers are educated.
- Therefore, all doctors are lawyers. ❌

**Real-world analogy:** All cars have wheels. All bicycles have wheels. Therefore, all cars are bicycles. Obviously absurd — sharing a property doesn't create identity.

#### Fallacy 2: Illicit Conversion of Universal Affirmative

**Structure:** All A are B. Therefore, All B are A.

**Why it fails:** A being inside B doesn't mean B is inside A. B is typically larger.

**Example:**
- All nurses are healthcare workers.
- Therefore, all healthcare workers are nurses. ❌

**The valid alternative:** The contrapositive IS valid: "If not a healthcare worker, then not a nurse."

#### Fallacy 3: Affirming the Consequent (with categories)

**Structure:** All A are B. X is B. Therefore, X is A.

**Why it fails:** X could be a B through a different path — B contains more than just A.

**Example:**
- All accountants are professionals.
- Anna is a professional.
- Therefore, Anna is an accountant. ❌

Anna could be a doctor, lawyer, engineer, or any other professional.

#### Fallacy 4: Denying the Antecedent (with categories)

**Structure:** All A are B. X is not A. Therefore, X is not B.

**Why it fails:** X could still be a B through membership in another subset of B.

**Example:**
- All senators are government officials.
- Pedro is not a senator.
- Therefore, Pedro is not a government official. ❌

Pedro could be a congressman, governor, mayor, or any other government official.

#### Fallacy 5: Existential Fallacy

**Structure:** All A are B. Therefore, some A are B (assuming A has members).

**Why it's tricky:** In modern logic, "All A are B" doesn't guarantee that any A's actually exist. "All unicorns are magical" is logically true even if unicorns don't exist.

**CSE Note:** Most CSE questions assume categories have members. But be cautious with abstract or hypothetical categories.

#### Fallacy 6: Drawing Affirmative from Two Negatives

**Structure:** No A are B. No B are C. Therefore, No A are C.

**Why it fails:** A and C might still overlap — they're just both excluded from B.

**Example:**
- No fish are mammals.
- No mammals are insects.
- Therefore, no fish are insects. ❌ (happens to be true in reality, but is NOT logically guaranteed by the premises)

#### Quick Fallacy Detection Checklist

| Red Flag | Likely Fallacy |
|----------|---------------|
| Conclusion reverses the direction of a premise | Illicit conversion |
| Two premises share a predicate but conclusion links their subjects | Undistributed middle |
| Conclusion is universal but a premise is particular | Illicit generalization |
| Both premises are negative | No valid conclusion possible (in standard syllogisms) |
| Conclusion is affirmative but a premise is negative | Invalid — negative premise requires negative conclusion |

### 4.9 Multi-Premise Syllogisms

Many CSE questions involve more than two premises, requiring you to chain logical relationships across multiple statements.

#### Transitive Chains (Sorites)

A **sorites** is an extended syllogism with multiple premises forming a chain:

**Example:**
- All directors are managers.
- All managers are employees.
- All employees are taxpayers.
- Therefore, all directors are taxpayers. ✅

**How it works:** Director → Manager → Employee → Taxpayer. Each link in the chain is a subset relationship, so the first category is a subset of the last.

#### Mixed Chains (Universal + Particular)

When a chain mixes universal and particular statements, the conclusion can only be as strong as the weakest link:

**Example:**
- All pharmacists are licensed.
- Some licensed professionals work in hospitals.
- Therefore, some pharmacists work in hospitals. ❌

**Why invalid:** "Some licensed professionals work in hospitals" doesn't guarantee that the pharmacist subset is among those who do. The "some" might refer to doctors or nurses, not pharmacists.

**Valid mixed chain:**
- All pharmacists are licensed.
- Some pharmacists work in hospitals.
- Therefore, some licensed professionals work in hospitals. ✅

**Why valid:** If some pharmacists work in hospitals, and all pharmacists are licensed, then those hospital-working pharmacists are licensed professionals who work in hospitals.

#### Combining Affirmative and Negative Premises

**Valid combination:**
- All managers are employees.
- No employee may accept gifts from contractors.
- Therefore, no manager may accept gifts from contractors. ✅

**Invalid combination:**
- No manager is a janitor.
- No janitor is a director.
- Therefore, no manager is a director. ❌

**Rule:** You cannot derive a valid conclusion from two negative premises in standard syllogistic logic.

#### Strategy for Multi-Premise Questions

1. **Identify the chain:** Which categories connect to which?
2. **Map the direction:** Are relationships subset (All A are B) or exclusion (No A are B)?
3. **Find the weakest link:** Any particular premise limits the conclusion to particular
4. **Check for gaps:** If two categories don't connect through a chain, no conclusion links them
5. **Apply the negative rule:** One negative premise → conclusion must be negative. Two negative premises → no valid standard conclusion.

### 4.10 Practical Applications of Syllogistic Thinking

Syllogistic reasoning isn't just an exam skill — it's a daily tool for civil servants who must interpret and apply categorical rules.

#### Workplace Decision-Making

**Eligibility Determination:**
- Policy: "All applicants with a master's degree are qualified for Salary Grade 18."
- Fact: "Ms. Cruz has a master's degree."
- Decision: Ms. Cruz is qualified for Salary Grade 18.

**Access Control:**
- Policy: "No employee below Security Level 3 may access classified documents."
- Fact: "Mr. Tan is Security Level 2."
- Decision: Mr. Tan may not access classified documents.

#### Government Policy Interpretation

**Budget Rules:**
- "All expenditures above ₱500,000 require the approval of the Regional Director."
- "The office renovation costs ₱750,000."
- Conclusion: The office renovation requires the Regional Director's approval.

**Personnel Rules:**
- "No employee on probation may be assigned to field operations."
- "All new hires are on probation for six months."
- "Officer Reyes was hired last month."
- Conclusion: Officer Reyes may not be assigned to field operations.

#### Legal Reasoning

**Regulatory Compliance:**
- "All financial institutions must submit quarterly reports to BSP."
- "Rural banks are financial institutions."
- Conclusion: Rural banks must submit quarterly reports to BSP.

**Disciplinary Proceedings:**
- "No employee found guilty of grave misconduct may be promoted within two years."
- "Mr. Aquino was found guilty of grave misconduct last year."
- Conclusion: Mr. Aquino may not be promoted within two years.

#### Why This Matters for the CSE

The CSE tests syllogisms because government work constantly requires:
- Classifying people, documents, and transactions into categories
- Applying universal rules to specific cases
- Determining what is permitted, prohibited, or required based on categorical membership
- Identifying when a rule does NOT apply (recognizing the limits of a universal statement)

### 4.11 Step-by-Step Syllogism Solving Strategies

#### The 5-Step Method

**Step 1: Identify the premises and conclusion**
- Separate what is given (premises) from what is asked (conclusion to evaluate)
- Mark each premise as A, E, I, or O type

**Step 2: Identify the categories**
- Find the three terms: major term (predicate of conclusion), minor term (subject of conclusion), middle term (appears in both premises but not the conclusion)

**Step 3: Map the relationships**
- Draw a quick diagram or mentally trace the subset/exclusion relationships
- Ask: Is A inside B? Is B inside C? Are they separate?

**Step 4: Test the conclusion**
- Does the conclusion necessarily follow?
- Can you imagine a scenario where premises are true but conclusion is false?

**Step 5: Eliminate wrong answers**
- Cross out answers that commit known fallacies
- Cross out answers that go beyond what the premises guarantee

#### Speed Techniques for the CSE

**Technique 1: The Chain Test**
If premises form a chain (A→B→C), the valid conclusion connects the endpoints (A→C for universal affirmatives).

**Technique 2: The Negative Rule**
- One negative premise → conclusion must be negative
- Two negative premises → no valid conclusion (eliminate all options or select "none of the above")

**Technique 3: The Particular Rule**
- One particular premise → conclusion must be particular (Some..., not All...)
- Two particular premises → usually no valid conclusion

**Technique 4: The Reversal Check**
If an answer choice reverses a premise's direction (turns "All A are B" into "All B are A"), it's almost certainly wrong.

**Technique 5: The "Must Be True" Filter**
- Ask: "Is this ALWAYS true given the premises, or only SOMETIMES true?"
- If only sometimes → invalid conclusion
- Only select conclusions that are guaranteed in every possible scenario

### 4.12 Common Errors in Syllogisms

Understanding why examinees get syllogism questions wrong is as important as knowing the correct reasoning. Here are the most frequent errors and how to avoid them.

#### Error 1: Reversing Logical Direction

**The mistake:** Treating "All A are B" as if it also means "All B are A."

**Why it happens:** In everyday conversation, we often use "all" loosely. "All my friends are students" might conversationally imply "the students are my friends." But logically, the group "students" is much larger than "my friends."

**How to avoid:** Always ask: "Is the subject completely inside the predicate, or vice versa?" Draw the circles if unsure.

#### Error 2: Assuming Unsupported Conclusions

**The mistake:** Adding real-world knowledge to fill gaps in the premises.

**Example:**
- All birds have feathers.
- Penguins are birds.
- Therefore, penguins can fly. ❌

The premises say nothing about flying. Even though we know penguins can't fly, the error here is importing information not in the premises. A different examinee might incorrectly conclude penguins CAN fly because "birds fly" — also not stated.

**How to avoid:** Treat the premises as the ONLY facts in the universe. Nothing else exists.

#### Error 3: Ignoring Qualifiers

**The mistake:** Reading "some" as "all" or "no" as "some...not."

**Example:**
- Some employees are managers.
- Examinee concludes: All employees are managers. ❌

**How to avoid:** Circle or underline the quantifier (All/No/Some) in each premise before reasoning.

#### Error 4: Confusing Possibility with Certainty

**The mistake:** Selecting an answer that COULD be true rather than one that MUST be true.

**Example:**
- All teachers are college graduates.
- Some college graduates are musicians.
- "Some teachers are musicians" — COULD be true, but is NOT guaranteed. ❌

**How to avoid:** For each answer choice, ask: "Is there ANY scenario where the premises are true but this conclusion is false?" If yes, it's not a valid conclusion.

#### Error 5: Selecting Partially Correct Answers

**The mistake:** Choosing an answer that captures part of the logic but overstates or understates the conclusion.

**Example:**
- All managers are employees.
- All employees pay taxes.
- Valid: "All managers pay taxes." ✅
- Trap answer: "Only managers pay taxes." ❌ (overstates — other employees also pay taxes)

**How to avoid:** Check that the answer says EXACTLY what the premises guarantee — no more, no less.

### 4.13 Advanced Categorical Reasoning

Advanced syllogism questions on the CSE involve nested categories, abstract structures, and multi-step deductive chains that require careful tracking of relationships.

#### Nested Categories

When multiple subset relationships exist, you must track the hierarchy:

**Example:**
- All department secretaries are presidential appointees.
- All presidential appointees are government officials.
- All government officials are public servants.
- Therefore, all department secretaries are public servants. ✅

The chain: Secretary ⊂ Presidential Appointee ⊂ Government Official ⊂ Public Servant

#### Combining Positive and Negative Chains

**Example:**
- All pharmacists are licensed professionals.
- All licensed professionals passed a board exam.
- No person who failed the board exam may practice independently.
- Therefore, all pharmacists may practice independently. ❌

**Why invalid:** The premises tell us pharmacists PASSED the board exam. The third premise tells us who may NOT practice (those who failed). But passing the board exam doesn't automatically mean you may practice independently — there could be other requirements.

**Valid conclusion:** No pharmacist failed the board exam. ✅ (contrapositive chain)

#### Abstract Syllogisms

CSE questions sometimes use abstract categories (letters or symbols) to prevent real-world knowledge from interfering:

**Example:**
- All X are Y.
- All Y are Z.
- Some Z are W.
- Which must be true?
  - (a) All X are Z ✅
  - (b) Some X are W ❌
  - (c) All Z are X ❌
  - (d) No X are W ❌

**Analysis:** X⊂Y⊂Z is guaranteed. But the relationship between Z and W is only partial ("some"), and we can't determine where X falls within Z relative to W.

#### Multiple Valid Conclusions

Some premise sets support more than one valid conclusion. CSE questions may ask you to identify ALL valid conclusions or the STRONGEST valid conclusion:

**Premises:**
- All roses are flowers.
- All flowers are plants.
- No plants are minerals.

**Valid conclusions:**
- All roses are plants. ✅ (chain)
- All roses are flowers. ✅ (given directly)
- No roses are minerals. ✅ (chain + negative)
- No flowers are minerals. ✅ (direct from premises 2+3)

**Invalid conclusions:**
- All plants are roses. ❌ (reversal)
- All flowers are roses. ❌ (reversal)
- Some minerals are plants. ❌ (contradicts premise 3)

---

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Syllogisms work by chaining categorical relationships: if all A are B, and all B are C, then all A are C. The middle term (B) connects the two premises and disappears in the conclusion. Validity depends entirely on the arrangement of quantifiers (all, some, no) and the position of terms — not on what the terms represent. This is why you can test validity using Venn diagrams: if the diagram of the premises forces the conclusion to be true, the syllogism is valid.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Basic Universal Affirmative

**Problem:** All government employees are taxpayers. Juan is a government employee. Which conclusion is valid?
(a) Juan is not a taxpayer
(b) All taxpayers are government employees
(c) Juan is a taxpayer
(d) Some taxpayers are not government employees

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Premise 1: All government employees are taxpayers (Gov. Employee ⊂ Taxpayer)
2. Premise 2: Juan is a government employee (Juan ∈ Gov. Employee)
3. Since Juan is in the subset (government employees) that is entirely contained within the superset (taxpayers), Juan must be a taxpayer.
4. (a) contradicts the premises. (b) reverses the relationship (illicit conversion). (d) may be true but isn't guaranteed by these premises alone.

**Answer:** (c) Juan is a taxpayer.

### Example 2: Universal Negative

**Problem:** No contractual worker is eligible for retirement benefits. Pedro is a contractual worker. Which must be true?
(a) Pedro is eligible for retirement benefits
(b) Pedro is not eligible for retirement benefits
(c) Some contractual workers are eligible for retirement benefits
(d) All workers not eligible for retirement benefits are contractual

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Premise 1: No contractual worker is eligible for retirement benefits (complete exclusion)
2. Premise 2: Pedro is a contractual worker
3. Since NO contractual worker is eligible, and Pedro IS a contractual worker, Pedro cannot be eligible.
4. (a) directly contradicts the premises. (c) contradicts premise 1. (d) reverses the relationship — there could be non-contractual workers also ineligible for other reasons.

**Answer:** (b) Pedro is not eligible for retirement benefits.

### Example 3: Particular Statement Trap

**Problem:** All lawyers passed the bar exam. Some government employees are lawyers. Which conclusion is valid?
(a) All government employees passed the bar exam
(b) Some government employees passed the bar exam
(c) All who passed the bar exam are government employees
(d) No government employee failed the bar exam

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Premise 1: All lawyers passed the bar exam (Lawyer ⊂ Bar Passer)
2. Premise 2: Some government employees are lawyers (Gov. Employee ∩ Lawyer ≠ ∅)
3. Those government employees who ARE lawyers must have passed the bar exam (from premise 1).
4. So SOME government employees passed the bar exam. ✅
5. (a) is too strong — only SOME gov employees are lawyers. (c) reverses the relationship. (d) is too strong — it claims ALL gov employees didn't fail, but we only know about those who are lawyers.

**Answer:** (b) Some government employees passed the bar exam.

### Example 4: Detecting the Undistributed Middle

**Problem:** All teachers are college graduates. All engineers are college graduates. Which conclusion is valid?
(a) All teachers are engineers
(b) Some teachers are engineers
(c) No teachers are engineers
(d) None of the above conclusions is valid

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Premise 1: All teachers are college graduates (Teacher ⊂ College Grad)
2. Premise 2: All engineers are college graduates (Engineer ⊂ College Grad)
3. Both groups are inside "college graduates" but we have NO information about their relationship to each other.
4. Teachers and engineers could overlap completely, partially, or not at all — all scenarios are consistent with the premises.
5. Since none of (a), (b), or (c) is guaranteed, the answer is (d).

**Answer:** (d) None of the above conclusions is valid.

### Example 5: Multi-Premise Chain

**Problem:** All directors are managers. All managers are employees. All employees receive benefits. Which must be true?
(a) All who receive benefits are directors
(b) All directors receive benefits
(c) All employees are directors
(d) Some managers are not employees

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Chain: Director ⊂ Manager ⊂ Employee ⊂ Benefits Receiver
2. Following the chain: All directors are managers, all managers are employees, all employees receive benefits.
3. Therefore: All directors receive benefits (transitive chain). ✅
4. (a) reverses the entire chain. (c) reverses the chain. (d) contradicts premise 2.

**Answer:** (b) All directors receive benefits.

### Example 6: Negative Chain

**Problem:** All nurses are healthcare workers. No healthcare worker is exempt from continuing education. Which must be true?
(a) Some nurses are exempt from continuing education
(b) No nurse is exempt from continuing education
(c) All who are not exempt are nurses
(d) Some healthcare workers are exempt from continuing education

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Premise 1: All nurses are healthcare workers (Nurse ⊂ Healthcare Worker)
2. Premise 2: No healthcare worker is exempt from continuing education (Healthcare Worker ∩ Exempt = ∅)
3. Since nurses are inside healthcare workers, and NO healthcare worker is exempt, NO nurse can be exempt.
4. (a) contradicts our conclusion. (c) reverses the relationship. (d) contradicts premise 2.

**Answer:** (b) No nurse is exempt from continuing education.

### Example 7: Advanced — Particular + Universal Combination

**Problem:** Some teachers are researchers. All researchers publish papers. Which must be true?
(a) All teachers publish papers
(b) Some teachers publish papers
(c) All who publish papers are teachers
(d) No teacher publishes papers

**Step-by-step solution:**
1. Premise 1: Some teachers are researchers (Teacher ∩ Researcher ≠ ∅)
2. Premise 2: All researchers publish papers (Researcher ⊂ Paper Publisher)
3. Those teachers who ARE researchers must publish papers (from premise 2).
4. Since at least some teachers are researchers, at least some teachers publish papers. ✅
5. (a) is too strong — only SOME teachers are researchers. (c) reverses the relationship. (d) contradicts our conclusion.

**Answer:** (b) Some teachers publish papers.

## Key Takeaways

- A syllogism is a deductive argument where a conclusion follows necessarily from two or more premises — if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false
- The four categorical statement types are: All A are B (universal affirmative), No A are B (universal negative), Some A are B (particular affirmative), Some A are not B (particular negative)
- "All A are B" does NOT mean "All B are A" — this reversal (illicit conversion) is the most common syllogism error
- "No A are B" IS validly convertible — it also means "No B are A" because mutual exclusion works both directions
- "Some" in logic means "at least one" — it is compatible with "all" and does not imply "not all"
- A conclusion is valid only if it is impossible for the premises to be true while the conclusion is false — use the counterexample method to test
- The undistributed middle fallacy occurs when two groups share a common superset but are assumed to be related to each other
- In multi-premise chains, the conclusion can only be as strong as the weakest link — a particular premise limits the conclusion to particular
- Two negative premises cannot produce a valid conclusion in standard syllogistic logic
- Always reason strictly from the given premises — never import real-world knowledge or assumptions
- Use the diagram method (circles) to verify: if you can draw a scenario where premises are true but conclusion is false, the conclusion is invalid
- On the CSE, eliminate answers that reverse relationships, overstate scope (turning "some" into "all"), or add information not in the premises
