# Conditional Reasoning

## Introduction

**Conditional Reasoning** is the ability to analyze "if-then" relationships and draw logically valid conclusions from them. It is one of the most heavily tested skills in the Analytical Ability section of the Philippine Civil Service Examination (CSE) because government work constantly requires evaluating rules, policies, and regulations that follow conditional structures.

Every law, memorandum, and administrative order contains conditional logic: "If the applicant meets requirement X, then benefit Y is granted." Civil servants must interpret these correctly — a single logical error can deny services to qualified citizens or approve them for unqualified ones.

Conditional reasoning tests your ability to:
- Identify logical dependencies between statements
- Distinguish what is guaranteed from what is merely possible
- Recognize valid deductions versus logical traps
- Follow chains of reasoning to their correct conclusions

Common mistakes examinees make in conditional logic questions:
- Reversing the direction of a conditional (assuming "If P then Q" means "If Q then P")
- Confusing necessary conditions with sufficient conditions
- Affirming the consequent (invalid reasoning that looks convincing)
- Denying the antecedent (another invalid pattern disguised as logic)
- Failing to follow multi-step logical chains to their conclusion

This lesson will systematically build your conditional reasoning skills from foundational concepts through advanced deductive analysis.

## Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define conditional reasoning and explain its role in logical analysis
- Identify the antecedent and consequent in any conditional statement
- Distinguish necessary conditions from sufficient conditions accurately
- Apply modus ponens and modus tollens to derive valid conclusions
- Recognize and reject affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent
- Analyze multi-step logical chains using hypothetical syllogism
- Translate conditional statements into symbolic notation (P → Q)
- Test the validity of conditional arguments systematically
- Solve CSE-style conditional reasoning questions under time pressure
- Eliminate incorrect answer choices using logical consistency checks

## 4.1 What Is Conditional Reasoning?

Conditional reasoning is the process of drawing conclusions from "if-then" statements. A conditional statement establishes a **logical dependency** between two propositions: if one thing is true, then another thing must also be true.

The basic structure is:

| Component | Name | Role | Example |
|-----------|------|------|---------|
| P | Antecedent | The condition (trigger) | "If it rains" |
| Q | Consequent | The result (outcome) | "the ground becomes wet" |
| P → Q | Conditional | The full if-then statement | "If it rains, the ground becomes wet." |

The antecedent is the **cause** or **trigger**. The consequent is the **effect** or **outcome**. The arrow (→) means "leads to" or "guarantees."

**Key principle:** A conditional statement only makes a claim about what happens when the antecedent is true. It says nothing definitive about what happens when the antecedent is false.

**Beginner examples:**

- If it rains, the ground becomes wet. (Rain triggers wetness.)
- If employees submit reports late, delays occur. (Late submission triggers delays.)
- If students study consistently, performance improves. (Consistent study triggers improvement.)

**Workplace examples:**

- If an applicant has 10 years of service, they qualify for promotion.
- If the budget request exceeds ₱1 million, director approval is required.
- If a government vehicle is used without authorization, a penalty is imposed.

**Critical insight:** The conditional does NOT claim that the antecedent is the ONLY way to produce the consequent. "If it rains, the ground becomes wet" does not mean rain is the only cause of wet ground — sprinklers, flooding, or spills can also wet the ground. The conditional only guarantees: rain → wet ground. It does not guarantee: wet ground → rain.

## 4.2 Structure of Conditional Statements

Recognizing the logical direction of a conditional statement is essential. The "if" part always identifies the antecedent, and the "then" part always identifies the consequent — regardless of sentence order.

### Identifying the Parts

| Statement | Antecedent (P) | Consequent (Q) |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| "If electricity fails, computers shut down." | Electricity fails | Computers shut down |
| "If traffic increases, travel time becomes longer." | Traffic increases | Travel time becomes longer |
| "If the employee is absent, work is reassigned." | Employee is absent | Work is reassigned |
| "Computers shut down if electricity fails." | Electricity fails | Computers shut down |

**Note:** In the last example, the sentence places the consequent first, but "if" still marks the antecedent. The word "if" is the logical marker — not sentence position.

### Natural Language Variations

Conditional statements appear in many forms in English. All of these translate to P → Q:

| English Phrasing | Logical Form | Example |
|-----------------|--------------|---------|
| "If P, then Q" | P → Q | If it rains, the streets get wet. |
| "Q if P" | P → Q | The streets get wet if it rains. |
| "P implies Q" | P → Q | Rain implies wet streets. |
| "Whenever P, Q" | P → Q | Whenever it rains, streets get wet. |
| "P is sufficient for Q" | P → Q | Rain is sufficient for wet streets. |
| "Q is necessary for P" | P → Q | Wet streets are necessary for rain. |
| "All P are Q" | P → Q | All rainy days produce wet streets. |
| "P only if Q" | P → Q | It rains only if streets get wet. |

**CSE Tip:** The phrase "only if" is the most commonly misread. "P only if Q" means P → Q (not Q → P). Think of it as: P cannot happen without Q. If P happened, Q must have happened.

### Why Order Matters

Reversing a conditional changes its meaning entirely:

- **Original:** "If you are a doctor, then you have a medical degree." (True)
- **Reversed:** "If you have a medical degree, then you are a doctor." (Not necessarily true — you might be a researcher, professor, or retired.)

The original and its reverse are NOT logically equivalent. This is one of the most common errors on the CSE.

## 4.3 Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

Understanding the difference between necessary and sufficient conditions is critical for the CSE. Many questions are designed specifically to test whether you confuse these two concepts.

### Sufficient Conditions

A **sufficient condition** is one that **guarantees** the outcome. If the sufficient condition is met, the outcome MUST occur — no additional requirements needed.

| Statement | Sufficient Condition | Guaranteed Outcome |
|-----------|---------------------|-------------------|
| "Being a square is sufficient for having four sides." | Being a square | Having four sides |
| "A presidential appointment guarantees the position." | Presidential appointment | Getting the position |
| "Scoring 100% guarantees passing." | Scoring 100% | Passing |

**Key insight:** A sufficient condition guarantees the outcome, but it is NOT the only way to achieve it. Scoring 100% guarantees passing, but you can also pass with 75%.

### Necessary Conditions

A **necessary condition** is one that **must exist** for the outcome to occur. Without it, the outcome is impossible — but having it alone does not guarantee the outcome.

| Statement | Necessary Condition | Required For |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------| 
| "Having four sides is necessary for being a square." | Having four sides | Being a square |
| "Passing the CSE is necessary for permanent government appointment." | Passing the CSE | Permanent appointment |
| "Oxygen is necessary for fire." | Oxygen | Fire |

**Key insight:** A necessary condition is required but not enough by itself. Oxygen is necessary for fire, but oxygen alone does not create fire — you also need fuel and heat.

### The Relationship Between Necessary and Sufficient

| If P is sufficient for Q... | Then Q is necessary for P |
|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| Being a square → having four sides | Having four sides is required for being a square |
| Rain → wet ground | Wet ground is required for rain to have occurred |

| If Q is necessary for P... | Then P is sufficient for Q |
|----------------------------|---------------------------|
| Four sides required for square → being a square guarantees four sides |
| CSE required for appointment → appointment guarantees CSE was passed |

**The confusion trap:** Examinees often mix up "guarantees" and "requires":
- "Passing the CSE is necessary for appointment" does NOT mean passing guarantees appointment
- "A presidential appointment is sufficient for the position" does NOT mean it's the only path

### Easy Examples

- Water is necessary for life. (No water → no life. But water alone doesn't create life.)
- Being 18 is necessary to vote. (Under 18 → cannot vote. But being 18 alone doesn't mean you will vote.)

### Medium Examples

- Completing training is necessary for certification. (No training → no certification. But training alone may not be enough — you might also need to pass an exam.)
- A valid ID is sufficient for building entry. (Valid ID → entry granted. But other methods like biometrics might also work.)

### Advanced Examples

- Having a law degree is necessary but not sufficient for practicing law. (You also need to pass the bar exam.)
- Being appointed by the President is both necessary and sufficient for becoming a Cabinet secretary. (It's the only path AND it guarantees the role.)

## 4.4 Valid Conditional Arguments

A valid conditional argument is one where the conclusion follows **necessarily** from the premises. If the premises are true, the conclusion MUST be true — there is no possible scenario where the premises hold but the conclusion fails.

### Modus Ponens (Affirming the Antecedent)

**Structure:**
- Premise 1: If P, then Q. (P → Q)
- Premise 2: P is true.
- Conclusion: Therefore, Q is true. ✅

**Why valid:** The conditional guarantees that whenever P is true, Q must be true. Since P is true, Q must follow.

**Easy example:**
- If it rains, the streets become wet.
- It is raining.
- Therefore, the streets are wet. ✅

**Medium example:**
- If an employee accumulates three absences without leave, a written warning is issued.
- Employee Santos accumulated three absences without leave.
- Therefore, a written warning is issued to Employee Santos. ✅

**Advanced example:**
- If the project budget exceeds ₱5 million AND the timeline exceeds 12 months, board approval is required.
- The project budget is ₱7 million and the timeline is 18 months.
- Therefore, board approval is required. ✅

### Modus Tollens (Denying the Consequent)

**Structure:**
- Premise 1: If P, then Q. (P → Q)
- Premise 2: Q is false. (¬Q)
- Conclusion: Therefore, P is false. (¬P) ✅

**Why valid:** If P guarantees Q, and Q did not happen, then P could not have happened. This is the contrapositive in action.

**Easy example:**
- If it rains, the streets become wet.
- The streets are not wet.
- Therefore, it did not rain. ✅

**Medium example:**
- If the document was approved, it would bear the director's signature.
- The document does not bear the director's signature.
- Therefore, the document was not approved. ✅

**Advanced example:**
- If all committee members agreed, the resolution would have been signed.
- The resolution was not signed.
- Therefore, not all committee members agreed. ✅

### Hypothetical Syllogism (Chain Reasoning)

**Structure:**
- Premise 1: If P, then Q. (P → Q)
- Premise 2: If Q, then R. (Q → R)
- Conclusion: Therefore, if P, then R. (P → R) ✅

**Why valid:** The chain is unbroken. P triggers Q, and Q triggers R, so P ultimately triggers R.

**Easy example:**
- If it rains, the ground gets wet.
- If the ground gets wet, the roads become slippery.
- Therefore, if it rains, the roads become slippery. ✅

**Medium example:**
- If an employee is late three times, they receive a memo.
- If an employee receives a memo, their performance rating decreases.
- Therefore, if an employee is late three times, their performance rating decreases. ✅

**Advanced example:**
- If the senator files the bill, the committee reviews it.
- If the committee reviews it, a public hearing is scheduled.
- If a public hearing is scheduled, stakeholders submit position papers.
- Therefore, if the senator files the bill, stakeholders submit position papers. ✅

### Disjunctive Syllogism

**Structure:**
- Premise 1: Either P or Q. (P ∨ Q)
- Premise 2: Not P. (¬P)
- Conclusion: Therefore, Q. ✅

**Easy example:**
- Either the report was submitted on time or a penalty applies.
- The report was not submitted on time.
- Therefore, a penalty applies. ✅

## 4.5 Invalid Conditional Arguments

An invalid conditional argument is one where the conclusion does NOT necessarily follow from the premises. Even if the premises are true, the conclusion might be false. These are **formal fallacies** — they have a structure that looks logical but is actually broken.

### Affirming the Consequent (INVALID)

**Structure:**
- Premise 1: If P, then Q. (P → Q)
- Premise 2: Q is true.
- Conclusion: Therefore, P is true. ❌ INVALID

**Why invalid:** Q being true does not prove P caused it. Q could be true for entirely different reasons.

**Easy example:**
- If it rains, the streets become wet.
- The streets are wet.
- Therefore, it rained. ❌

**Why wrong:** The streets could be wet from a water main break, street cleaning, or a spill. Rain is not the only cause of wet streets.

**Medium example:**
- If an employee is promoted, their salary increases.
- Employee Garcia's salary increased.
- Therefore, Employee Garcia was promoted. ❌

**Why wrong:** The salary increase could be from a step increment, cost-of-living adjustment, or reclassification — not necessarily a promotion.

**Advanced example:**
- If the committee approved the project, funding was released.
- Funding was released.
- Therefore, the committee approved the project. ❌

**Why wrong:** Funding could have been released through an emergency allocation, executive order, or alternative budget line.

**CSE trap:** This fallacy is the most common distractor in CSE conditional reasoning questions. The conclusion sounds reasonable but is not logically guaranteed.

### Denying the Antecedent (INVALID)

**Structure:**
- Premise 1: If P, then Q. (P → Q)
- Premise 2: P is false. (¬P)
- Conclusion: Therefore, Q is false. (¬Q) ❌ INVALID

**Why invalid:** The conditional only says what happens when P is true. It says nothing about what happens when P is false. Q might still be true through other means.

**Easy example:**
- If it rains, the streets become wet.
- It did not rain.
- Therefore, the streets are not wet. ❌

**Why wrong:** The streets could still be wet from other causes.

**Medium example:**
- If a student studies, the student passes.
- The student did not study.
- Therefore, the student did not pass. ❌

**Why wrong:** The student might pass through prior knowledge, natural aptitude, or lucky guessing.

**Advanced example:**
- If the budget is approved by Congress, the infrastructure project proceeds.
- The budget was not approved by Congress.
- Therefore, the infrastructure project does not proceed. ❌

**Why wrong:** The project might proceed through emergency executive funding, private-public partnerships, or foreign aid.

### Summary: Valid vs. Invalid at a Glance

| Argument Form | Structure | Valid? | Name |
|--------------|-----------|--------|------|
| P → Q, P ∴ Q | Affirm antecedent | ✅ Valid | Modus Ponens |
| P → Q, ¬Q ∴ ¬P | Deny consequent | ✅ Valid | Modus Tollens |
| P → Q, Q → R ∴ P → R | Chain premises | ✅ Valid | Hypothetical Syllogism |
| P ∨ Q, ¬P ∴ Q | Eliminate option | ✅ Valid | Disjunctive Syllogism |
| P → Q, Q ∴ P | Affirm consequent | ❌ Invalid | Formal Fallacy |
| P → Q, ¬P ∴ ¬Q | Deny antecedent | ❌ Invalid | Formal Fallacy |

## 4.6 Logical Chains

Logical chains extend conditional reasoning across multiple linked statements. When conditionals share terms, you can connect them into a chain and derive conclusions that span the entire sequence.

### Basic Chain Reasoning

If A → B and B → C, then A → C.

The middle term (B) acts as a bridge connecting the first condition to the final conclusion.

**Easy example:**
- If Maria passes the exam, she gets certified. (A → B)
- If Maria gets certified, she qualifies for promotion. (B → C)
- Therefore, if Maria passes the exam, she qualifies for promotion. (A → C) ✅

**Medium example:**
- If the department exceeds its budget, an audit is triggered. (A → B)
- If an audit is triggered, all expenditures are reviewed. (B → C)
- If all expenditures are reviewed, unauthorized spending is detected. (C → D)
- Therefore, if the department exceeds its budget, unauthorized spending is detected. (A → D) ✅

### Chain Consistency Rules

For a chain to be valid:
1. Each link must share a term with the next link
2. The shared term must appear as the consequent of one statement and the antecedent of the next
3. No link in the chain can be broken by a qualifier like "some" or "might"

**Valid chain:**
- All managers attend meetings. (Manager → Attends meetings)
- All who attend meetings receive the memo. (Attends meetings → Receives memo)
- Therefore, all managers receive the memo. (Manager → Receives memo) ✅

**Broken chain (invalid):**
- All managers attend meetings. (Manager → Attends meetings)
- Some who attend meetings receive the memo. (Some attenders → Receives memo)
- Therefore, all managers receive the memo. ❌

**Why broken:** "Some" breaks the universal chain. Not all meeting attendees get the memo, so we cannot guarantee managers do.

### Indirect Deductions

Sometimes you must combine chain reasoning with modus tollens:

- If A → B → C, and C is false, then A must be false.

**Example:**
- If the project is approved, construction begins. (A → B)
- If construction begins, materials are ordered. (B → C)
- Materials were not ordered. (¬C)
- Therefore, the project was not approved. (¬A) ✅

**Reasoning:** If A were true, then B would be true, then C would be true. But C is false. So A cannot be true.

### Advanced Multi-Branch Chains

Some CSE questions present chains with multiple conditions feeding into one conclusion:

- If A and B, then C. ((A ∧ B) → C)
- If C, then D. (C → D)
- A is true. B is true.
- Therefore, D is true. ✅

**Example:**
- If the applicant has a degree AND passes the interview, they are hired.
- If they are hired, they undergo orientation.
- The applicant has a degree. The applicant passed the interview.
- Therefore, the applicant undergoes orientation. ✅

## 4.7 Symbolic Representation of Conditionals

Symbolic notation allows you to analyze the logical structure of arguments without being distracted by content. This is especially useful when CSE questions use unfamiliar or emotionally loaded topics.

### Core Symbols

| Symbol | Name | Meaning | Example |
|--------|------|---------|---------|
| P, Q, R | Variables | Stand for propositions | P = "It rains" |
| → | Conditional | "If...then" | P → Q = "If it rains, streets get wet" |
| ¬ | Negation | "Not" / "It is not the case that" | ¬P = "It does not rain" |
| ∧ | Conjunction | "And" (both true) | P ∧ Q = "It rains and it's cold" |
| ∨ | Disjunction | "Or" (at least one true) | P ∨ Q = "It rains or it's cold" |
| ∴ | Therefore | Conclusion marker | P → Q, P ∴ Q |

### Translating Conditionals to Symbols

| English | Symbolic |
|---------|----------|
| "If the employee is qualified, they are promoted." | P → Q |
| "The employee is not qualified." | ¬P |
| "If the employee is qualified and experienced, they are promoted." | (P ∧ Q) → R |
| "Either the report is approved or it is returned." | P ∨ Q |
| "If the report is not approved, it is returned." | ¬P → Q |
| "The project proceeds only if the budget is approved." | P → Q (Project → Budget approved) |

### Representing Valid Arguments Symbolically

**Modus Ponens:**
```
P → Q
P
∴ Q
```

**Modus Tollens:**
```
P → Q
¬Q
∴ ¬P
```

**Hypothetical Syllogism:**
```
P → Q
Q → R
∴ P → R
```

**Disjunctive Syllogism:**
```
P ∨ Q
¬P
∴ Q
```

### Representing Invalid Arguments Symbolically

**Affirming the Consequent (INVALID):**
```
P → Q
Q
∴ P  ← NOT VALID
```

**Denying the Antecedent (INVALID):**
```
P → Q
¬P
∴ ¬Q  ← NOT VALID
```

### Practice: Translate and Evaluate

**Statement:** "If all supervisors must submit reports weekly and Carla is a supervisor, then Carla must submit reports weekly."

**Symbolic translation:**
- Let P = "All supervisors must submit reports weekly"
- Let Q = "Carla is a supervisor"
- Let R = "Carla must submit reports weekly"
- Structure: (P ∧ Q) → R
- P is true (given rule). Q is true (given fact). Therefore R is true. ✅ (Modus Ponens)

## 4.8 Testing Conditional Validity

Testing validity means checking whether a conclusion is logically guaranteed by the premises. There are three systematic methods you can use on the CSE.

### Method 1: The Counterexample Test

Ask: "Can I imagine a scenario where the premises are true but the conclusion is false?"

- If YES → the argument is **invalid**
- If NO → the argument is **valid**

**Test this argument:**
- Premise: If an employee passes the CSE, they are eligible for appointment.
- Premise: Juan is eligible for appointment.
- Conclusion: Juan passed the CSE.

**Counterexample:** Juan could be eligible through a different pathway (e.g., he's a presidential appointee exempt from CSE). Premises true, conclusion false → **INVALID** (affirming the consequent).

### Method 2: Pattern Matching

Identify which standard form the argument matches:

1. Does it affirm the antecedent? → Modus Ponens (VALID)
2. Does it deny the consequent? → Modus Tollens (VALID)
3. Does it chain conditionals? → Hypothetical Syllogism (VALID)
4. Does it affirm the consequent? → INVALID
5. Does it deny the antecedent? → INVALID

**Quick identification:**
- Look at what is being affirmed or denied
- Match it to the antecedent or consequent of the conditional
- Determine validity instantly

### Method 3: Truth Table Verification

For complex arguments, construct a truth table:

| P | Q | P → Q | Argument Valid? |
|---|---|-------|-----------------|
| T | T | T | Check if conclusion holds |
| T | F | F | Conditional is false here — skip |
| F | T | T | Check if conclusion holds |
| F | F | T | Check if conclusion holds |

An argument is valid if there is NO row where all premises are true but the conclusion is false.

### Common Validity Traps on the CSE

**Trap 1: The "sounds reasonable" distractor**
- The conclusion seems plausible in real life but is not logically guaranteed
- Always test with the counterexample method, not intuition

**Trap 2: The "partial truth" distractor**
- The conclusion could be true but is not necessarily true
- "Could be true" ≠ "Must be true"

**Trap 3: The "extra information" distractor**
- The conclusion introduces information not present in the premises
- Valid conclusions only use information from the given premises

## 4.9 Practical Applications of Conditional Reasoning

Conditional reasoning is not an abstract academic exercise — it directly applies to the daily work of civil servants.

### Workplace Procedures

**Leave policy:**
- Rule: "If an employee exhausts all sick leave credits, they may charge illness absences to vacation leave."
- Symbolic: Exhausted sick leave → May use vacation leave for illness
- Application: Employee Reyes has no sick leave left and is ill. Conclusion: She may use vacation leave. ✅

**Overtime authorization:**
- Rule: "If overtime work is not pre-approved by the supervisor, no overtime pay is granted."
- Symbolic: ¬(Pre-approved) → ¬(Overtime pay)
- Contrapositive: Overtime pay → Pre-approved ✅
- Application: Employee Cruz received overtime pay. Conclusion: The overtime was pre-approved. ✅

### Government Regulations

**Eligibility determination:**
- Regulation: "If a government employee has rendered at least 15 years of service and is at least 60 years old, they are eligible for optional retirement."
- Symbolic: (Service ≥ 15 years ∧ Age ≥ 60) → Eligible for optional retirement
- Application: Director Lim has 20 years of service and is 62 years old. Conclusion: She is eligible. ✅
- Application: Analyst Ramos has 10 years of service and is 63 years old. Conclusion: NOT eligible (both conditions must be met). ❌

### Administrative Decision-Making

**Procurement rules:**
- Rule: "If the procurement amount exceeds ₱50,000, competitive bidding is required."
- Symbolic: Amount > ₱50,000 → Competitive bidding required
- Modus Tollens: No competitive bidding was conducted → Amount did not exceed ₱50,000 ✅
- Invalid conclusion: Amount is ₱30,000 → Competitive bidding is not required ❌ (denying the antecedent — bidding might still be required for other reasons)

### Project Management

**Milestone dependencies:**
- If Phase 1 is complete, Phase 2 can begin.
- If Phase 2 is complete, testing can start.
- If testing is complete, deployment is authorized.
- Chain: Phase 1 complete → Phase 2 → Testing → Deployment authorized
- Modus Tollens: Deployment not authorized → Testing not complete → Phase 2 not complete → Phase 1 not complete

### Safety Protocols

**Emergency procedures:**
- Rule: "If a fire alarm sounds, all personnel must evacuate immediately."
- Symbolic: Fire alarm → Immediate evacuation
- Application: The fire alarm sounded. Conclusion: All personnel must evacuate. ✅
- Invalid: Personnel evacuated. Conclusion: The fire alarm sounded. ❌ (Could be a drill, earthquake, or bomb threat.)

## 4.10 Step-by-Step Conditional Solving Strategies

### The ACID Method

Use this four-step method for every conditional reasoning question on the CSE:

**A — Analyze the conditional.** Identify the antecedent (P) and consequent (Q). Write P → Q.

**C — Check what is given.** Is P affirmed? Is Q denied? Is P denied? Is Q affirmed? This determines which argument form applies.

**I — Identify the form.** Match to modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, affirming the consequent, or denying the antecedent.

**D — Determine validity.** If the form is valid, the conclusion follows. If invalid, the conclusion is NOT guaranteed.

### Elimination Strategy for Multiple Choice

When facing four answer choices:

Step 1: Eliminate any choice that contradicts a given premise (immediately wrong).

Step 2: Eliminate any choice that reverses the conditional direction (converse fallacy).

Step 3: Eliminate any choice that uses "all" when premises only support "some."

Step 4: Eliminate any choice that introduces information not in the premises.

The remaining choice is your answer.

### Time-Pressure Shortcuts

| Pattern You See | Instant Conclusion |
|----------------|-------------------|
| "If P then Q" + "P is true" | Q is true (Modus Ponens) |
| "If P then Q" + "Q is false" | P is false (Modus Tollens) |
| "If P then Q" + "Q is true" | CANNOT conclude P (Invalid) |
| "If P then Q" + "P is false" | CANNOT conclude ¬Q (Invalid) |
| "If P then Q" + "If Q then R" + "P is true" | R is true (Chain + MP) |
| "Either P or Q" + "Not P" | Q is true (Disjunctive Syllogism) |

### Solving Under Time Pressure

1. Read the conditional statement FIRST — before the question or choices
2. Identify P and Q immediately
3. Read what is given (affirmed or denied)
4. Match the pattern — don't reason from scratch each time
5. Select the answer that matches the valid conclusion
6. If no valid conclusion exists, select "Cannot be determined" or the equivalent

**CSE Tip:** If you find yourself thinking "well, it COULD be true..." — that's a sign the conclusion is NOT logically guaranteed. Choose the answer that MUST be true, not one that merely might be.

## 4.11 Common Errors in Conditional Reasoning

### Error 1: Reversing the Conditional

**Wrong thinking:** "If it rains, the ground gets wet" → "If the ground is wet, it rained"

**Correction:** The converse is NOT logically equivalent to the original. Multiple causes can produce the same effect. Always check: am I reversing the arrow?

### Error 2: Confusing Necessary and Sufficient

**Wrong thinking:** "Passing the CSE is necessary for appointment" → "Passing the CSE guarantees appointment"

**Correction:** Necessary means required, not guaranteed. You need it, but it alone is not enough.

### Error 3: Affirming the Consequent

**Wrong thinking:** "If promoted, salary increases. Salary increased. Therefore, promoted."

**Correction:** The salary could have increased for other reasons. The consequent being true does not prove the antecedent.

### Error 4: Denying the Antecedent

**Wrong thinking:** "If it rains, streets are wet. It didn't rain. Therefore, streets aren't wet."

**Correction:** Streets can be wet from other causes. The antecedent being false tells you nothing about the consequent.

### Error 5: Overlooking Hidden Assumptions

**Wrong thinking:** "If the project is funded, it will succeed." (Assumes funding is the only factor.)

**Correction:** Success may require funding AND competent staff AND proper planning. The conditional may be oversimplified.

### Error 6: Treating "Some" as "All"

**Wrong thinking:** "Some employees are engineers. Juan is an employee. Therefore, Juan is an engineer."

**Correction:** "Some" means at least one, not all. Juan might or might not be an engineer — you cannot conclude either way.

### Error 7: Ignoring Qualifiers

**Wrong thinking:** "If ALL conditions are met, approval is granted" → "One condition is met, so approval is granted."

**Correction:** "All" means every single one. Meeting one condition out of many is insufficient.

### Why Distractors Appear Convincing

CSE question writers design wrong answers to exploit these exact errors:
- They present the converse as if it were the original
- They present affirming the consequent as if it were modus ponens
- They present denying the antecedent as if it were modus tollens
- They use emotionally plausible scenarios to override logical analysis

**Defense:** Always check the logical structure, not the real-world plausibility. A conclusion can sound reasonable and still be logically invalid.

## 4.12 Advanced Conditional Analysis

### Nested Conditions

Some CSE questions involve conditions within conditions:

**Example:** "If the budget is approved and the director signs, then the project proceeds only if the timeline is feasible."

**Symbolic:** (P ∧ Q) → (R → S)
- P = budget approved
- Q = director signs
- R = project proceeds
- S = timeline is feasible

**Analysis:** Even if budget is approved AND director signs, the project still requires a feasible timeline. The outer conditional enables the inner conditional.

### Compound Conditionals

**Conjunctive antecedent:** (P ∧ Q) → R
- Both P and Q must be true for R to follow
- If either P or Q is false, the conditional tells us nothing about R

**Example:** "If the applicant has a degree AND three years of experience, they qualify."
- Has degree but only 1 year experience → Cannot conclude qualification
- Has 5 years experience but no degree → Cannot conclude qualification
- Has degree AND 4 years experience → Qualifies ✅

**Disjunctive antecedent:** (P ∨ Q) → R
- Either P or Q (or both) being true triggers R

**Example:** "If the employee is late OR absent, a deduction applies."
- Late but not absent → Deduction applies ✅
- Absent but not late → Deduction applies ✅
- Both late and absent → Deduction applies ✅
- Neither late nor absent → Cannot conclude deduction

### Contrapositive of Compound Conditionals

The contrapositive of (P ∧ Q) → R is ¬R → ¬(P ∧ Q), which simplifies to ¬R → (¬P ∨ ¬Q).

**In plain language:** If the result did NOT occur, then at least one of the conditions was NOT met.

**Example:**
- Rule: If an employee has a degree AND passes the interview, they are hired.
- Fact: The employee was not hired.
- Conclusion: Either they don't have a degree, OR they didn't pass the interview (or both). ✅

### Abstract Logical Structures

CSE questions sometimes use abstract variables to prevent you from relying on real-world knowledge:

**Example:**
- If all X are Y, and all Y are Z, what can be concluded?
- Answer: All X are Z. (Hypothetical syllogism with universal statements.)

**Example:**
- If no A are B, and some C are A, what can be concluded?
- Answer: Some C are not B. ✅
- Invalid conclusion: No C are B. ❌ (Only SOME C are A; other C might be B.)

### Multi-Condition Reasoning

**Example with three premises:**
- Premise 1: If the report is complete, it is submitted to the supervisor.
- Premise 2: If the report is submitted to the supervisor, it is reviewed within 3 days.
- Premise 3: If the report is reviewed within 3 days, feedback is provided by Friday.
- Given: The report is complete.
- Chain conclusion: Feedback is provided by Friday. ✅
- Given instead: Feedback was NOT provided by Friday.
- Modus Tollens through chain: The report is NOT complete. ✅

## Mini Practice Set

**1.** If all managers must attend the Monday meeting and Pedro is a manager, what can be validly concluded?
**Answer:** Pedro must attend the Monday meeting.
**Explanation:** This is modus ponens. The universal rule applies to Pedro since he is a manager.

**2.** If it rains, the outdoor event is cancelled. The outdoor event was not cancelled. What can be concluded?
**Answer:** It did not rain.
**Explanation:** This is modus tollens. If rain guarantees cancellation and cancellation didn't happen, rain didn't happen.

**3.** If an employee passes the training, they receive certification. Employee Reyes received certification. Can we conclude she passed the training?
**Answer:** No — this is affirming the consequent (invalid). She might have received certification through another pathway.
**Explanation:** The conditional only guarantees: training → certification. It does not guarantee: certification → training.

**4.** If the budget exceeds ₱1 million, board approval is required. The budget is ₱500,000. Can we conclude board approval is NOT required?
**Answer:** No — this is denying the antecedent (invalid). Board approval might be required for other reasons.
**Explanation:** The conditional says nothing about what happens when the budget does NOT exceed ₱1 million.

**5.** If A then B. If B then C. If C then D. A is true. What is true?
**Answer:** B, C, and D are all true.
**Explanation:** Chain reasoning (hypothetical syllogism) plus modus ponens. A triggers B, B triggers C, C triggers D.

**6.** Having a valid passport is necessary for international travel. Maria does not have a valid passport. What can be concluded?
**Answer:** Maria cannot travel internationally.
**Explanation:** Necessary condition: No passport → No international travel. This is the contrapositive of: International travel → Valid passport.

**7.** Passing the bar exam is sufficient for practicing law. Juan did not pass the bar exam. Can we conclude he cannot practice law?
**Answer:** No — this is denying the antecedent. There might be other paths to practice (though in reality there aren't, the logical structure alone doesn't guarantee it).
**Explanation:** Sufficient means it guarantees the outcome, but the conditional doesn't say it's the ONLY path.

**8.** If the alarm sounds, evacuate immediately. If you evacuate, proceed to the assembly point. The alarm sounded. What should you do?
**Answer:** Proceed to the assembly point.
**Explanation:** Chain: Alarm → Evacuate → Assembly point. Alarm sounded (modus ponens through chain).

**9.** Either the report was submitted on time or a penalty is imposed. The report was submitted on time. Can we conclude no penalty is imposed?
**Answer:** Not necessarily — "or" in logic is inclusive unless stated otherwise. Both could be true.
**Explanation:** P ∨ Q being true and P being true does not eliminate Q. The penalty might still apply for other reasons.

**10.** If all employees who work overtime receive extra pay, and no extra pay was given to Department X, what can be concluded about Department X?
**Answer:** No employee in Department X worked overtime.
**Explanation:** Modus tollens on the universal: Overtime → Extra pay. No extra pay → No overtime.

**11.** If the document is classified, only authorized personnel may access it. The document is classified. What follows?
**Answer:** Only authorized personnel may access it.
**Explanation:** Modus ponens. The condition (classified) is met, so the restriction applies.

**12.** Being a Filipino citizen is necessary for taking the Civil Service Exam. Carlos is taking the CSE. What can we conclude?
**Answer:** Carlos is a Filipino citizen.
**Explanation:** Necessary condition means: Taking CSE → Filipino citizen. Carlos is taking CSE, so he must be Filipino.

**13.** If the project is approved AND funding is secured, construction begins. The project is approved but funding is not secured. What can be concluded?
**Answer:** We cannot conclude that construction begins.
**Explanation:** Both conditions must be met (conjunctive antecedent). One alone is insufficient.

**14.** If traffic is heavy, employees arrive late. If employees arrive late, the morning meeting is delayed. Traffic is not heavy. Can we conclude the meeting is not delayed?
**Answer:** No — this is denying the antecedent through a chain. The meeting could be delayed for other reasons.
**Explanation:** The chain only guarantees: heavy traffic → late → delayed. No heavy traffic tells us nothing definitive.

**15.** All licensed professionals passed a board exam. Dr. Santos is a licensed professional. What must be true?
**Answer:** Dr. Santos passed a board exam.
**Explanation:** Universal rule + specific instance = modus ponens.

**16.** If the applicant scores above 80, they are shortlisted. If they are shortlisted, they are invited for interview. The applicant was not invited for interview. What can be concluded?
**Answer:** The applicant did not score above 80.
**Explanation:** Modus tollens through chain: Not invited → Not shortlisted → Did not score above 80.

**17.** Completing the training is sufficient for receiving a certificate. Ana completed the training. What follows?
**Answer:** Ana receives a certificate.
**Explanation:** Sufficient condition met → outcome guaranteed. Modus ponens.

**18.** If no unauthorized personnel enter the building, security is maintained. Security was not maintained. What can be concluded?
**Answer:** Unauthorized personnel entered the building.
**Explanation:** Modus tollens. ¬Q → ¬P. Security not maintained → unauthorized entry occurred.

**19.** If either the manager or the supervisor approves, the request is processed. Neither the manager nor the supervisor approved. What can be concluded?
**Answer:** The request is not processed.
**Explanation:** (P ∨ Q) → R. ¬(P ∨ Q) means ¬P ∧ ¬Q. Contrapositive: ¬R → ¬(P ∨ Q). Since ¬(P ∨ Q) is true, we use modus tollens in reverse — actually, since the antecedent (P ∨ Q) is false, the conditional tells us nothing. However, the contrapositive ¬R → ¬(P ∨ Q) combined with ¬(P ∨ Q) doesn't give us ¬R directly. The correct analysis: the antecedent is false, so we cannot conclude anything about R from this conditional alone. But if this is the ONLY way to process the request, then ¬R follows.
**Explanation:** This depends on whether the conditional represents the only path. In strict logic, denying the antecedent is invalid. The answer depends on context provided in the actual question.

**20.** If all team members submit their reports AND the deadline has not passed, the compilation is finalized. All team members submitted their reports. The deadline has not passed. What can be concluded?
**Answer:** The compilation is finalized.
**Explanation:** Both parts of the conjunctive antecedent are satisfied. Modus ponens applies.

## Exam Strategies

- Always identify P and Q before looking at answer choices — this prevents emotional reasoning
- If the question affirms Q and asks you to conclude P, it's affirming the consequent — INVALID
- If the question denies P and asks you to conclude ¬Q, it's denying the antecedent — INVALID
- The contrapositive (¬Q → ¬P) is ALWAYS valid when the original (P → Q) is given
- For chain questions, trace the links step by step — don't skip
- "Only if" means the consequent is necessary: "P only if Q" = P → Q
- "Unless" typically means "if not": "Q unless P" = ¬P → Q
- When in doubt, try the counterexample method — if you can imagine one exception, the conclusion is invalid
- Never select an answer just because it sounds true in real life — test the logical structure
- On timed exams, pattern-match first (MP, MT, HS, AC, DA) before reasoning from scratch

## Memory Aids

- **MP = Match the P.** Modus Ponens: you have P → Q and P. Match! Conclude Q.
- **MT = Miss the T(hen).** Modus Tollens: the "then" part (Q) is missing/false. Conclude ¬P.
- **AC = Already Concluded.** Affirming the Consequent: you already see Q and jump to P. WRONG — Q could have other causes.
- **DA = Doesn't Apply.** Denying the Antecedent: P didn't happen, so you assume Q doesn't apply. WRONG — Q might still happen.
- **Chain = Train.** Like train cars linked together: A→B→C→D. Pull the first car (A), all follow.
- **Necessary = Need it.** You NEED it, but having it isn't enough alone.
- **Sufficient = Sure thing.** It's a SURE THING — guarantees the outcome.
- **Contrapositive = Flip and Negate.** P → Q becomes ¬Q → ¬P. Always valid.
- **Converse = Convert (and probably wrong).** Q → P. Converting the direction usually breaks validity.

## Quick Recap

Conditional reasoning is the analysis of "if-then" relationships to determine what conclusions are logically guaranteed. The antecedent (P) is the condition; the consequent (Q) is the result. Valid argument forms include modus ponens (P → Q, P ∴ Q), modus tollens (P → Q, ¬Q ∴ ¬P), and hypothetical syllogism (P → Q, Q → R ∴ P → R). Invalid forms include affirming the consequent (P → Q, Q ∴ P) and denying the antecedent (P → Q, ¬P ∴ ¬Q). Necessary conditions are required but not sufficient; sufficient conditions guarantee but are not the only path. Logical chains connect multiple conditionals through shared terms. Always test conclusions with the counterexample method and match argument patterns rather than relying on intuition.
