# Cause-Effect and Progression Relationships

## Introduction

**Cause-Effect and Progression Relationships** are a critical category of word analogy questions that test your ability to recognize logical sequences, causal connections, and developmental pathways between concepts. Unlike synonym or antonym analogies that compare meaning equivalence or opposition, these questions require you to understand how one event, action, or state leads to another — either as a direct result or as a step in a larger process.

In the Philippine Civil Service Examination, cause-effect and progression analogies appear because they directly mirror the analytical thinking required in government work: understanding how policies produce outcomes, how procedures follow sequences, and how actions generate consequences. Civil servants must constantly evaluate cause-and-effect chains — budget decisions leading to program outcomes, policy violations leading to sanctions, training programs leading to competency development — making this reasoning skill both testable and practically essential.

These relationship types are distinct from synonym/antonym analogies in a fundamental way: they test **sequential and causal reasoning** rather than **semantic equivalence**. You are not asking "do these words mean the same thing?" but rather "does A lead to B, cause B, or come before B in a logical sequence?"

### Why Cause-Effect and Progression Relationships Matter

- Government decision-making requires tracing causes to effects (policy → outcome)
- Administrative procedures follow strict sequences (application → review → approval → implementation)
- Performance evaluation depends on linking actions to consequences (negligence → error → accountability)
- Project management requires understanding progression (planning → execution → monitoring → evaluation)
- These questions efficiently test logical precision — many examinees confuse simple association with actual causation, revealing weak analytical skills
- Understanding progression relationships improves problem-solving in workplace scenarios

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Confusing association with causation** — "doctor : hospital" is a workplace association, not cause-effect; "virus : illness" is genuine causation
2. **Reversing the causal direction** — "flood : rain" reverses the logic; rain causes flood, not the other way around
3. **Selecting loosely related outcomes** — choosing "fire : water" (used to fight fire) instead of "fire : smoke" (fire produces smoke)
4. **Ignoring chronological order** — failing to verify that the first element genuinely precedes the second in time or logic
5. **Confusing progression with category** — "puppy : dog" is a growth progression, but "dog : animal" is classification
6. **Treating correlation as causation** — two things that happen together are not necessarily in a cause-effect relationship
7. **Overlooking the specificity of the relationship** — choosing a vague connection when the question demands precise causal or sequential correspondence


## Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define cause-and-effect relationships and identify them accurately in word pairs
- Identify action-and-consequence relationships and distinguish intended from unintended outcomes
- Recognize sequence and chronological relationships in ordered word pairs
- Identify process and progression structures where one stage develops into another
- Distinguish logical causal order from unrelated association or simple co-occurrence
- Analyze analogy structures by labeling the exact relationship type before evaluating choices
- Apply systematic elimination to solve CSE-style progression analogy questions efficiently
- Recognize and avoid traps that exploit confusion between association and causation

## 4.1 What Is a Cause-Effect or Progression Analogy?

A **cause-effect or progression analogy** is a logical comparison that states: two words share a specific causal or sequential relationship, and you must find another pair of words that shares the *same type* of relationship.

The standard format is:

**A : B :: C : D**

Read as: "A is to B as C is to D."

The key principle: **the relationship between A and B must be identical in type and direction to the relationship between C and D.**

### The "A Leads to B" Structure

In cause-effect analogies, the first word produces, triggers, or results in the second word:

- "Rain leads to flood" → relationship: cause produces effect
- "Study leads to knowledge" → relationship: cause produces effect
- Therefore: Rain : Flood :: Study : Knowledge ✓

### The "A Happens Before B" Structure

In progression analogies, the first word represents an earlier stage that develops into the second:

- "Seed develops into plant" → relationship: earlier stage to later stage
- "Caterpillar develops into butterfly" → relationship: earlier stage to later stage
- Therefore: Seed : Plant :: Caterpillar : Butterfly ✓

### How These Differ from Other Analogy Types

| Analogy Type | What It Tests | Example |
|-------------|---------------|---------|
| Synonym | Meaning equivalence | brave : courageous |
| Antonym | Meaning opposition | hot : cold |
| Part-Whole | Structural composition | wheel : car |
| Classification | Category membership | rose : flower |
| **Cause-Effect** | **Causal production** | **fire : smoke** |
| **Progression** | **Sequential development** | **seed : tree** |

### Why Logical Consistency Matters

The first pair establishes a **relationship template**. The second pair must follow that template exactly — same relationship type, same direction, same level of specificity.

**Example of consistency:**
- Rain : Flood :: Drought : Famine ✓ (both pairs: natural cause → natural disaster)

**Example of inconsistency:**
- Rain : Flood :: Umbrella : Dry ✗ (first pair is cause-effect; second pair is tool-purpose)

### Bridge Sentences for Cause-Effect

Form a precise sentence connecting the first pair:
- "A **causes** B" (direct causation)
- "A **leads to** B" (indirect causation)
- "A **produces** B" (creation/generation)
- "A **results in** B" (outcome)
- "A **develops into** B" (progression)
- "A **comes before** B" (sequence)

Then test each answer choice with the same bridge sentence.

## 4.2 Cause-and-Result Relationships

A **cause-and-result relationship** exists when one event, condition, or action directly produces or triggers another. The first word is the trigger; the second word is what happens as a result.

### Types of Causal Relationships

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Direct physical cause | One physical event produces another | fire : smoke |
| Behavioral cause | An action produces a predictable outcome | exercise : fitness |
| Environmental cause | A natural condition produces a natural result | drought : famine |
| Emotional cause | A stimulus produces an emotional response | insult : anger |
| Institutional cause | A policy or decision produces a systemic outcome | investment : growth |

### Identifying Causes and Effects

Ask yourself: "Does A directly produce or trigger B?"

- "Fire produces smoke." ✓ (cause-effect)
- "Exercise produces fitness." ✓ (cause-effect)
- "Rain produces flood." ✓ (cause-effect)
- "Doctor produces hospital." ✗ (workplace association, not causation)

### Critical Distinction: Causation vs. Association

| Pair | Relationship | Why |
|------|-------------|-----|
| fire : smoke | **Cause-effect** | Fire directly produces smoke |
| fire : firefighter | **Association** | Firefighters respond to fire but fire doesn't cause firefighters |
| rain : flood | **Cause-effect** | Excessive rain directly causes flooding |
| rain : umbrella | **Association** | Umbrellas are used during rain but rain doesn't produce umbrellas |
| study : knowledge | **Cause-effect** | Studying directly produces knowledge |
| study : library | **Association** | Libraries are where people study but studying doesn't cause libraries |

**Rule:** If you can say "A directly produces/triggers/generates B," it is cause-effect. If you can only say "A is related to B" or "A is associated with B," it is NOT cause-effect.

## 4.3 Action-and-Consequence Relationships

An **action-and-consequence relationship** exists when a deliberate human action produces a predictable outcome — either positive or negative. Unlike pure cause-effect (which can be natural/automatic), action-consequence implies human agency and often moral or legal judgment.

### Types of Action-Consequence Relationships

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Positive action → positive consequence | Good behavior produces reward | practice : improvement |
| Negative action → negative consequence | Bad behavior produces punishment | cheating : punishment |
| Action → natural consequence | Any action produces its logical result | saving : security |
| Action → legal consequence | Behavior triggers legal/institutional response | speeding : violation |

### Identifying Actions and Consequences

Ask yourself: "If someone does A, what predictably happens as B?"

- "If someone practices, improvement follows." ✓ (action-consequence)
- "If someone cheats, punishment follows." ✓ (action-consequence)
- "If someone saves money, security follows." ✓ (action-consequence)
- "If someone speeds, a violation follows." ✓ (action-consequence)


### Positive vs. Negative Consequences

| Action | Positive Consequence | Negative Consequence |
|--------|---------------------|---------------------|
| study | success | — |
| neglect | — | failure |
| save | wealth | — |
| waste | — | poverty |
| exercise | health | — |
| overwork | — | burnout |
| cooperate | harmony | — |
| conflict | — | division |

### Common Action-Consequence Pairs for the CSE

#### Workplace Consequences

| Action | Consequence | Type |
|--------|------------|------|
| training | competence | positive |
| negligence | accident | negative |
| collaboration | efficiency | positive |
| absenteeism | termination | negative |
| innovation | progress | positive |
| misconduct | suspension | negative |
| diligence | promotion | positive |
| insubordination | dismissal | negative |

#### Legal and Social Consequences

| Action | Consequence | Type |
|--------|------------|------|
| crime | punishment | negative |
| compliance | approval | positive |
| fraud | prosecution | negative |
| honesty | trust | positive |
| speeding | fine | negative |
| volunteering | recognition | positive |
| plagiarism | expulsion | negative |
| charity | goodwill | positive |

### Distinguishing Intended from Unintended Consequences

- **Intended:** practice → improvement (the person practices *in order to* improve)
- **Unintended:** negligence → accident (the person did not *intend* the accident)
- Both are valid action-consequence relationships for analogy purposes
- The key test: "Does A predictably lead to B?" — not "Did the person want B?"

## 4.4 Sequence Relationships

A **sequence relationship** exists when two words represent consecutive stages, steps, or positions in an established order. The first word comes immediately before (or logically precedes) the second word in a recognized sequence.

### Types of Sequence Relationships

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Numerical sequence | Consecutive positions in a counting system | first : second |
| Temporal sequence | Consecutive time periods | Monday : Tuesday |
| Procedural sequence | Consecutive steps in a process | draft : revision |
| Developmental sequence | Consecutive life or growth stages | infancy : childhood |
| Hierarchical sequence | Consecutive ranks in a system | private : corporal |


### Identifying Sequence Relationships

Ask yourself: "Does A come directly before B in a recognized order?"

- "Monday comes before Tuesday." ✓ (temporal sequence)
- "Draft comes before final copy." ✓ (procedural sequence)
- "Infancy comes before childhood." ✓ (developmental sequence)
- "Private comes before corporal." ✓ (hierarchical sequence)

### Critical Distinction: Sequence vs. Category

| Pair | Relationship | Why |
|------|-------------|-----|
| Monday : Tuesday | **Sequence** | Tuesday follows Monday in the weekly order |
| Monday : weekday | **Category** | Monday is a type of weekday |
| infancy : childhood | **Sequence** | Childhood follows infancy in development |
| infancy : life stage | **Category** | Infancy is a type of life stage |
| private : corporal | **Sequence** | Corporal is the next rank after private |
| private : soldier | **Category** | A private is a type of soldier |

**Rule:** If A and B are on the same level and one follows the other in order, it is a sequence. If one is a broader category that contains the other, it is classification.

## 4.5 Chronological Order Relationships

A **chronological order relationship** is a specific type of sequence where the ordering is based on time — what happens first in time versus what happens later. These differ from general sequences because they emphasize the irreversible flow of time.

### Types of Chronological Relationships

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Daily chronology | Events ordered within a day | sunrise : sunset |
| Life chronology | Events ordered within a lifetime | birth : death |
| Historical chronology | Events ordered in history | war : peace treaty |
| Procedural chronology | Steps ordered in time | enrollment : graduation |
| Natural chronology | Natural events in time order | planting : harvesting |


### Identifying Chronological Relationships

Ask yourself: "Does A always happen before B in time?"

- "Sunrise always happens before sunset." ✓ (daily chronology)
- "Birth always happens before death." ✓ (life chronology)
- "Enrollment always happens before graduation." ✓ (procedural chronology)
- "Planting always happens before harvesting." ✓ (natural chronology)

### Critical Distinction: Chronological vs. Cause-Effect

Some pairs are BOTH chronological AND causal. Others are only chronological:

| Pair | Chronological? | Causal? | Primary Relationship |
|------|---------------|---------|---------------------|
| sunrise : sunset | ✓ | ✗ | Chronological only (sunrise doesn't cause sunset) |
| planting : harvesting | ✓ | Partially | Chronological (planting enables but doesn't directly cause harvest) |
| study : pass | ✓ | ✓ | Cause-effect (studying causes passing) |
| infection : fever | ✓ | ✓ | Cause-effect (infection causes fever) |

**Rule:** If A merely comes before B in time without producing B, it is purely chronological. If A both precedes AND produces B, it is cause-effect (the stronger relationship).

### Common Chronological Pairs

| Before | After | Context |
|--------|-------|---------|
| sunrise | sunset | daily cycle |
| breakfast | lunch | meals |
| birth | death | life span |
| enrollment | graduation | education |
| planning | implementation | project management |
| engagement | marriage | life events |
| interview | hiring | employment |
| trial | verdict | legal process |
| election | inauguration | government |
| proposal | legislation | lawmaking |

### Recognizing Irreversible Progression

Some chronological relationships are irreversible — they can only go in one direction:
- Birth → Death (cannot reverse)
- Seed → Tree (cannot un-grow)
- Raw material → Finished product (cannot un-manufacture easily)

Others are cyclical — they repeat:
- Sunrise → Sunset → Sunrise (daily cycle)
- Spring → Summer → Autumn → Winter → Spring (seasonal cycle)
- Planting → Harvesting → Planting (agricultural cycle)

For analogy purposes, always follow the forward direction within one cycle.

## 4.6 Process and Progression Relationships

A **process and progression relationship** exists when the first word represents an earlier developmental stage that transforms or evolves into the second word. This is different from simple sequence (which is about order) — progression implies transformation or growth.

### Types of Progression Relationships

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Biological progression | Living thing develops into mature form | caterpillar : butterfly |
| Educational progression | Learner advances through stages | student : graduate |
| Organizational progression | Person advances through ranks | trainee : manager |
| Material progression | Raw material becomes finished product | ore : metal |
| Document progression | Draft becomes final version | proposal : policy |


### Identifying Progression Relationships

Ask yourself: "Does A develop into, transform into, or mature into B?"

- "A caterpillar transforms into a butterfly." ✓ (biological progression)
- "A trainee develops into a manager." ✓ (organizational progression)
- "Raw material transforms into a product." ✓ (material progression)
- "A proposal develops into a policy." ✓ (document progression)

### Critical Distinction: Progression vs. Causation

| Pair | Progression? | Cause-Effect? | Why |
|------|-------------|---------------|-----|
| caterpillar : butterfly | ✓ | ✗ | The caterpillar *becomes* the butterfly — it IS the butterfly |
| seed : tree | ✓ | ✗ | The seed *becomes* the tree — same organism |
| rain : flood | ✗ | ✓ | Rain doesn't become a flood — rain causes a flood (different things) |
| study : knowledge | ✗ | ✓ | Study doesn't become knowledge — study produces knowledge |
| trainee : expert | ✓ | ✗ | The trainee *becomes* the expert — same person |
| negligence : accident | ✗ | ✓ | Negligence doesn't become an accident — it causes one |

**Rule:** If A *transforms into* or *becomes* B (same entity changing form), it is progression. If A *produces* or *triggers* B (different entities), it is cause-effect.

## 4.7 Relationship Analysis Techniques

Solving cause-effect and progression analogies requires a systematic approach. Here are the core techniques:

### Technique 1: The Bridge Sentence Method

Create a precise sentence connecting the first pair, then test each answer choice:

**Question:** Rain : Flood :: Drought : _____
- Bridge: "Rain causes flood."
- Test A: "Drought causes desert." (desert is a place, not an event — weak match)
- Test B: "Drought causes famine." ✓ (natural cause → natural disaster)
- Test C: "Drought causes heat." (heat causes drought, not the reverse)
- Test D: "Drought causes water." (drought reduces water, doesn't cause it)

**Answer:** B) Famine


### Technique 2: Relationship Type Labeling

Before looking at choices, label the relationship:
1. Read Pair 1
2. Ask: "Is this cause-effect, action-consequence, sequence, or progression?"
3. Label it precisely
4. Only consider choices that match your label

This eliminates wrong answers that use a different relationship type.

### Technique 3: Direction Verification

Always verify that both pairs flow in the same direction:
- If Pair 1 goes cause → effect, Pair 2 must also go cause → effect
- If Pair 1 goes earlier → later, Pair 2 must also go earlier → later
- NEVER accept a pair where the direction is reversed

**Example of direction trap:**
- Seed : Tree :: Butterfly : _____
- WRONG answer: Caterpillar (this reverses the direction — later → earlier)
- The question asks: "What does butterfly develop INTO?" not "What was butterfly before?"

### Technique 4: Specificity Matching

Match the level of specificity between pairs:
- If Pair 1 is a direct, one-step cause-effect, Pair 2 should also be direct
- If Pair 1 is a multi-step progression, Pair 2 should also span multiple steps
- Don't mix immediate causes with distant consequences

| Pair 1 | Good Match | Bad Match | Why Bad |
|--------|-----------|-----------|---------|
| rain : flood (immediate) | heat : fire (immediate) | pollution : extinction (multi-step) | Different specificity |
| seed : tree (full progression) | egg : bird (full progression) | egg : crack (partial) | Incomplete progression |

### Technique 5: The Elimination Cascade

When stuck, eliminate in this order:
1. **Wrong relationship type** — if Pair 1 is cause-effect, eliminate any synonym, antonym, or category choices
2. **Wrong direction** — eliminate any choice where the effect comes before the cause
3. **Wrong specificity** — eliminate choices that are too broad or too narrow
4. **Weakest causal link** — among remaining choices, pick the strongest, most direct connection

### Workplace Decision-Making
- Tracing policy decisions to their outcomes (budget cut → reduced services → public complaints)
- Predicting consequences of proposed actions before implementation
- Identifying root causes of problems rather than treating symptoms

- Understanding how programs progress through stages (proposal → pilot → full implementation)

- Sequencing project activities in logical order for effective execution

- Ordering tasks in correct sequence (requirements → design → development → testing → deployment)

- Understanding how delays in one stage cascade to later stages

### Problem-Solving
- Working backward from effects to identify causes (high turnover → investigate causes)
- Predicting downstream effects of proposed changes
- Distinguishing correlation from causation in data analysis

## 4.11 Step-by-Step Analogy Solving Strategies

Use this systematic approach for every cause-effect and progression analogy question:

### The FLOW Method

| Step | Action | What You Do |
|------|--------|-------------|
| **F** — Find the relationship | Read Pair 1 | Ask: "Does A cause B, come before B, or develop into B?" |
| **L** — Label precisely | Classify it | Cause-effect? Sequence? Progression? Action-consequence? |
| **O** — Orient the direction | Check flow | Confirm: A → B (not B → A) |
| **W** — Weed out mismatches | Eliminate | Remove choices with wrong type, direction, or specificity |

### Detailed Solving Process

**Step 1: Identify the relationship type (3-5 seconds)**
- Read the first pair
- Classify: Is this cause-effect, action-consequence, sequence, or progression?
- Form your bridge sentence

**Step 2: Verify direction (2 seconds)**
- Confirm the first word leads to/causes/precedes the second
- If reversed, re-read carefully

**Step 3: Test each choice (5-8 seconds)**
- Apply your bridge sentence to each answer option
- The correct answer will fit the same relationship type AND direction

**Step 4: Verify specificity (2-3 seconds)**
- Confirm the answer matches the same level of directness
- Immediate cause → immediate effect (not distant consequence)

### Time Management

Total time per question: 12-18 seconds maximum

- If you cannot identify the relationship within 5 seconds, flag and return
- If two choices seem equally valid, pick the more direct causal/sequential link
- Never spend more than 25 seconds on a single analogy item

## 4.12 Common Errors in Cause-Effect and Progression Analogies

### Error 1: Confusing Association with Causation

**Wrong:** Rain : Umbrella (umbrellas are associated with rain but rain doesn't produce umbrellas)
**Right:** Rain : Flood (rain directly produces flooding)

Association means two things often appear together. Causation means one directly produces the other.

### Error 2: Reversing the Causal Direction

**Wrong:** Smoke : Fire (smoke doesn't cause fire — fire causes smoke)
**Right:** Fire : Smoke (fire produces smoke — correct direction)

Always verify: does the FIRST word produce/cause/precede the SECOND word?

### Error 3: Selecting Weak Causal Links

**Wrong:** Poverty : Sadness (poverty may correlate with sadness but doesn't directly cause it)
**Right:** Poverty : Hunger (poverty directly causes inability to afford food → hunger)

Choose the most direct, strongest causal connection available.

### Error 4: Confusing Progression with Category

**Wrong:** Treating "puppy : animal" as progression (it's classification — puppy is a type of animal)
**Right:** "Puppy : Dog" is progression (a puppy grows into a dog — same entity developing)

Progression = same entity changing form. Category = entity belonging to a group.

### Error 5: Ignoring Chronological Consistency

**Wrong:** Graduation : Enrollment (reverses the time order)
**Right:** Enrollment : Graduation (enrollment comes first in time)

In sequence/chronological analogies, the first word must always precede the second in time.

### Error 6: Mixing Relationship Types Between Pairs

**Wrong:** Fire : Smoke :: Monday : Tuesday
- Pair 1 is cause-effect (fire produces smoke)
- Pair 2 is sequence (Monday precedes Tuesday but doesn't cause it)
- These are DIFFERENT relationship types — invalid analogy

**Right:** Fire : Smoke :: Drought : Famine
- Both pairs are cause-effect (natural phenomenon produces natural disaster)

### Multi-Step Causal Chains

Some advanced questions test your ability to identify the correct link in a longer chain:

**Full chain:** Deforestation → Erosion → Flooding → Displacement

**Possible analogy pairs from this chain:**
- Deforestation : Erosion (immediate cause-effect) ✓
- Deforestation : Flooding (indirect cause-effect — skips a step)
- Erosion : Flooding (immediate cause-effect) ✓

**Rule for advanced questions:** Match the NUMBER of steps. If Pair 1 is an immediate (one-step) cause-effect, Pair 2 should also be immediate.

## Quick Recap

| Concept | Key Takeaway |
|---------|-------------|
| Cause-and-result | A directly produces or triggers B (fire : smoke) |
| Action-and-consequence | A deliberate action leads to a predictable outcome (cheating : punishment) |
| Sequence | A comes immediately before B in an established order (Monday : Tuesday) |
| Chronological order | A happens before B in time, emphasizing temporal flow (sunrise : sunset) |
| Process and progression | A transforms into or develops into B — same entity changing (seed : tree) |
| Causation vs. association | "A produces B" is causation; "A is related to B" is mere association |
| Progression vs. causation | "A becomes B" is progression; "A produces B" is causation |
| Direction matters | Always verify first word leads to/precedes second word |
| Bridge sentence | Form "A causes/precedes/becomes B" and test each choice with it |
| Elimination order | Wrong type → wrong direction → wrong specificity → weakest link |

## Memory Aids

### The "PRODUCES or BECOMES" Quick Check

Before looking at any answer choices, ask ONE question about Pair 1:
- "Does A **produce** B?" → This is cause-effect (fire produces smoke)
- "Does A **become** B?" → This is progression (seed becomes tree)
- "Does A **come before** B?" → This is sequence (Monday comes before Tuesday)

This single question identifies the relationship type instantly.

### The Direction Arrow

Always draw a mental arrow: A → B

- Fire → Smoke ✓ (fire produces smoke)
- Smoke → Fire ✗ (smoke doesn't produce fire)

If the arrow doesn't work left-to-right, the pair is reversed or invalid.

### The "Same Entity" Test for Progression

**"Is A the same thing as B, just at a different stage?"**

- Seed and tree = same organism at different stages → PROGRESSION ✓
- Rain and flood = different things (water vs. disaster) → CAUSE-EFFECT ✓
- Caterpillar and butterfly = same organism at different stages → PROGRESSION ✓
- Negligence and accident = different things (behavior vs. event) → CAUSE-EFFECT ✓

### The Specificity Ladder

```
IMMEDIATE:    fire → smoke (one step)
NEAR:         fire → damage (two steps: fire → burn → damage)
DISTANT:      fire → homelessness (many steps)
```

Match the rung. If Pair 1 is immediate, Pair 2 must also be immediate.

### Common Trap Patterns to Memorize

| Trap Type | Example | Why It's Wrong |
|-----------|---------|---------------|
| Association disguised as cause | rain : umbrella | Rain doesn't produce umbrellas |
| Reversed direction | smoke : fire | Smoke doesn't cause fire |
| Category disguised as progression | puppy : animal | Animal is a category, not a later stage |
| Tool disguised as effect | fire : extinguisher | Extinguisher fights fire, isn't caused by it |
| Workplace association | doctor : hospital | Doctor works at hospital, hospital isn't caused by doctor |
