# Language, Meaning, and Context Relationships

## Explanations

### Introduction

**Language, Meaning, and Context Relationships** represent a sophisticated category of word analogy questions that test your ability to recognize how words relate through definitions, contextual usage, vocabulary associations, and multiple meanings. Unlike synonym/antonym analogies that focus on equivalence or opposition, these questions demand that you understand *how language operates in context* — how the same word shifts meaning depending on usage, how words connect to their domains, and how definitions map precisely to concepts.

These questions appear in the Philippine Civil Service Examination because government work requires precise language interpretation. Civil servants read memoranda, draft policy documents, interpret regulations, and communicate across departments — all tasks that demand contextual vocabulary mastery. A word like "draft" means something entirely different in a budget meeting versus a ventilation report versus a writing workshop. The CSE tests whether you can navigate these shifts reliably.

Language and context analogies are distinct from pure synonym/antonym items because they test *semantic flexibility* — your ability to hold multiple meanings simultaneously and select the correct one based on relational cues. This mirrors real workplace reasoning: when you read "The committee will table the motion," you must know whether "table" means to postpone (American English) or to present for discussion (British/Philippine English) based on institutional context.

### Why Language and Context Analogies Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination tests these relationships because:

- Government documents use specialized vocabulary where context determines meaning — "resolution" means different things in a barangay council versus a screen display specification
- Civil servants must interpret words precisely in legal, administrative, and policy contexts without misreading intent
- Multiple-meaning words create ambiguity in official communications; the ability to disambiguate is a core professional skill
- Vocabulary association questions test whether examinees understand how concepts cluster within professional domains
- These items separate examinees who understand language *in use* from those who only memorize isolated definitions
- Context-based reasoning directly predicts job performance in document-heavy government roles

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Selecting the most common meaning instead of the contextual meaning** — choosing "bank = place for money" when the analogy requires "bank = river edge"
2. **Confusing word-and-definition pairs with synonym pairs** — "physician : doctor" is a definition relationship (formal term : common term), not a simple synonym pair
3. **Ignoring domain signals** — failing to notice that the first pair operates in a legal context, then selecting an answer from a different domain
4. **Treating all associations as definitions** — "nurse : hospital" is an association (worker : workplace), not a definition relationship
5. **Overlooking polysemy traps** — not recognizing that a word in the question is being used in its secondary or tertiary meaning
6. **Applying synonym logic to context questions** — these questions often require you to identify *which meaning* is active, not whether two words mean the same thing
7. **Rushing past the relationship type** — jumping to an answer without first determining whether the analogy tests definition, context, association, or multiple meaning

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define word-and-definition relationships and distinguish them from synonym relationships
- Identify context-based meanings by analyzing surrounding relational cues
- Recognize vocabulary associations that connect words to their domains or fields
- Identify multiple-meaning words and determine which meaning is active in an analogy
- Distinguish literal from contextual meanings in analogy structures
- Analyze analogy structures that depend on semantic flexibility
- Solve CSE-style language and context analogy questions efficiently under time pressure
- Eliminate incorrect answer choices by testing relationship type consistency

### 4.1 What Are Language, Meaning, and Context Analogies?

A **language, meaning, and context analogy** tests whether you can identify how words relate through *semantic connections* that go beyond simple equivalence or opposition. These analogies ask: "What is the precise nature of the relationship between these two words — and can you find another pair with the same relationship?"

There are four core relationship types in this category:

| Relationship Type | What It Tests | Example |
|-------------------|--------------|---------|
| Word-and-Definition | Formal term mapped to its meaning | physician : one who treats illness |
| Context-Based Meaning | A word's meaning determined by usage context | cold (personality) : unfriendly |
| Vocabulary Association | A word connected to its domain or field | scalpel : surgery |
| Multiple-Meaning | Same word with different meanings in different contexts | bark (tree) : bark (dog sound) |

**How these differ from synonym/antonym analogies:**

In synonym analogies, both words in a pair mean the same thing. In language/context analogies, the relationship is more nuanced:

- "Physician : doctor" looks like synonyms, but the analogy relationship is *formal term : common term* (a definition-register relationship)
- "Bank : money" looks like an association, but the analogy might be testing *institution : what it manages* (a functional definition)
- "Light : not heavy" is a *word : its definition in a specific context* (excluding the "illumination" meaning)

**The key insight:** These analogies require you to identify *which type of semantic relationship* is operating before you can find the matching pair.

#### The "A means B" Structure

Many language/context analogies follow this pattern:

- Physician : one who practices medicine (word : definition)
- Frugal : careful with money (word : contextual definition)
- Verdict : courtroom (word : domain where it belongs)
- Spring : season OR coil (word : one of its multiple meanings)

Your job is to determine which structure the first pair uses, then find the answer pair that uses the *identical* structure.

#### Beginner Examples

- **Brief : short** → Word-and-definition (brief means short in duration or length)
- **Bank : financial institution** → Word-and-definition (bank defined by its function)
- **Light : illumination** → Word mapped to one specific meaning (excluding "not heavy")
- **Pen : writing instrument** → Word mapped to one specific meaning (excluding "enclosure for animals")

### 4.2 Word-and-Definition Relationships

A **word-and-definition relationship** exists when one word in the pair is the term and the other word (or phrase) is its precise meaning. This is more specific than a synonym relationship — it maps a word to its *definitional core*.

#### How Definitions Differ from Synonyms

| Relationship | Example | Why It's Different |
|-------------|---------|-------------------|
| Synonym | big : large | Two interchangeable words |
| Definition | enormous : extremely large in size | Word mapped to its precise meaning |
| Synonym | smart : intelligent | Two equivalent adjectives |
| Definition | prodigy : exceptionally talented young person | Word mapped to its full meaning |

**The distinction matters because:** In definition analogies, one element is more precise or explanatory than the other. In synonym analogies, both elements are at the same level of specificity.

#### Types of Definition Relationships

**Direct Definition (word : its meaning)**
- Enormous : extremely large
- Diligent : showing careful and persistent effort
- Fragile : easily broken or damaged
- Novice : a person new to a field or activity

**Formal-to-Common Definition (technical term : everyday equivalent)**
- Physician : doctor
- Domicile : home
- Commence : begin
- Remuneration : payment

**Function-Based Definition (thing : what it does)**
- Thermometer : measures temperature
- Auditor : examines financial records
- Catalyst : speeds up a reaction
- Mediator : resolves disputes between parties

**Category Definition (specific : its category)**
- Sonnet : poem
- Oak : tree
- Sedan : vehicle
- Memorandum : document

#### Identifying the Strongest Definition Match

In CSE questions, you must select the *most precise* definition match. Consider:

**Question:** PEDAGOGUE : _____
- A) Person
- B) Teacher
- C) One who instructs in a formal educational setting
- D) School worker

**Analysis:**
- A) Too broad — everyone is a person
- B) Good — but "teacher" is more of a synonym than a definition
- C) Best — this is the precise definition of pedagogue
- D) Too vague — janitors and administrators also work in schools

**Rule:** The correct answer in a definition analogy is the one that *uniquely identifies* the word — not just a related concept or a broad category.

#### Easy Examples

| Word | Definition | Relationship |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Enormous | very large | word : meaning |
| Diligent | hardworking | word : meaning |
| Fragile | easily broken | word : meaning |
| Novice | beginner | word : meaning |
| Abundant | existing in large quantities | word : meaning |

#### Medium Examples

| Word | Definition | Relationship |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Pedagogue | teacher/instructor | formal term : common equivalent |
| Remuneration | payment for work | formal term : meaning |
| Jurisprudence | study of law | field name : what it studies |
| Philanthropy | desire to promote welfare of others | concept : its definition |
| Bureaucracy | system of government by officials | system : its definition |

#### Advanced Examples

| Word | Definition | Relationship |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Epistemology | study of knowledge and belief | branch : what it examines |
| Recalcitrant | stubbornly uncooperative | word : precise behavioral definition |
| Obsequious | excessively eager to please authority | word : nuanced definition |
| Perspicacious | having keen mental perception | word : cognitive definition |
| Magnanimous | generous in forgiving; free from pettiness | word : character definition |

### 4.3 Context-Based Meaning Relationships

A **context-based meaning relationship** exists when a word's meaning depends entirely on the situation, sentence, or domain in which it appears. These analogies test whether you can identify *which meaning* of a word is active based on contextual clues embedded in the analogy structure.

#### Why Context Changes Meaning

English is rich in polysemy — words that carry multiple legitimate meanings. The correct meaning is determined by context:

| Word | Context 1 | Meaning 1 | Context 2 | Meaning 2 |
|------|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Cold | weather | low temperature | personality | unfriendly, distant |
| Bright | light | emitting strong light | intelligence | quick to learn |
| Draft | writing | preliminary version | airflow | current of cool air |
| Issue | problem | matter requiring resolution | publication | edition of a magazine |
| Current | electricity | flow of charge | time | happening now |
| Volume | sound | loudness level | book | single book in a series |
| Charge | electricity | stored energy | accusation | formal claim of wrongdoing |
| Run | movement | move quickly on foot | business | manage or operate |

#### How Context Analogies Work in the CSE

The analogy structure itself provides the context. When you see:

**Cold : Unfriendly :: Bright : ?**

The first pair tells you: "cold" is being used in its *personality* sense (not temperature). So "bright" must also be used in a non-literal sense. The answer is "intelligent" (not "luminous" or "shiny").

**The context clue is the second word in the first pair.** It tells you which meaning of the first word is active, and you must apply the same logic to the second pair.

#### Identifying Contextual Meaning in Analogies

**Step 1:** Look at the second word in Pair 1 — it reveals which meaning of the first word is being used.

**Step 2:** Determine the *type* of meaning shift (literal → figurative, physical → abstract, etc.)

**Step 3:** Apply the same type of shift to the incomplete pair.

**Example walkthrough:**

**Question:** Run : Manage :: Break : _____

- "Run" has many meanings: move fast, operate, flow, etc.
- "Manage" tells us "run" means "to operate/manage" (as in "run a business")
- So we need: what does "break" mean in a non-literal sense?
- Answer: "Pause" (as in "take a break" = a pause in activity)
- NOT "shatter" (that's the literal/physical meaning)

#### Context Clue Types

| Clue Type | How It Works | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Synonym clue | Second word is a synonym of the intended meaning | cold : unfriendly (unfriendly = the personality meaning of cold) |
| Domain clue | Second word places the first in a specific field | interest : finance (interest = money earned on deposits) |
| Function clue | Second word describes what the first does in context | monitor : observe (monitor = to watch/observe, not the screen) |
| Result clue | Second word is the outcome of the first in context | sentence : punishment (sentence = a judicial punishment, not a grammar unit) |

#### Easy Examples

- **Cold : Unfriendly** → "cold" in personality context means distant/unfriendly
- **Bright : Intelligent** → "bright" in cognitive context means smart
- **Sharp : Clever** → "sharp" in mental context means quick-witted
- **Warm : Friendly** → "warm" in personality context means approachable
- **Hard : Difficult** → "hard" in task context means challenging (not physically solid)

#### Medium Examples

- **Run : Manage** → "run" in business context means to operate
- **Draft : Preliminary version** → "draft" in writing context means first attempt
- **Table : Postpone** → "table" in meeting context means to delay discussion
- **File : Organize documents** → "file" in office context means to store systematically
- **Address : Deal with** → "address" in problem-solving context means to handle

#### Advanced Examples

- **Tender : Submit formally** → "tender" in procurement context means to offer/submit (not soft/gentle)
- **Execute : Carry out a plan** → "execute" in project context means to implement (not to kill)
- **Sanction : Authorize** → "sanction" can mean both to approve AND to penalize — context determines which
- **Qualify : Limit or modify** → "qualify" in legal context means to add conditions (not to become eligible)
- **Disposition : Temperament** → "disposition" in personality context means character (not arrangement or legal transfer)

### 4.4 Vocabulary Association Relationships

A **vocabulary association relationship** connects a word to its natural domain, field, or conceptual cluster. Unlike definitions (which explain what a word means) or context meanings (which show how meaning shifts), associations test whether you understand *where a word belongs* in the landscape of knowledge.

#### How Associations Differ from Definitions

| Relationship | Example | Type |
|-------------|---------|------|
| Definition | Attorney : one who practices law | word : its meaning |
| Association | Attorney : courtroom | word : where it operates |
| Definition | Scalpel : surgical cutting instrument | word : its meaning |
| Association | Scalpel : surgery | word : its domain |

**The key difference:** Definitions tell you *what something is*. Associations tell you *where something belongs* or *what field it connects to*.

#### Types of Vocabulary Associations

**Professional Domain Associations (word : field)**

### 4.5 Multiple-Meaning Word Relationships

**Multiple-meaning words** (also called polysemous words or homonyms) are words that have two or more distinct meanings. In analogy questions, these test whether you can identify which meaning is active and find a parallel word that exhibits the same type of meaning duality.

#### Types of Multiple-Meaning Words

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Homonyms | Same spelling, same pronunciation, different meanings | bark (tree covering) / bark (dog sound) |
| Homographs | Same spelling, different pronunciation, different meanings | lead (to guide) / lead (metal) |
| Polysemy | One word with related but distinct meanings | head (body part) / head (leader) / head (top of page) |

#### How Multiple-Meaning Analogies Work

These analogies typically take one of two forms:

**Form 1: Word : One Meaning :: Word : Parallel Meaning**
- Bark : Tree covering :: Pen : Enclosure
- (Both words have a "less obvious" meaning — bark isn't just a dog sound, pen isn't just a writing tool)

**Form 2: Word (Meaning A) : Word (Meaning B) — parallel structure**
- Spring : Season :: Fall : Season (both are seasons AND have other meanings)
- Spring : Coil :: Fall : Descent (both words have a physical-action meaning)

#### Common Multiple-Meaning Words Tested in the CSE

| Word | Meaning 1 | Meaning 2 | Meaning 3 |
|------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Bank | Financial institution | River edge | To rely on |
| Bark | Dog sound | Tree covering | A type of boat |
| File | Document folder | Metal tool | To submit formally |
| Spring | Season | Coil/bounce | Water source |
| Wave | Water movement | Hand gesture | Physics: energy transfer |
| Current | Water flow | Happening now | Electrical flow |
| Charge | Electrical energy | Accusation | Fee/cost |
| Draft | Preliminary version | Air current | Military selection |
| Sentence | Grammar unit | Legal punishment | — |
| Resolution | Determination | Screen clarity | Formal decision |
| Interest | Curiosity | Financial return | Legal stake |
| Volume | Loudness | Book | Space occupied |
| Degree | Temperature unit | Academic qualification | Extent/level |
| Case | Container | Legal matter | Instance/example |
| Term | Word/phrase | Time period | Condition |

#### Identifying the Active Meaning

When you encounter a multiple-meaning word in an analogy, the *other word in the pair* tells you which meaning is active:

- **Bank : River** → "river" activates the "edge of water" meaning
- **Bank : Savings** → "savings" activates the "financial institution" meaning
- **Bank : Rely** → "rely" activates the "to depend on" meaning

**Rule:** The companion word in the pair is your context clue. It disambiguates the multiple-meaning word.

#### Easy Examples

- **Bark : Tree** → bark = outer covering of a tree (not dog sound)
- **Pen : Animal enclosure** → pen = fenced area (not writing instrument)
- **Bat : Flying mammal** → bat = animal (not sports equipment)
- **Ring : Sound** → ring = noise made by a bell (not jewelry)
- **Light : Not heavy** → light = lacking weight (not illumination)

#### Medium Examples

- **Draft : Air current** → draft = flow of air (not preliminary document)
- **File : Metal tool** → file = tool for smoothing (not document storage)
- **Spring : Water source** → spring = natural water outlet (not season or coil)
- **Current : Present time** → current = happening now (not water/electrical flow)
- **Volume : Space occupied** → volume = three-dimensional measurement (not loudness or book)

#### Advanced Examples

- **Tender : Legal currency** → tender = money that must be accepted as payment (not soft/gentle or to submit)
- **Sanction : Penalty** → sanction = punitive measure (not approval — the word means both)
- **Cleave : To split apart** → cleave = to divide (not to cling to — the word means both)
- **Oversight : Failure to notice** → oversight = an error of omission (not supervision/management)
- **Qualify : To limit or restrict** → qualify = to add conditions (not to become eligible)

### 4.6 Context Clue Analysis Techniques

When solving language and context analogies, you need systematic techniques for identifying which meaning or relationship is active. These techniques work regardless of vocabulary difficulty.

#### Technique 1: The Companion Word Test

The second word in Pair 1 is your primary context clue. Ask: "What does this companion word tell me about the first word's meaning?"

**Process:**
1. Read both words in Pair 1
2. Ask: "Which meaning of Word 1 connects logically to Word 2?"
3. That connection IS the relationship type
4. Apply the same relationship type to Pair 2

**Example:** File : Organize :: Address : ?
- "Organize" tells me "file" means "to arrange/store documents" (not the metal tool)
- The relationship is: word (action meaning) : what that action accomplishes
- "Address" in action meaning = "to deal with/handle"
- Answer: "Resolve" (addressing a problem = resolving it)

#### Technique 2: Domain Identification

Identify which professional or conceptual domain the pair operates in, then ensure your answer stays in a parallel domain.

**Process:**
1. What field does Pair 1 belong to? (legal, medical, financial, etc.)
2. Does Pair 2 need to be in the same field, or a parallel one?
3. Select the answer that maintains domain consistency

**Example:** Verdict : Courtroom :: Diagnosis : ?
- Pair 1 domain: legal/judicial
- Relationship: conclusion : setting where it's reached
- Pair 2 domain: medical
- Answer: "Clinic" (a diagnosis is reached in a clinic, as a verdict is reached in a courtroom)

#### Technique 3: Meaning-Shift Classification

Classify the type of meaning shift in Pair 1, then find the same shift type in the answer:

| Shift Type | Example | Pattern |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| Physical → Abstract | Cold : Unfriendly | temperature → personality |
| Literal → Figurative | Sharp : Clever | blade quality → mental quality |
| Object → Action | File : To organize | noun → verb meaning |
| General → Specific | Interest : Finance | broad concept → specific domain |
| Common → Technical | Bug : Software error | everyday word → technical meaning |

#### Technique 4: Elimination by Relationship Mismatch

If Pair 1 shows a definition relationship, eliminate all choices that create association, context, or multiple-meaning relationships (and vice versa).

**Example:** Physician : Doctor :: Attorney : ?
- Pair 1 relationship: formal term : common equivalent
- A) Court (association — ELIMINATE)
- B) Lawyer (common equivalent ✓)
- C) Legal (adjective — wrong part of speech — ELIMINATE)
- D) Judge (different profession — ELIMINATE)
- Answer: B) Lawyer

#### Technique 5: The Parallel Structure Test

Both pairs must have the same internal structure. If Pair 1 is "noun : noun," the answer must create "noun : noun." If Pair 1 is "adjective : definition," the answer must be a definition.

### 4.9 Practical Applications of Contextual Thinking

Understanding language, meaning, and context relationships is not just an exam skill — it directly applies to civil service work.

#### Government Memoranda and Correspondence

When reading official documents, civil servants encounter vocabulary that requires contextual interpretation:

- "The department will **table** the proposal at the next meeting" — Does this mean present it for discussion or postpone it? (In Philippine government context: present for discussion)
- "The **resolution** was passed unanimously" — This is a formal decision, not a personal determination
- "Please **address** the concerns raised in the audit" — This means deal with/respond to, not provide a location
- "The **brief** was submitted to the committee" — This is a short document, not an adjective meaning concise

#### Policy Interpretation

Misreading context in policy documents can lead to implementation errors:

- "Employees must **file** their leave applications" — submit formally (not organize into folders)
- "The **order** takes effect immediately" — directive/command (not sequence or purchase)
- "All **parties** must sign the agreement" — persons involved in the legal matter (not political groups or celebrations)

#### Report Writing

Choosing the right word in context improves clarity:

- Using "current" to mean "present/ongoing" vs. "flow of electricity" — context must make the intended meaning unambiguous
- Using "volume" to mean "amount" vs. "loudness" — the surrounding sentence must disambiguate
- Using "significant" to mean "statistically meaningful" vs. "important" — precision matters in government reports

#### Inter-Agency Communication

Different agencies may use the same word with different contextual meanings:

- "Compliance" in a regulatory agency = following rules; in a medical context = patient following treatment instructions
- "Protocol" in diplomacy = rules of ceremony; in IT = communication standard; in research = experimental procedure
- "Tender" in procurement = formal bid submission; in finance = legal currency; in everyday language = soft/gentle

### 4.10 Step-by-Step Analogy Solving Strategies

Use this systematic approach for every language, meaning, and context analogy question:

#### The DCME Method

| Step | Action | What You Do |
|------|--------|-------------|
| **D** — Determine relationship type | Read Pair 1 | Ask: "Is this definition, context, meaning-shift, or association?" |
| **C** — Clarify the specific connection | Form a bridge | "A is defined as B" / "A in [context] means B" / "A belongs to field B" |
| **M** — Match to answer choices | Test each option | Insert each choice into your bridge statement |
| **E** — Eliminate mismatches | Remove wrong types | If Pair 1 is definition, reject associations and context-shifts |

#### Detailed Solving Process

**Step 1: Classify the relationship type (3-5 seconds)**

Read Pair 1 and immediately ask: "What KIND of relationship is this?"

- Does Word 2 *define* Word 1? → Definition relationship
- Does Word 2 reveal *which meaning* of Word 1 is active? → Context/multiple-meaning relationship
- Does Word 2 name the *field or domain* where Word 1 belongs? → Association relationship
- Is Word 1 being used in a *non-literal* way, with Word 2 showing the figurative meaning? → Context-based meaning

**Step 2: Form your bridge statement (2-3 seconds)**

- Definition: "[Word 1] means [Word 2]" or "[Word 1] is defined as [Word 2]"
- Context: "[Word 1] in [domain] context means [Word 2]"
- Association: "[Word 1] belongs to the field of [Word 2]"
- Multiple meaning: "[Word 1] can mean [Word 2] in one context"

**Step 3: Test each answer choice (5-8 seconds)**

Apply your bridge statement to each option. The correct answer fits perfectly; wrong answers require changing the bridge.

**Step 4: Verify consistency (2-3 seconds)**

- Same relationship type in both pairs?
- Same level of specificity?
- Same part of speech pattern?
- Same domain type (if applicable)?

#### Time Management

- Total time per question: 12-18 seconds maximum
- If you cannot classify the relationship within 5 seconds, flag and return later
- If two choices seem valid, pick the one with the more precise/parallel relationship
- Never spend more than 25 seconds on a single item

#### Worked Example

**Question:** Draft : Preliminary version :: Tender : ?

**Step 1 — Classify:** "Draft" has multiple meanings. "Preliminary version" tells me which meaning is active (the writing/document meaning). This is a multiple-meaning word mapped to one specific definition.

**Step 2 — Bridge:** "[Word] in [specific context] is defined as [meaning]"
- "Draft in writing context is defined as preliminary version"

**Step 3 — Test choices:**
- A) Soft → "Tender in texture context is defined as soft" ✓ (valid parallel)
- B) Money → "Tender in finance context is defined as money" ✓ (valid parallel — legal tender)
- C) Offer → "Tender in procurement context is defined as offer/bid" ✓ (valid parallel)
- D) Gentle → "Tender in personality context is defined as gentle" ✓ (valid parallel)

**Step 4 — Verify:** Multiple valid answers? Look for the MOST parallel structure:
- "Draft" is a noun with a specific technical meaning in a professional context
- "Tender" as "formal offer/bid" is also a noun with a specific technical meaning in a professional context
- Answer: C) Offer (procurement context parallels writing context — both professional/formal)

### 4.11 Common Errors in Language and Context Analogies

#### Error 1: Selecting the Most Common Meaning

**Wrong thinking:** "Bank always means a financial institution"
**Right thinking:** "What does the companion word tell me about which meaning is active?"

If the analogy says "Bank : Edge," the answer must relate to the river-bank meaning, not the financial meaning. The most common meaning is often the trap.

#### Error 2: Confusing Definitions with Associations

**Wrong:** Treating "Scalpel : Surgery" as a definition (a scalpel is NOT defined as surgery)
**Right:** Recognizing this as an association (a scalpel is USED IN surgery)

Definitions answer "What is it?" Associations answer "Where does it belong?" or "What is it connected to?"

#### Error 3: Ignoring the Relationship Type

**Wrong:** Seeing "Physician : Doctor" and selecting "Hospital : Building" (true statement but wrong relationship type)
**Right:** Selecting "Attorney : Lawyer" (same relationship: formal term : common equivalent)

Both pairs must share the SAME TYPE of relationship, not just both be "true facts."

#### Error 4: Falling for Surface-Level Connections

**Wrong:** "Current : Water" because currents exist in water
**Right:** Checking whether the analogy requires "current = water flow" or "current = present time" or "current = electrical flow"

The companion word disambiguates. Without checking it, you're guessing.

#### Error 5: Mixing Literal and Figurative Meanings

**Wrong:** If Pair 1 uses a figurative meaning (Cold : Unfriendly), selecting a literal meaning for Pair 2
**Right:** Both pairs must use the same TYPE of meaning (both figurative, or both literal)

If the first pair shows a physical-to-abstract shift, the answer pair must also show a physical-to-abstract shift.

#### Error 6: Selecting Partial Matches

CSE questions often include choices that are *related* to the correct answer but don't match the precise relationship:

**Question:** Verdict : Courtroom :: Diagnosis : ?
- Trap: "Doctor" (related to diagnosis, but the relationship is conclusion : setting, not conclusion : person)
- Correct: "Clinic" (diagnosis is reached in a clinic, as verdict is reached in a courtroom)

#### Error 7: Not Verifying Parallel Structure

Both pairs must have the same grammatical and logical structure:
- If Pair 1 is "noun : noun," Pair 2 must be "noun : noun"
- If Pair 1 is "action word : its domain," Pair 2 must be "action word : its domain"
- If Pair 1 is "word : its non-literal meaning," Pair 2 must follow the same pattern

#### Decoding Strategy for Unknown Words

When you encounter a word you've never seen:

1. **Check prefixes:** un-, dis-, re-, pre-, post-, anti-, pro-, bene-, mal-
2. **Check roots:** -dict (say), -scrib (write), -port (carry), -ject (throw), -spect (look)
3. **Check suffixes:** -tion (action/state), -ous (full of), -ive (tending to), -ment (result of)
4. **Use the companion word:** Even if you don't know Word 1, Word 2 tells you the relationship type
5. **Eliminate confidently wrong answers:** You may not know the right answer, but you can often identify 2-3 wrong ones

**Instructions:** Identify the relationship type (definition, context-based meaning, vocabulary association, or multiple-meaning), then choose the best answer.

**1.** Physician : Doctor :: Domicile : _____

- **Answer:** B) Home
- **Explanation:** Physician is the formal term for doctor. Domicile is the formal term for home. Both pairs show formal-to-common definition relationships.

- **Answer:** C) Intelligent
- **Explanation:** Cold in personality context means unfriendly. Bright in cognitive context means intelligent. Both pairs show physical-to-abstract meaning shifts.

- **Answer:** B) Courtroom
- **Explanation:** A scalpel is a tool used in surgery. A gavel is a tool used in a courtroom. Both pairs show tool-to-setting associations.

**4.** Draft : Preliminary version :: Current : _____

- **Answer:** B) Happening now
- **Explanation:** Draft has multiple meanings; "preliminary version" identifies the writing meaning. Current has multiple meanings; "happening now" identifies the temporal meaning. Both map a multiple-meaning word to one specific definition. The parallel is strongest with "happening now" because both selected meanings are the most abstract/non-physical option.

**5.** Budget : Finance :: Curriculum : _____
- A) School  B) Education  C) Teacher  D) Books
- **Answer:** B) Education

**7.** Remuneration : Payment :: Commence : _____

- **Answer:** B) Begin

**13.** Minute : Official meeting record :: Brief : _____

## Memory Aids

### The "WHAT KIND?" Quick Check

Before looking at answer choices, ask ONE question about Pair 1:
- "Does Word 2 DEFINE Word 1?" → Definition relationship
- "Does Word 2 show WHERE Word 1 belongs?" → Association relationship
- "Does Word 2 reveal WHICH MEANING of Word 1 is active?" → Context/multiple-meaning relationship

This single classification eliminates most wrong answers instantly.

### The "Companion Word" Rule

**"The second word is the key that unlocks the first word's meaning."**

- Bank : River → "river" unlocks the water-edge meaning
- Bank : Savings → "savings" unlocks the financial meaning
- Bank : Rely → "rely" unlocks the trust/depend meaning

Always read Word 2 FIRST — it tells you everything about how Word 1 is being used.

### The Domain Shortcut

If you see a professional/technical word in Pair 1, the answer likely involves a parallel professional/technical context:
- Legal term : legal definition → Medical term : medical definition
- Finance word : finance meaning → Accounting word : accounting meaning
- Government term : government context → Education term : education context

### The "Two Hats" Memory Trick for Multiple-Meaning Words

Think of multiple-meaning words as wearing different hats:
- "Draft" wears a WRITER hat (preliminary version), an AIRFLOW hat (breeze), and a MILITARY hat (conscription)
- The companion word tells you WHICH HAT the word is wearing in this analogy
- Your answer must put its word in the SAME TYPE of hat
