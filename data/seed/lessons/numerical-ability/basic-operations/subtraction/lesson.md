# Subtraction

## Explanations

### Introduction

**Subtraction** is the arithmetic operation of finding the difference between two quantities — removing one amount from another. It is the inverse of addition: if 5 + 3 = 8, then 8 − 3 = 5. In the Philippine Civil Service Examination (CSE), subtraction appears in direct computation items, word problems involving decrease or comparison, budgeting scenarios, payroll deductions, and as a sub-step in multi-operation Numerical Ability questions.

This subtopic covers four critical areas:
- **Subtraction of Whole Numbers** — single-digit through multi-digit subtraction with borrowing/regrouping
- **Subtraction of Integers** — handling positive and negative numbers using sign rules
- **Subtraction of Decimals** — aligning decimal points and computing differences accurately
- **Subtraction of Fractions** — finding common denominators and simplifying results

Mastering subtraction is essential for any government employee. Calculating budget variances, determining remaining inventory, computing payroll deductions, measuring decreases in population or revenue, and reconciling financial records all require accurate subtraction. The CSE tests subtraction because errors in this operation lead directly to audit findings, incorrect disbursements, and flawed statistical reports.

### Why Subtraction Is Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Exam tests subtraction because:
- Government employees compute budget variances daily — comparing actual expenditures against allocations requires subtraction
- Payroll processing involves deducting taxes, insurance premiums, and loan payments from gross pay
- Inventory management requires subtracting issued items from available stock
- Statistical reports track decreases in population, revenue, or service delivery metrics
- Financial reconciliation depends on finding discrepancies between recorded and actual amounts
- Subtraction errors in official documents can result in overpayments, underpayments, or audit exceptions
- The ability to subtract quickly and accurately under time pressure indicates the numerical fluency required for administrative work

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Incorrect borrowing (regrouping)** — forgetting to reduce the digit you borrowed from, or borrowing across multiple zeros incorrectly
2. **Subtracting the smaller digit from the larger regardless of position** — computing 43 − 27 as 24 (subtracting 3 from 7 instead of borrowing)
3. **Sign confusion with integers** — not converting subtraction of a negative to addition
4. **Misaligning decimal points** — subtracting 5.6 − 0.34 without proper alignment
5. **Subtracting denominators in fractions** — computing 5/8 − 3/8 as 2/0 or mishandling unlike denominators
6. **Failing to simplify fraction answers** — leaving 4/6 instead of reducing to 2/3
7. **Borrowing errors across zeros** — 1,000 − 567 is a classic error trap
8. **Confusing subtraction direction** — computing 34 − 56 when the problem states 56 − 34

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Subtract whole numbers of any size accurately, including problems requiring multiple regroupings and borrowing across zeros
- Apply integer subtraction rules correctly by converting subtraction to addition of the opposite
- Subtract decimals with proper place-value alignment
- Subtract fractions with like and unlike denominators, including mixed numbers
- Solve CSE-style word problems involving subtraction across all number types
- Use estimation and mental math strategies to verify answers quickly
- Identify and avoid common subtraction errors under exam conditions

### 4.1 What Is Subtraction?

**Subtraction** is the mathematical operation of taking one number away from another to find the **difference**. The number you start with is the **minuend**, the number you take away is the **subtrahend**, and the result is the **difference**.

```
Minuend − Subtrahend = Difference
   8    −     3      =     5
```

The symbol **−** (minus sign) indicates subtraction. When you see "8 − 3," you read it as "eight minus three" or "the difference of eight and three."

#### Key Relationships

**Subtraction is the inverse of addition:**
```
If  a + b = c,  then  c − b = a  and  c − a = b
If  5 + 3 = 8,  then  8 − 3 = 5  and  8 − 5 = 3
```

This inverse relationship is your primary tool for checking subtraction answers: add your difference to the subtrahend — you should get the minuend.

#### Properties of Subtraction

Unlike addition, subtraction is **NOT commutative** and **NOT associative**:

```
8 − 3 ≠ 3 − 8        (NOT commutative)
(10 − 5) − 2 ≠ 10 − (5 − 2)   (NOT associative)
    5 − 2 ≠ 10 − 3
      3   ≠   7
```

**Identity Property:** Subtracting zero from any number gives that number.
```
a − 0 = a
45 − 0 = 45
```

**Self-Subtraction:** Any number minus itself equals zero.
```
a − a = 0
99 − 99 = 0
```

#### Real-Life Applications of Subtraction

| Context | Example |
|---------|---------|
| Budgeting | Remaining budget = allocation − expenditures |
| Payroll | Net pay = gross pay − total deductions |
| Inventory | Remaining stock = beginning inventory − items issued |
| Statistics | Population decrease = previous count − current count |
| Engineering | Clearance = total space − object dimension |
| Accounting | Deficit = revenue − expenses (when expenses exceed revenue) |
| Time | Duration = end time − start time |
| Temperature | Change = final temperature − initial temperature |

### 4.2 Subtraction of Whole Numbers

Whole numbers are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ... (non-negative integers with no fractional or decimal part). Subtracting whole numbers is the most basic form of subtraction and the foundation for all other types.

#### Single-Digit Subtraction

Single-digit subtraction involves a minuend from 0 to 18 and a subtrahend from 0 to 9 (since these derive from addition facts). These are the building blocks.

**Key facts to know instantly:**
```
10 − 1 = 9    10 − 2 = 8    10 − 3 = 7    10 − 4 = 6    10 − 5 = 5
18 − 9 = 9    16 − 8 = 8    14 − 7 = 7    12 − 6 = 6
17 − 9 = 8    15 − 8 = 7    13 − 7 = 6    11 − 6 = 5
```

These "complements" are essential for borrowing in multi-digit subtraction.

#### Multi-Digit Subtraction Without Borrowing

When every digit in the minuend is greater than or equal to the corresponding digit in the subtrahend, subtraction is straightforward:

**Example:** 876 − 432

```
  876
− 432
-----
  444
```

Step-by-step:
- Ones: 6 − 2 = 4
- Tens: 7 − 3 = 4
- Hundreds: 8 − 4 = 4

**Result: 444**

#### Multi-Digit Subtraction With Borrowing (Regrouping)

When a digit in the minuend is smaller than the corresponding digit in the subtrahend, you must **borrow** (regroup) from the next higher place value.

**Example:** 742 − 368

```
  6 13 12
  7  4  2
− 3  6  8
---------
  3  7  4
```

Step-by-step:
- Ones: 2 < 8, so borrow 1 ten from the tens place. The 4 becomes 3, and the 2 becomes 12. Now: 12 − 8 = 4
- Tens: 3 < 6, so borrow 1 hundred from the hundreds place. The 7 becomes 6, and the 3 becomes 13. Now: 13 − 6 = 7
- Hundreds: 6 − 3 = 3

**Result: 374**

**Verification:** 374 + 368 = 742 ✓

#### Why Borrowing Works

Borrowing is based on the place value system. When you borrow 1 from the tens column, you are taking 1 ten (which equals 10 ones) and adding it to the ones column. The total value of the number does not change — you are simply redistributing it.

```
742 = 700 + 40 + 2
    = 700 + 30 + 12    (borrow 1 ten → ones)
    = 600 + 130 + 12   (borrow 1 hundred → tens)
```

#### Borrowing Across Zeros

This is the most error-prone subtraction scenario. When you need to borrow but the next column is 0, you must borrow from the next non-zero column, cascading through the zeros.

**Example:** 1,000 − 567

```
  0  9  9 10
  1  0  0  0
−    5  6  7
-----------
     4  3  3
```

Step-by-step:
- Ones: 0 < 7. Need to borrow from tens, but tens is 0.
- Borrow from hundreds, but hundreds is 0.
- Borrow from thousands: 1 thousand becomes 0 thousands, and hundreds becomes 10.
- Now borrow from hundreds: 10 hundreds becomes 9 hundreds, and tens becomes 10.
- Now borrow from tens: 10 tens becomes 9 tens, and ones becomes 10.
- Ones: 10 − 7 = 3
- Tens: 9 − 6 = 3
- Hundreds: 9 − 5 = 4
- Thousands: 0

**Result: 433**

**Verification:** 433 + 567 = 1,000 ✓

**Shortcut for subtracting from powers of 10:**
For 1,000 − 567: subtract each digit from 9, except the last digit which you subtract from 10.
- 9 − 5 = 4
- 9 − 6 = 3
- 10 − 7 = 3
- **Answer: 433**

This shortcut works for 10 − n, 100 − n, 1000 − n, etc.

#### Subtracting Large Numbers

**Example:** 85,204 − 37,568

```
  7 14 11 9 14
  8  5  2  0  4
− 3  7  5  6  8
--------------
  4  7  6  3  6
```

Step-by-step:
- Ones: 4 < 8. Borrow from tens, but tens is 0. Borrow from hundreds: 2 becomes 1, tens becomes 10. Now borrow from tens: 10 becomes 9, ones becomes 14. 14 − 8 = 6
- Tens: 9 − 6 = 3
- Hundreds: 1 < 5. Borrow from thousands: 5 becomes 4, hundreds becomes 11. 11 − 5 = 6
- Thousands: 4 < 7. Borrow from ten-thousands: 8 becomes 7, thousands becomes 14. 14 − 7 = 7
- Ten-thousands: 7 − 3 = 4

**Result: 47,636**

#### Common Borrowing Mistakes

| Mistake | Example | Correct |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Forgetting to reduce after borrowing | Borrow from 5 but leave it as 5 | Must reduce to 4 |
| Subtracting smaller from larger regardless of position | 43 − 27 → "7 − 3 = 4, 4 − 2 = 2" → 24 | Must borrow: 13 − 7 = 6, 3 − 2 = 1 → 16 |
| Incorrect cascade through zeros | 500 − 234 → getting 334 | Correct: 266 |
| Borrowing but not adding 10 | Borrow from tens, ones stays same | Ones gets +10 |

### 4.3 Word Problems Involving Whole Number Subtraction

Word problems test whether you can identify subtraction situations in real-world contexts. The CSE heavily favors word problems because government work involves interpreting written data.

#### Keywords That Signal Subtraction

| Keyword/Phrase | Example |
|---------------|---------|
| difference | What is the difference between 500 and 320? |
| less than | 45 is how much less than 78? |
| decreased by | The budget decreased by ₱100,000. |
| reduced by | Staff was reduced by 15 employees. |
| fewer | There are 23 fewer students this year. |
| remain / remaining | How many items remain? |
| left | How much money is left? |
| take away | Take away 30 from 95. |
| minus | 800 minus 350 |
| how much more | How much more does A earn than B? |
| change (money) | What change will you receive? |
| deducted | ₱500 was deducted from the salary. |
| lost | The company lost ₱2 million. |
| spent | She spent ₱1,500 from her ₱5,000. |

#### Strategy for Solving Word Problems

1. **Read** the entire problem carefully — identify what is being taken away from what
2. **Identify** the minuend (starting amount) and subtrahend (amount removed)
3. **Determine** the operation — look for decrease/removal keywords
4. **Set up** the subtraction correctly (larger number minus smaller number for positive results)
5. **Compute** carefully with proper borrowing
6. **Verify** — does the answer make sense? Is it smaller than the starting amount?

#### Easy Example

> A government office had 120 reams of bond paper at the start of the month. During the month, 47 reams were used. How many reams remain?

**Solution:** 120 − 47 = 73 reams

**Verification:** 73 + 47 = 120 ✓

#### Medium Example

> A municipality had a population of 45,678 in 2020. By 2023, the population decreased to 42,345. What was the population decrease?

**Solution:**
```
  45,678
− 42,345
--------
   3,333
```

**Population decrease: 3,333**

#### Hard Example

> A government agency's annual budget is ₱15,000,000. The following amounts have been obligated: Q1 — ₱4,234,567; Q2 — ₱3,876,543; Q3 — ₱4,012,890. How much of the budget remains unobligated?

**Solution:**
Step 1: Total obligated = 4,234,567 + 3,876,543 + 4,012,890 = 12,124,000
Step 2: Remaining = 15,000,000 − 12,124,000 = 2,876,000

**Remaining budget: ₱2,876,000**

### 4.4 Subtraction of Integers

Integers include all positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero: {..., -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...}. Integer subtraction introduces a critical concept: **subtracting a number is the same as adding its opposite (additive inverse).**

#### The Fundamental Rule of Integer Subtraction

```
a − b = a + (−b)
```

**To subtract an integer, add its opposite.** This single rule converts every subtraction problem into an addition problem, which you already know how to solve.

**Examples:**
```
8 − 5 = 8 + (−5) = 3         (subtract positive → add negative)
8 − (−5) = 8 + (+5) = 13     (subtract negative → add positive)
−8 − 5 = −8 + (−5) = −13     (subtract positive → add negative)
−8 − (−5) = −8 + (+5) = −3   (subtract negative → add positive)
```

#### Understanding the Additive Inverse

Every number has an **additive inverse** (opposite) — the number that, when added to it, gives zero.

```
Opposite of +5 is −5    because (+5) + (−5) = 0
Opposite of −3 is +3    because (−3) + (+3) = 0
Opposite of 0 is 0      because 0 + 0 = 0
```

When you subtract a number, you add its opposite:
- Subtracting +5 means adding −5 (moving left on the number line)
- Subtracting −5 means adding +5 (moving right on the number line)

#### Case 1: Positive − Positive

```
12 − 7 = 12 + (−7) = 5
```
This is standard subtraction. Convert to addition: start at 12, add −7 (move 7 left). Land on 5.

If the subtrahend is larger:
```
7 − 12 = 7 + (−12) = −5
```
Start at 7, add −12 (move 12 left). Land on −5.

#### Case 2: Positive − Negative (Subtracting a Negative)

```
8 − (−3) = 8 + (+3) = 11
```
Subtracting a negative is the same as adding a positive. The two negatives "cancel out."

**Think of it as:** Removing a debt is the same as gaining money.

#### Case 3: Negative − Positive

```
−6 − 4 = −6 + (−4) = −10
```
Start at −6, add −4 (move 4 more to the left). Land on −10.

#### Case 4: Negative − Negative

```
−6 − (−4) = −6 + (+4) = −2
```
Start at −6, add +4 (move 4 to the right). Land on −2.

```
−3 − (−8) = −3 + (+8) = +5
```
Start at −3, add +8 (move 8 to the right). Land on +5.

#### Number Line Visualization

**Example:** 3 − 7

```
Start at 3. Subtract 7 means add (−7): move 7 units left.
←←←←←←←
-4 -3 -2 -1  0  1  2  3
                        ^start
^end
```
**Result: −4**

**Example:** −2 − (−6)

```
Convert: −2 + 6. Start at −2, move 6 units right.
→→→→→→
-2 -1  0  1  2  3  4
 ^start              ^end
```
**Result: 4**

### 4.5 Integer Subtraction Strategies

#### The Keep-Change-Change (KCC) Rule

This is the most reliable method for integer subtraction:

1. **Keep** the first number (minuend) as is
2. **Change** the subtraction sign to addition
3. **Change** the sign of the second number (subtrahend) to its opposite

Then apply integer addition rules.

**Examples using KCC:**

```
(+9) − (+4)  →  (+9) + (−4) = +5
(+9) − (−4)  →  (+9) + (+4) = +13
(−9) − (+4)  →  (−9) + (−4) = −13
(−9) − (−4)  →  (−9) + (+4) = −5
```

#### Quick Sign Analysis

After applying KCC, you have an addition problem. Use these rules:
- **Same signs:** Add absolute values, keep the shared sign
- **Different signs:** Subtract absolute values, take the sign of the larger absolute value

**Example:** (−15) − (+8)
- KCC: (−15) + (−8)
- Same signs (both negative): |15| + |8| = 23, keep negative
- **Answer: −23**

**Example:** (−15) − (−8)
- KCC: (−15) + (+8)
- Different signs: |15| − |8| = 7, larger absolute value is 15 (negative)
- **Answer: −7**

#### Mental Math for Integer Subtraction

**Shortcut 1: Subtracting a negative = adding a positive**
Whenever you see "minus negative," immediately think "plus positive."
```
20 − (−5) = 20 + 5 = 25
−7 − (−10) = −7 + 10 = 3
```

**Shortcut 2: Sign pattern recognition**
```
(+) − (+) → could be positive or negative (depends on magnitudes)
(+) − (−) → always positive (adding to a positive)
(−) − (+) → always negative (subtracting from a negative)
(−) − (−) → could be positive or negative (depends on magnitudes)
```

**Shortcut 3: Distance on number line**
The difference between two numbers equals the distance between them on the number line, with sign indicating direction:
```
5 − (−3) = 8    (distance from −3 to 5 is 8 units)
−3 − 5 = −8     (distance from 5 to −3 is 8 units, going left)
```

#### Elimination Strategy for Multiple Choice

On the CSE, quickly determine the sign of the answer to eliminate choices:
- Positive minus a smaller positive → positive answer
- Positive minus a larger positive → negative answer
- Any number minus a negative → result is larger than the first number
- Negative minus a positive → result is more negative than the first number

**Example:** What is (−23) − (+17)?
- Negative minus positive → answer must be negative and more negative than −23
- Answer must be less than −23
- If choices are: a) −6  b) 6  c) −40  d) 40
- Only c) −40 is more negative than −23. **Answer: c) −40**

#### Real-Life Integer Subtraction

| Scenario | Computation |
|----------|-------------|
| Temperature drop | From 5°C to −3°C: change = −3 − 5 = −8°C |
| Elevation difference | Mountain peak 2,500m, ocean floor −500m: difference = 2,500 − (−500) = 3,000m |
| Financial loss vs gain | This year: −₱20,000 loss. Last year: +₱15,000 profit. Change = −20,000 − 15,000 = −₱35,000 |
| Time zones | Manila (+8) vs New York (−5): difference = 8 − (−5) = 13 hours |

### 4.6 Subtraction of Decimals

Subtracting decimals follows the same process as whole number subtraction, with the critical rule: **align the decimal points vertically before subtracting.**

#### The Golden Rule: Align Decimal Points

Before subtracting, write the numbers so that decimal points are directly above each other. Fill empty places with trailing zeros.

**Example:** 5.6 − 0.34

```
WRONG:          CORRECT:
  5.6             5.60
− 0.34          − 0.34
------          ------
  5.26            5.26
```

In this case both happen to give 5.26, but consider: 5.6 − 3.4

```
WRONG:          CORRECT:
  5.6             5.6
− 3.4           − 3.4
-----           -----
  2.2             2.2
```

The alignment matters most when decimal places differ significantly.

#### Step-by-Step Method

1. Write numbers vertically with decimal points aligned
2. Fill empty decimal places with trailing zeros
3. Subtract column by column from right to left, borrowing as needed
4. Place the decimal point in the answer directly below the other decimal points

#### Easy Example

**Subtract: 8.5 − 3.2**

```
  8.5
− 3.2
-----
  5.3
```
- Tenths: 5 − 2 = 3
- Ones: 8 − 3 = 5

**Result: 5.3**

#### Medium Example

**Subtract: 24.03 − 8.567**

```
  2 3  9 12 10
  2 4 . 0  3  0
−  8 . 5  6  7
--------------
  1 5 . 4  6  3
```

Step-by-step:
- Thousandths: 0 < 7. Borrow from hundredths: 3 becomes 2, thousandths becomes 10. 10 − 7 = 3
- Hundredths: 2 < 6. Borrow from tenths: 0 becomes... need to borrow from ones first.
- Borrow from ones: 4 becomes 3, tenths becomes 10. Now borrow from tenths: 10 becomes 9, hundredths becomes 12. 12 − 6 = 6
- Tenths: 9 − 5 = 4
- Ones: 3 < 8. Borrow from tens: 2 becomes 1, ones becomes 13. 13 − 8 = 5
- Tens: 1 − 0 = 1

**Result: 15.463**

**Verification:** 15.463 + 8.567 = 24.030 = 24.03 ✓

#### Hard Example

**Subtract: 10,000.00 − 3,456.78**

```
   9  9  9  9  9 10
  10  0  0  0. 0  0
−  3  4  5  6. 7  8
-----------------
   6  5  4  3. 2  2
```

Using the power-of-10 shortcut (subtract each digit from 9, last from 10):
- 9 − 3 = 6
- 9 − 4 = 5
- 9 − 5 = 4
- 9 − 6 = 3
- 9 − 7 = 2
- 10 − 8 = 2

**Result: 6,543.22**

#### Trailing Zeros Rule

Adding trailing zeros after the last decimal digit does NOT change the value:
- 5.6 = 5.60 = 5.600
- 12 = 12.0 = 12.00

Use this to make both numbers have the same number of decimal places before subtracting.

### 4.7 Decimal Subtraction in Real Life

Decimal subtraction appears constantly in government and professional contexts.

#### Money Computations (Payroll Deductions)

**Example:** An employee's gross pay is ₱25,450.00. Deductions are: Tax ₱3,567.50, PhilHealth ₱450.00, Pag-IBIG ₱200.00, GSIS ₱2,290.50. What is the net pay?

Step 1: Total deductions = 3,567.50 + 450.00 + 200.00 + 2,290.50 = 6,508.00
Step 2: Net pay = 25,450.00 − 6,508.00 = 18,942.00

**Net Pay: ₱18,942.00**

−   743.75

    256.25

**Change: ₱256.25**

### 4.8 Subtraction of Fractions

Subtracting fractions follows the same logic as adding fractions, but you subtract numerators instead of adding them. The denominator rules are identical: you need a common denominator before you can subtract.

#### Subtracting Fractions with Like Denominators

When fractions have the **same denominator**, subtract the numerators and keep the denominator unchanged.

**Formula:**
```
a/d − b/d = (a − b)/d
```

**Example:** 5/8 − 3/8

```
5/8 − 3/8 = (5 − 3)/8 = 2/8 = 1/4
```

**Example:** 7/9 − 2/9

```
7/9 − 2/9 = (7 − 2)/9 = 5/9
```

**Critical reminder:** NEVER subtract the denominators. The denominator tells you the size of the parts — it stays the same.

#### Subtracting Fractions with Unlike Denominators

When denominators are different, find the **Least Common Denominator (LCD)** first.

**Step-by-step method:**
1. Find the LCD of the denominators
2. Convert each fraction to an equivalent fraction with the LCD
3. Subtract the numerators
4. Keep the LCD as the denominator
5. Simplify if possible

**Example:** 3/4 − 1/3

Step 1: LCD of 4 and 3 = 12
Step 2: Convert:
- 3/4 = 9/12 (multiply top and bottom by 3)
- 1/3 = 4/12 (multiply top and bottom by 4)
Step 3: Subtract numerators: 9 − 4 = 5
Step 4: Result: 5/12

**Answer: 5/12**

**Example:** 5/6 − 2/9

Step 1: LCD of 6 and 9 = 18
Step 2: Convert:
- 5/6 = 15/18 (multiply by 3)
- 2/9 = 4/18 (multiply by 2)
Step 3: 15 − 4 = 11
Step 4: Result: 11/18

**Answer: 11/18**

**Example:** 7/8 − 1/6

Step 1: LCD of 8 and 6 = 24
Step 2: Convert:
- 7/8 = 21/24 (multiply by 3)
- 1/6 = 4/24 (multiply by 4)
Step 3: 21 − 4 = 17
Step 4: Result: 17/24

**Answer: 17/24**

#### Finding the LCD (Review)

**Method 1: List multiples**
- Multiples of 6: 6, 12, **18**, 24, 30...
- Multiples of 9: 9, **18**, 27, 36...
- LCD = 18

**Method 2: Prime factorization**
- 8 = 2³
- 6 = 2 × 3
- LCD = 2³ × 3 = 24

**Method 3: Quick check** — Is the larger denominator a multiple of the smaller? If yes, LCD = larger denominator.
- 12 and 4: 12 is a multiple of 4, so LCD = 12
- 15 and 5: 15 is a multiple of 5, so LCD = 15

#### Subtracting Mixed Numbers

A **mixed number** has a whole number part and a fraction part (e.g., 5¾).

**Method 1: Subtract whole parts and fraction parts separately**

**Example:** 5¾ − 2¼

Step 1: Subtract whole numbers: 5 − 2 = 3
Step 2: Subtract fractions: 3/4 − 1/4 = 2/4 = 1/2
Step 3: Combine: 3½

**Answer: 3½**

**Method 2: Borrowing with mixed numbers**

When the fraction part of the subtrahend is larger than the fraction part of the minuend, you must borrow 1 from the whole number.

**Example:** 5⅓ − 2¾

Step 1: Compare fractions: 1/3 vs 3/4. Find LCD = 12.
- 1/3 = 4/12
- 3/4 = 9/12
- 4/12 < 9/12, so we need to borrow.

Step 2: Borrow 1 from the whole number 5:
- 5 4/12 becomes 4 16/12 (because 1 = 12/12, and 12/12 + 4/12 = 16/12)

Step 3: Now subtract:
- Whole numbers: 4 − 2 = 2
- Fractions: 16/12 − 9/12 = 7/12

**Answer: 2 7/12**

**Method 3: Convert to improper fractions**

**Example:** 5⅓ − 2¾

Step 1: Convert to improper fractions:
- 5⅓ = (5×3 + 1)/3 = 16/3
- 2¾ = (2×4 + 3)/4 = 11/4

Step 2: Find LCD = 12
- 16/3 = 64/12
- 11/4 = 33/12

Step 3: Subtract: 64/12 − 33/12 = 31/12

Step 4: Convert back: 31 ÷ 12 = 2 remainder 7 → 2 7/12

**Answer: 2 7/12**

#### Simplifying Fraction Answers

Always reduce your answer to lowest terms:

```
6/8 → GCD(6,8) = 2 → 3/4
10/15 → GCD(10,15) = 5 → 2/3
4/6 → GCD(4,6) = 2 → 2/3
9/12 → GCD(9,12) = 3 → 3/4
```

### 4.9 Fraction Subtraction Strategies

#### Cross-Multiplication Shortcut (Two Fractions Only)

For subtracting exactly two fractions:

```
a/b − c/d = (a×d − b×c) / (b×d)
```

**Example:** 5/6 − 2/5

```
= (5×5 − 6×2) / (6×5)
= (25 − 12) / 30
= 13/30
```

#### Butterfly Method (Visual Shortcut)

```
  5     2
  -  −  -
  6     5

Cross-multiply:
5 × 5 = 25 (left wing)
6 × 2 = 12 (right wing)

Numerator: 25 − 12 = 13
Denominator: 6 × 5 = 30

Answer: 13/30
```

#### LCD Shortcut When One Denominator Divides the Other

When one denominator is a multiple of the other, the LCD is the larger denominator:

**Example:** 7/12 − 1/4

- 12 is a multiple of 4, so LCD = 12
- Convert only the second fraction: 1/4 = 3/12
- Subtract: 7/12 − 3/12 = 4/12 = 1/3

#### Borrowing in Mixed Numbers — Quick Method

When you need to borrow:
1. Reduce the whole number by 1
2. Add the denominator to the numerator of the fraction part

**Example:** 7 2/5 − 3 4/5
- 2/5 < 4/5, need to borrow
- 7 2/5 becomes 6 7/5 (added 5 to the numerator 2, reduced 7 to 6)
- Now: 6 7/5 − 3 4/5 = 3 3/5

#### CSE-Style Fraction Subtraction Examples

**Easy:** 7/8 − 3/8 = 4/8 = 1/2

**Medium:** 5/6 − 3/8
- LCD = 24
- 5/6 = 20/24
- 3/8 = 9/24
- 20/24 − 9/24 = 11/24

**Hard:** 8⅓ − 3⅝
- Convert: 25/3 − 29/8
- LCD = 24
- 200/24 − 87/24 = 113/24 = 4 17/24

### 4.10 Mixed Operations Involving Subtraction

In real CSE problems, subtraction often combines with other number types or requires multiple steps.

#### Subtracting Whole Numbers and Decimals

Treat whole numbers as decimals with .0:

**Example:** 100 − 34.75

```
  100.00
−  34.75
--------
   65.25
```

#### Subtracting Whole Numbers and Fractions

**Example:** 10 − 3¾

Convert 10 to a mixed number with the same denominator:
- 10 = 9 4/4
- 9 4/4 − 3 3/4 = 6 1/4

Or use improper fractions:
- 10 = 40/4
- 3¾ = 15/4
- 40/4 − 15/4 = 25/4 = 6¼

#### Multi-Step Subtraction Problems

**Example:** A clerk starts with ₱50,000.00 in petty cash. Three disbursements are made: ₱12,345.50, ₱8,765.25, and ₱15,432.75. What is the remaining balance?

Step 1: Total disbursements = 12,345.50 + 8,765.25 + 15,432.75 = 36,543.50
Step 2: Remaining = 50,000.00 − 36,543.50 = 13,456.50

**Remaining: ₱13,456.50**

#### Subtraction in Comparison Problems

**Example:** Department A has 234 employees. Department B has 189 employees. How many more employees does Department A have?

234 − 189 = 45 more employees

### 4.11 Estimation and Mental Subtraction

#### Rounding Technique

Round each number to the nearest convenient value, then subtract:

**Example:** 8,234 − 4,876

- 8,234 ≈ 8,200
- 4,876 ≈ 4,900
- Estimate: 8,200 − 4,900 = 3,300
- Exact: 3,358

If choices are 3,258 / 3,358 / 3,458 / 3,558 — your estimate of 3,300 points to 3,358.

#### Counting Up (Making Change Method)

Instead of subtracting, count up from the subtrahend to the minuend:

**Example:** 1,000 − 683

Count up from 683 to 1,000:
- 683 + 7 = 690
- 690 + 10 = 700
- 700 + 300 = 1,000
- Total added: 7 + 10 + 300 = 317

**Answer: 317**

This is how cashiers make change — and it works great for mental math.

#### Compensation Method

Round the subtrahend to a convenient number, subtract, then adjust:

**Example:** 500 − 198

- Round 198 to 200: 500 − 200 = 300
- You subtracted 2 too many, so add 2 back: 300 + 2 = 302

**Answer: 302**

**Example:** 1,000 − 497

- Round 497 to 500: 1,000 − 500 = 500
- You subtracted 3 too many: 500 + 3 = 503

**Answer: 503**

#### Front-End Estimation

Subtract only the leading digits, then adjust:

**Example:** 7,845 − 3,267

- Front-end: 7,000 − 3,000 = 4,000
- Adjust: 845 − 267 ≈ 600
- Estimate: 4,000 + 600 = 4,600
- Exact: 4,578

#### Compatible Numbers

Find numbers that subtract cleanly:

**Example:** 73 − 28
- Think: 73 − 30 = 43, then add back 2 → 45
- Or: 73 − 23 = 50, then subtract 5 more → 45

### 4.12 Common Errors in Subtraction

Understanding common errors helps you avoid them and catch mistakes during answer-checking.

#### Error 1: Subtracting Smaller from Larger Regardless of Position

```
WRONG:              CORRECT:
  43                  3 13
− 27                  4  3
----                − 2  7
  24 ✗              ------
                      1  6 ✓
```

The wrong approach subtracts 3 from 7 and 2 from 4. You must borrow when the top digit is smaller.

#### Error 2: Forgetting to Reduce After Borrowing

```
WRONG:              CORRECT:
  5 12                4 12
  5  2                5  2
− 2  7              − 2  7
------              ------
  3  5 ✗             2  5 ✓
```

When you borrow 1 from the tens column, the tens digit must decrease by 1.

#### Error 3: Incorrect Borrowing Across Zeros

```
WRONG: 400 − 156 = 354    (didn't cascade borrow correctly)
CORRECT: 400 − 156 = 244
```

Use the shortcut: 9 − 1 = 8, 9 − 5 = 4, 10 − 6 = 4 → 244

#### Error 4: Sign Errors in Integer Subtraction

```
WRONG: 5 − (−3) = 2       (subtracted instead of adding)
CORRECT: 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8
```

Remember: subtracting a negative = adding a positive.

#### Error 5: Misaligned Decimal Points

```
WRONG:              CORRECT:
  12.5                12.50
−  3.75             −  3.75
------              -------
   8.75 ✗             8.75 ✓
```

In this case the answer happens to be the same, but consider:
```
WRONG:              CORRECT:
  12.5                12.5
−  0.75             −  0.75 → need: 12.50 − 0.75
------              --------
  11.75 ✗            11.75 ✓
```

Always align decimal points and pad with zeros.

#### Error 6: Subtracting Denominators

```
WRONG: 5/8 − 3/8 = 2/0    (subtracted both top and bottom)
CORRECT: 5/8 − 3/8 = 2/8 = 1/4
```

**Never subtract denominators.** Only numerators change.

#### Error 7: Incorrect LCD Computation

```
WRONG: LCD of 4 and 6 = 24    (used product instead of LCM)
CORRECT: LCD of 4 and 6 = 12
```

While using the product (4 × 6 = 24) works, it creates larger numbers. The LCD (12) is more efficient and less error-prone.

#### Error 8: Failing to Simplify

If your answer is 4/6 and the choices show 2/3, you must recognize they are equal. Always simplify.

### Exam Strategies for Subtraction

#### Strategy 1: Verify by Adding Back

After subtracting, add your answer to the subtrahend. You should get the minuend.
```
If 845 − 367 = 478, then 478 + 367 should = 845 ✓
```

#### Strategy 2: Check the Last Digit

The last digit of the difference depends only on the last digits:
```
8,456 − 3,289 → last digits: 6 − 9 (borrow) → 16 − 9 = 7
```
If only one choice ends in 7, you've found the answer.

#### Strategy 3: Estimate First

Round and estimate before computing:
```
7,832 − 4,567 ≈ 8,000 − 4,600 = 3,400
```
Eliminate choices far from 3,400.

#### Strategy 4: Use the Compensation Method

For numbers near round values:
```
5,000 − 2,997 = 5,000 − 3,000 + 3 = 2,003
```

#### Strategy 5: Powers-of-10 Shortcut

For subtracting from 10, 100, 1000, etc.:
- Subtract each digit from 9, except the last digit from 10
```
1,000 − 456: 9−4=5, 9−5=4, 10−6=4 → 544
10,000 − 3,782: 9−3=6, 9−7=2, 9−8=1, 10−2=8 → 6,218
```

#### Strategy 6: Eliminate Impossible Choices

- The difference must be less than the minuend
- If minuend > subtrahend, the difference is positive
- The difference cannot be larger than the minuend (for positive numbers)

### Quick Recap

| Topic | Key Rule |
|-------|----------|
| Whole number subtraction | Align place values, borrow when top digit < bottom digit |
| Borrowing across zeros | Cascade: borrow from nearest non-zero digit, reduce each zero to 9 |
| Power-of-10 shortcut | Subtract each digit from 9, last digit from 10 |
| Integer subtraction | Keep-Change-Change: a − b = a + (−b) |
| Subtracting a negative | Two negatives make a positive: a − (−b) = a + b |
| Decimal subtraction | Align decimal points, pad with trailing zeros, then subtract |
| Fraction subtraction (like) | Subtract numerators, keep denominator |
| Fraction subtraction (unlike) | Find LCD, convert, subtract numerators |
| Mixed number borrowing | Borrow 1 from whole number = add denominator to numerator |
| Verification | Add difference + subtrahend = minuend |

### Memory Aids

**Borrowing Reminder:** "When the top is small, borrow from next door — reduce the neighbor by 1, add 10 to yourself."

**Integer Subtraction Mnemonic — KCC:** "Keep, Change, Change" — Keep the first number, Change subtraction to addition, Change the sign of the second number.

**Subtracting a Negative:** "Minus a minus is a plus" — two negatives make a positive.

**Decimal Alignment:** "Point under point" — always line up the decimal points before computing.

**Fraction Rule:** "Same bottom? Just subtract the top. Different bottom? Find the LCD and convert."

**Mixed Number Borrowing:** "Steal 1 from the whole, turn it into denominator-over-denominator, add to the fraction."

**Power-of-10 Shortcut:** "All nines except the last ten" — subtract each digit from 9, last from 10.

**Verification Trick:** "Difference + subtrahend = minuend" — always check by adding back.
