# Percentage Applications

## Introduction

**Percentage applications** represent the practical, real-world use of percentage concepts in contexts that Civil Service Examination takers encounter daily — population analysis, grade computation, survey interpretation, budget allocation, and financial management. Unlike basic percentage problems that ask you to find a part, rate, or whole in isolation, application problems embed percentages inside realistic scenarios that require you to identify what is being asked, select the correct formula, and interpret the result meaningfully.

The Philippine Civil Service Exam heavily tests percentage applications because government employees must:
1. Interpret census and population reports showing growth rates and demographic shifts
2. Compute academic performance metrics and passing rates
3. Analyze survey results and statistical reports for policy decisions
4. Manage budgets, allocate funds, and track financial performance
5. Evaluate employee performance using weighted scoring systems

Percentage applications appear in virtually every professional context:
- **Population analysis** — census growth rates, migration percentages, demographic distributions
- **Academic performance** — grade computation, weighted averages, passing rates, class rankings
- **Surveys and statistics** — response distributions, approval ratings, satisfaction scores
- **Financial management** — budget allocation, savings rates, expense analysis, interest computation
- **Government reports** — year-over-year comparisons, resource utilization, efficiency metrics
- **Business operations** — profit margins, market share, inventory turnover, payroll percentages

**Common mistakes examinees make in percentage application problems:**
1. Using the wrong value as the base (denominator) in contextual problems
2. Confusing "percentage OF" with "percentage MORE THAN" or "percentage LESS THAN"
3. Failing to identify which value represents the whole in survey problems
4. Incorrectly computing weighted averages by treating all components equally
5. Misinterpreting what "percent of the total" means in multi-category problems
6. Arithmetic errors when working with multiple percentage operations in sequence
7. Not reading the question carefully enough to identify exactly what is being asked
8. Rounding intermediate values too early, causing accumulated error

## 4.1 Understanding Percentage Applications

### What Makes a Problem an "Application" Problem?

A percentage application problem places a percentage calculation inside a real-world context. Instead of asking "What is 15% of 200?", it asks "A barangay with 200 households reported that 15% experienced flooding. How many households were affected?"

The math is the same, but the challenge is:
1. **Identifying the values** — which number is the whole? Which is the part? What is the rate?
2. **Selecting the formula** — is this a "find the part," "find the rate," or "find the whole" problem?
3. **Interpreting the answer** — does the result make sense in context?

### The Three Core Percentage Formulas

Every percentage application problem uses one of these three formulas:

**Formula 1: Finding the Part (Amount)**
```
Part = Whole × Rate
```
Use when you know the total and the percentage, and need to find the amount.

**Formula 2: Finding the Rate (Percentage)**
```
Rate = Part ÷ Whole × 100
```
Use when you know the part and the whole, and need to find what percentage the part is.

**Formula 3: Finding the Whole (Total)**
```
Whole = Part ÷ (Rate ÷ 100)
```
Use when you know the amount and the percentage, and need to find the total.

### Identifying Which Formula to Use

| The question asks... | You need... | Formula |
|---------------------|-------------|---------|
| "How many...?" or "How much...?" | The Part | Part = Whole × Rate |
| "What percent...?" or "What rate...?" | The Rate | Rate = Part ÷ Whole × 100 |
| "What is the total...?" or "How many originally...?" | The Whole | Whole = Part ÷ (Rate ÷ 100) |

### Context Clues for Identifying Values

**The Whole (Base/Total) is usually:**
- The total number of respondents in a survey
- The total population of a place
- The total number of items or students
- The original price or salary
- The total budget

**The Part (Amount) is usually:**
- The number who answered a certain way
- The number affected by something
- The number who passed or failed
- The amount spent or saved
- The portion allocated

**The Rate (Percentage) is usually:**
- Stated directly with a "%" symbol
- Described as "fraction of" or "proportion of"
- Given as a decimal that needs conversion

## 4.2 Population Problems

### Why Population Problems Appear on the CSE

Government employees work with census data, demographic reports, and community planning documents. The CSE tests whether you can interpret and compute population-related percentages accurately.

### Types of Population Problems

1. **Population growth** — finding how much a population increased
2. **Population decline** — finding how much a population decreased
3. **Percentage of total** — finding what fraction of a larger population a subgroup represents
4. **Future population projection** — computing expected population given a growth rate
5. **Migration problems** — computing net changes due to people moving in or out

### Key Formulas for Population Problems

**Growth Rate:**
```
Growth Rate = (New Population − Original Population) ÷ Original Population × 100
```

**Future Population (single period):**
```
Future Population = Current Population × (1 + Growth Rate ÷ 100)
```

**Population After Decline:**
```
New Population = Current Population × (1 − Decline Rate ÷ 100)
```

**Percentage of Total:**
```
Percentage = Subgroup ÷ Total Population × 100
```

### Easy Examples

**Example 1: Finding Population Growth Rate**

A municipality's population grew from 50,000 to 54,000 over one year. What is the growth rate?

```
Growth = 54,000 − 50,000 = 4,000
Growth Rate = 4,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 8%
```

**Answer: 8% growth rate**

**Example 2: Finding New Population After Growth**

A barangay has 12,000 residents. If the population grows by 5%, how many residents will there be?

```
New Population = 12,000 × (1 + 5/100)
New Population = 12,000 × 1.05 = 12,600
```

**Answer: 12,600 residents**

### Medium Examples

**Example 3: Percentage of Total Population**

In a city of 250,000 people, 45,000 are senior citizens. What percentage of the population are senior citizens?

```
Percentage = 45,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = 18%
```

**Answer: 18% are senior citizens**

**Example 4: Migration Problem**

A town has 80,000 residents. During the year, 2,400 people moved in and 1,600 moved out. What is the net population change as a percentage?

```
Net change = 2,400 − 1,600 = 800 (net increase)
Percentage = 800 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 1%
```

**Answer: 1% net population increase**

### Hard Examples

**Example 5: Multi-Step Population Problem**

A province has 500,000 residents. The urban population is 60% of the total, and 15% of the urban population are government employees. How many urban government employees are there?

```
Step 1: Urban population = 500,000 × 0.60 = 300,000
Step 2: Urban government employees = 300,000 × 0.15 = 45,000
```

**Answer: 45,000 urban government employees**

**Example 6: Reverse Population Problem**

After a 12% population increase, a city now has 112,000 residents. What was the original population?

```
Original = 112,000 ÷ 1.12 = 100,000
```

**Answer: 100,000 residents originally**

## 4.3 Grade Computation

### Why Grade Problems Appear on the CSE

Academic percentage problems test your ability to compute weighted averages and interpret performance data — skills directly applicable to employee performance evaluations, training assessments, and educational program management.

### Types of Grade Problems

1. **Percentage score** — converting raw scores to percentages
2. **Weighted grades** — computing final grades with different component weights
3. **Passing rate** — finding what percentage of students passed
4. **Class average** — computing the mean percentage score
5. **Required score** — finding what score is needed to achieve a target average

### Key Formulas for Grade Problems

**Percentage Score:**
```
Percentage Score = (Score Obtained ÷ Total Possible Score) × 100
```

**Weighted Grade:**
```
Weighted Grade = (Score₁ × Weight₁) + (Score₂ × Weight₂) + ... + (Scoreₙ × Weightₙ)
```
Where all weights sum to 1.00 (or 100%).

**Passing Rate:**
```
Passing Rate = (Number Who Passed ÷ Total Number of Students) × 100
```

### Easy Examples

**Example 1: Simple Percentage Score**

A student answered 36 out of 50 questions correctly. What is the percentage score?

```
Percentage = 36 ÷ 50 × 100 = 72%
```

**Answer: 72%**

**Example 2: Finding Passing Rate**

In a class of 40 students, 32 passed the exam. What is the passing rate?

```
Passing Rate = 32 ÷ 40 × 100 = 80%
```

**Answer: 80% passing rate**

### Medium Examples

**Example 3: Weighted Grade Computation**

A student's final grade is computed as follows:
- Quizzes (30%): scored 85%
- Midterm (30%): scored 78%
- Final Exam (40%): scored 90%

What is the weighted final grade?

```
Weighted Grade = (85 × 0.30) + (78 × 0.30) + (90 × 0.40)
             = 25.5 + 23.4 + 36.0
             = 84.9%
```

**Answer: 84.9%**

**Example 4: Required Score Problem**

A student has scores of 80%, 75%, and 85% on three equally weighted exams. What score is needed on the fourth exam to achieve an overall average of 82%?

```
Target total = 82 × 4 = 328
Current total = 80 + 75 + 85 = 240
Required score = 328 − 240 = 88%
```

**Answer: 88% needed on the fourth exam**

### Hard Examples

**Example 5: Multi-Component Weighted Grade**

An employee's performance rating uses this system:
- Attendance (10%): 95%
- Project completion (35%): 88%
- Quality of work (35%): 92%
- Teamwork (20%): 85%

What is the overall performance rating?

```
Rating = (95 × 0.10) + (88 × 0.35) + (92 × 0.35) + (85 × 0.20)
       = 9.5 + 30.8 + 32.2 + 17.0
       = 89.5%
```

**Answer: 89.5% overall performance rating**

**Example 6: Percentage of Students in Score Ranges**

In a class of 50 students:
- 8 scored 90% and above
- 15 scored 80%–89%
- 18 scored 70%–79%
- 9 scored below 70%

What percentage scored 80% or above?

```
Students scoring 80%+ = 8 + 15 = 23
Percentage = 23 ÷ 50 × 100 = 46%
```

**Answer: 46% scored 80% or above**

## 4.4 Statistics and Surveys

### Why Survey Problems Appear on the CSE

Government agencies conduct surveys constantly — employee satisfaction, public opinion, service quality, demographic studies. Civil servants must interpret survey data accurately and compute percentages from raw response counts.

### Types of Survey and Statistics Problems

1. **Response percentage** — what percent of respondents chose a particular option
2. **Comparison problems** — comparing percentages across groups
3. **Majority/minority analysis** — determining which response dominates
4. **Missing data** — finding the percentage for an unlisted category
5. **Sample-to-population projection** — estimating totals from survey percentages

### Key Formulas for Survey Problems

**Response Percentage:**
```
Response % = (Number Who Chose Option ÷ Total Respondents) × 100
```

**Finding Missing Percentage:**
```
Missing % = 100% − Sum of All Other Percentages
```

**Projecting to Population:**
```
Estimated Count = Population × (Survey Percentage ÷ 100)
```

### Easy Examples

**Example 1: Basic Survey Percentage**

In a survey of 200 employees, 70 said they prefer flexible work hours. What percentage prefer flexible hours?

```
Percentage = 70 ÷ 200 × 100 = 35%
```

**Answer: 35%**

**Example 2: Finding the Count from Percentage**

A survey found that 40% of 500 respondents support a new policy. How many respondents support it?

```
Count = 500 × 0.40 = 200
```

**Answer: 200 respondents**

### Medium Examples

**Example 3: Missing Category Percentage**

In a transportation survey of commuters:
- 35% use jeepneys
- 25% use buses
- 20% use tricycles
- The rest use private vehicles

What percentage use private vehicles?

```
Private vehicles = 100% − 35% − 25% − 20% = 20%
```

**Answer: 20% use private vehicles**

**Example 4: Comparing Survey Groups**

In a satisfaction survey:
- 45% rated the service "Excellent"
- 30% rated it "Good"
- 15% rated it "Fair"
- 10% rated it "Poor"

What percentage rated the service "Good" or better?

```
Good or better = 45% + 30% = 75%
```

**Answer: 75% rated the service "Good" or better**

### Hard Examples

**Example 5: Multi-Step Survey Analysis**

A company surveyed 800 employees about training preferences:
- 40% prefer online training
- 35% prefer classroom training
- 25% prefer on-the-job training

Of those who prefer online training, 60% want self-paced modules. How many employees want self-paced online modules?

```
Step 1: Online preference = 800 × 0.40 = 320 employees
Step 2: Self-paced = 320 × 0.60 = 192 employees
```

**Answer: 192 employees want self-paced online modules**

**Example 6: Population Projection from Survey**

A survey of 1,000 residents found that 12% are unemployed. If the city has 150,000 residents, approximately how many are unemployed?

```
Estimated unemployed = 150,000 × 0.12 = 18,000
```

**Answer: Approximately 18,000 residents are unemployed**

## 4.5 Financial Applications

### Why Financial Problems Appear on the CSE

Government employees handle budgets, process financial reports, and manage public funds. The CSE tests whether you can compute percentages in financial contexts accurately — a skill essential for procurement, auditing, payroll, and budget management.

### Types of Financial Percentage Problems

1. **Budget allocation** — finding what percentage goes to each category
2. **Savings percentage** — computing how much of income is saved
3. **Expense analysis** — determining what fraction of a budget is spent on each item
4. **Simple interest** — computing interest earned or owed
5. **Salary-based problems** — deductions, raises, take-home pay

### Key Formulas for Financial Problems

**Budget Allocation Percentage:**
```
Allocation % = (Amount for Category ÷ Total Budget) × 100
```

**Savings Percentage:**
```
Savings % = (Amount Saved ÷ Total Income) × 100
```

**Amount After Deduction:**
```
Take-Home = Salary × (1 − Deduction Rate ÷ 100)
```

**Simple Interest:**
```
Interest = Principal × Rate × Time
```

### Easy Examples

**Example 1: Budget Allocation**

A household earns ₱35,000 monthly and spends ₱10,500 on food. What percentage of income goes to food?

```
Percentage = 10,500 ÷ 35,000 × 100 = 30%
```

**Answer: 30% goes to food**

**Example 2: Savings Computation**

An employee earns ₱28,000 and saves ₱5,600 each month. What percentage of salary is saved?

```
Savings % = 5,600 ÷ 28,000 × 100 = 20%
```

**Answer: 20% savings rate**

### Medium Examples

**Example 3: Multiple Deductions**

An employee's gross salary is ₱40,000. Deductions are:
- Tax: 10%
- SSS: 4%
- PhilHealth: 3%

What is the total deduction amount and take-home pay?

```
Total deduction rate = 10% + 4% + 3% = 17%
Total deduction = ₱40,000 × 0.17 = ₱6,800
Take-home pay = ₱40,000 − ₱6,800 = ₱33,200
```

**Answer: ₱6,800 deducted; ₱33,200 take-home pay**

**Example 4: Budget Distribution**

A department has a ₱500,000 annual budget allocated as:
- Personnel: 60%
- Operations: 25%
- Capital outlay: 15%

How much is allocated to each?

```
Personnel = 500,000 × 0.60 = ₱300,000
Operations = 500,000 × 0.25 = ₱125,000
Capital outlay = 500,000 × 0.15 = ₱75,000
```

**Answer: ₱300,000 / ₱125,000 / ₱75,000**

### Hard Examples

**Example 5: Reverse Budget Problem**

A family spends 25% of their income on rent, which amounts to ₱8,750. What is their total monthly income?

```
Total income = 8,750 ÷ 0.25 = ₱35,000
```

**Answer: ₱35,000 monthly income**

**Example 6: Multi-Step Financial Problem**

An office has a ₱200,000 monthly budget. They spend 45% on salaries, 20% on utilities, and 15% on supplies. The remaining budget is split equally between maintenance and miscellaneous. How much goes to maintenance?

```
Step 1: Allocated = 45% + 20% + 15% = 80%
Step 2: Remaining = 100% − 80% = 20%
Step 3: Maintenance = 20% ÷ 2 = 10%
Step 4: Maintenance amount = 200,000 × 0.10 = ₱20,000
```

**Answer: ₱20,000 for maintenance**

## 4.6 Multi-Step Percentage Application Problems

### Why Multi-Step Problems Are Important

The CSE frequently presents problems that require two or more percentage operations in sequence. These test your ability to organize computations logically and avoid errors that compound across steps.

### Strategy for Multi-Step Problems

1. **Read the entire problem first** — identify all the percentage operations needed
2. **Break into steps** — solve one operation at a time
3. **Label intermediate values** — write down what each number represents
4. **Use the result of one step as input to the next**
5. **Check reasonableness** — does the final answer make sense?

### Easy Examples

**Example 1: Two-Step Population Problem**

A city has 200,000 residents. 60% are adults, and 25% of adults are government employees. How many government employees are there?

```
Step 1: Adults = 200,000 × 0.60 = 120,000
Step 2: Government employees = 120,000 × 0.25 = 30,000
```

**Answer: 30,000 government employees**

**Example 2: Two-Step Budget Problem**

A company allocates 40% of its ₱1,000,000 budget to marketing. Of the marketing budget, 30% goes to online advertising. How much is spent on online advertising?

```
Step 1: Marketing budget = 1,000,000 × 0.40 = ₱400,000
Step 2: Online advertising = 400,000 × 0.30 = ₱120,000
```

**Answer: ₱120,000 for online advertising**

### Medium Examples

**Example 3: Survey with Subgroup Analysis**

A school surveyed 500 students about their preferred study method:
- 40% prefer group study
- Of those who prefer group study, 75% study in the library

How many students prefer group study in the library?

```
Step 1: Group study = 500 × 0.40 = 200 students
Step 2: Library group study = 200 × 0.75 = 150 students
```

**Answer: 150 students**

**Example 4: Salary After Multiple Adjustments**

An employee earns ₱30,000. They receive a 10% raise, then 15% of the new salary is deducted for taxes. What is the take-home pay after the raise?

```
Step 1: After raise = 30,000 × 1.10 = ₱33,000
Step 2: Tax deduction = 33,000 × 0.15 = ₱4,950
Step 3: Take-home = 33,000 − 4,950 = ₱28,050
```

**Answer: ₱28,050 take-home pay**

### Hard Examples

**Example 5: Three-Step Problem**

A province has 400,000 residents. The urban population is 55% of the total. Among urban residents, 70% are of working age. Of working-age urban residents, 8% are unemployed. How many urban residents are unemployed?

```
Step 1: Urban = 400,000 × 0.55 = 220,000
Step 2: Working age = 220,000 × 0.70 = 154,000
Step 3: Unemployed = 154,000 × 0.08 = 12,320
```

**Answer: 12,320 unemployed urban residents**

**Example 6: Combined Financial and Survey Problem**

A company with 600 employees surveyed satisfaction. 80% responded. Of respondents, 65% were satisfied. The company plans to spend ₱500 per satisfied respondent on a reward program. What is the total reward cost?

```
Step 1: Respondents = 600 × 0.80 = 480
Step 2: Satisfied = 480 × 0.65 = 312
Step 3: Total cost = 312 × ₱500 = ₱156,000
```

**Answer: ₱156,000 total reward cost**

## 4.7 Percentage Interpretation and Analysis

### Comparing Percentage Values

When comparing percentages, always consider the base values. A higher percentage does not always mean a larger absolute quantity.

**Example:** 
- Department A: 20% of 50 employees were promoted → 10 people
- Department B: 10% of 200 employees were promoted → 20 people

Department A has a higher promotion *rate*, but Department B promoted more *people*.

### Percentage vs. Actual Quantity

**Critical distinction for the CSE:**
- "30% of employees are absent" tells you the proportion, not the count
- To find the count, you need the total: 30% of 200 = 60 employees absent
- A "5% increase" in a large number can be more significant than a "50% increase" in a small number

### Identifying Trends

When data shows percentages over time, look for:
- **Consistent direction** — all increasing or all decreasing
- **Acceleration** — the rate of change is itself increasing
- **Reversal** — a change in direction from increase to decrease or vice versa

**Example:** A department's budget allocation over 3 years:
- Year 1: 15% of total budget
- Year 2: 18% of total budget  
- Year 3: 22% of total budget

The trend shows increasing allocation (growing by 3-4 percentage points per year).

### Misleading Percentage Interpretations

**Trap 1: Percentages that don't add to 100%**
In surveys where respondents can choose multiple options, percentages may exceed 100%. This is not an error — it means the categories overlap.

**Trap 2: Percentage of a subset vs. percentage of the whole**
- "60% of respondents who exercise prefer morning workouts" ≠ "60% of all respondents prefer morning workouts"
- The base matters enormously

**Trap 3: Small sample sizes**
- "100% of surveyed residents agree" sounds unanimous
- But if only 3 people were surveyed, it means very little

### Workplace Application Examples

| Statement | Correct Interpretation |
|-----------|----------------------|
| "75% of employees completed training" | 3 out of every 4 employees finished |
| "Customer complaints dropped 40%" | If 100 complaints before → now 60 |
| "15% of the budget is unspent" | 85% has been utilized |
| "Satisfaction rose from 60% to 72%" | 12 percentage point increase; 20% relative increase |
| "3 out of 5 offices met targets" | 60% compliance rate |

## 4.8 Practical Applications in Government and Business

### Population Census Reports

**Example:** According to a census, a municipality's population distribution is:
- 0–14 years: 28%
- 15–64 years: 65%
- 65+ years: 7%

If the total population is 120,000, how many are in the working-age group (15–64)?

```
Working age = 120,000 × 0.65 = 78,000
```

### Government Budget Allocations

**Example:** A local government unit has a ₱50 million annual budget:
- Education: 30%
- Health: 20%
- Infrastructure: 25%
- Social services: 15%
- Administration: 10%

The education budget must be split: 70% for teacher salaries, 30% for facilities. How much goes to facilities?

```
Education budget = 50,000,000 × 0.30 = ₱15,000,000
Facilities = 15,000,000 × 0.30 = ₱4,500,000
```

### Educational Performance Reports

**Example:** A school district reports:
- Total students: 8,000
- Passed national assessment: 6,200
- Scored "Outstanding": 1,440

What percentage passed? What percentage of those who passed scored "Outstanding"?

```
Passing rate = 6,200 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = 77.5%
Outstanding among passers = 1,440 ÷ 6,200 × 100 ≈ 23.2%
```

### Office Performance Metrics

**Example:** A government office processed 2,500 applications this quarter:
- Approved: 1,875
- Denied: 375
- Pending: 250

What is the approval rate? What percentage are still pending?

```
Approval rate = 1,875 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = 75%
Pending rate = 250 ÷ 2,500 × 100 = 10%
```

### Payroll Analysis

**Example:** A company's monthly payroll is ₱2,400,000 for 80 employees. If 15% of employees earn above ₱40,000 monthly, how many employees earn above ₱40,000?

```
Employees above ₱40,000 = 80 × 0.15 = 12 employees
```

### Inventory Management

**Example:** A warehouse has 4,000 items in stock. After a month, 12% were sold, 3% were damaged, and the rest remain. How many items remain?

```
Sold = 4,000 × 0.12 = 480
Damaged = 4,000 × 0.03 = 120
Remaining = 4,000 − 480 − 120 = 3,400
(Or: 100% − 12% − 3% = 85%; 4,000 × 0.85 = 3,400)
```

## 4.9 Problem-Solving Strategies

### Strategy 1: Identify Known and Unknown Values

Before computing, list what you know and what you need:

| Given | Need to Find | Approach |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| Total and percentage | The amount | Multiply: Total × Rate |
| Amount and total | The percentage | Divide: Amount ÷ Total × 100 |
| Amount and percentage | The total | Divide: Amount ÷ Rate |
| Two amounts | The percentage relationship | Divide smaller by larger (or part by whole) |

### Strategy 2: Translate Words to Math

Common phrasings and their mathematical translations:

| Phrase | Math Operation |
|--------|---------------|
| "X% of Y" | Y × (X/100) |
| "What percent of Y is X?" | (X ÷ Y) × 100 |
| "X is what percent of Y?" | (X ÷ Y) × 100 |
| "X% of what number is Y?" | Y ÷ (X/100) |
| "How many out of..." | Part ÷ Whole × 100 |

### Strategy 3: Estimate Before Solving

Use benchmark percentages to quickly estimate:
- 10% → divide by 10
- 25% → divide by 4
- 50% → divide by 2
- 33% → divide by 3
- 20% → divide by 5

**Example:** "What is 23% of 400?"
- Estimate: 25% of 400 = 100, so 23% is slightly less → about 92
- Exact: 400 × 0.23 = 92 ✓

### Strategy 4: Check Answer Reasonableness

After solving, verify:
- Is the part smaller than the whole? (It must be, unless dealing with percentages > 100%)
- Is the percentage between 0% and 100% for standard problems?
- Does the answer make real-world sense? (e.g., a salary can't be negative)
- If you found the whole, is it larger than the given part?

### Strategy 5: Work Backwards to Verify

After finding your answer, plug it back into the problem:
- If you found that 25% of X = 50, verify: X = 200, and 25% of 200 = 50 ✓
- If you found a passing rate of 80%, verify: 80% of total = number who passed ✓

## 4.10 Estimation and Mental Math Techniques

### Benchmark Percentage Shortcuts

Memorize these for instant computation:

| Percentage | Shortcut | Example: 15% of 240 |
|-----------|----------|---------------------|
| 1% | Divide by 100 | 240 ÷ 100 = 2.4 |
| 5% | Half of 10% | 12 |
| 10% | Move decimal left | 24 |
| 15% | 10% + 5% | 24 + 12 = 36 |
| 20% | Double 10% | 48 |
| 25% | Divide by 4 | 60 |
| 33⅓% | Divide by 3 | 80 |
| 50% | Divide by 2 | 120 |
| 75% | 50% + 25% | 120 + 60 = 180 |

### Rapid Approximation for Survey Problems

When a survey has round numbers, use fractions:
- 200 out of 800 = 1/4 = 25%
- 150 out of 500 = 3/10 = 30%
- 180 out of 600 = 3/10 = 30%

### Elimination Strategies for Multiple Choice

**Step 1:** Estimate the answer range
**Step 2:** Eliminate choices that are clearly too high or too low
**Step 3:** Compute only if two choices remain close

**Example:** "What is 18% of 450?"
- 10% of 450 = 45
- 20% of 450 = 90
- 18% is between 45 and 90, closer to 90
- Estimate: about 81
- If choices are A) 45, B) 81, C) 90, D) 108 → Answer is B

### Quick Grade Computation

For equally weighted scores, use the average shortcut:
- Scores: 80, 85, 90, 75
- Quick average: midpoint is about 82-83
- Exact: (80 + 85 + 90 + 75) ÷ 4 = 330 ÷ 4 = 82.5

For weighted scores, compute the dominant weight first:
- If one component is 50% weight, start there and adjust

## 4.11 Common Errors in Percentage Applications

### Error 1: Using the Wrong Base Value

**Wrong:** "150 out of 200 students passed. What percentage failed?"
- Wrong approach: 150 ÷ 200 = 75% failed ✗
- Right approach: Failed = 200 − 150 = 50. Percentage = 50 ÷ 200 × 100 = 25% ✓

### Error 2: Incorrect Percentage Interpretation

**Wrong:** "A survey shows 60% approval. In a group of 300, that means 60 people approve."
- Right: 60% of 300 = 300 × 0.60 = 180 people approve

The error: confusing "60%" with "60 people."

### Error 3: Using Incorrect Formulas

**Wrong:** "What percent of 80 is 20?" → 80 ÷ 20 = 4 = 400%
- Right: 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = 25%

The error: dividing the whole by the part instead of part by whole.

### Error 4: Overlooking Weighted Values

**Wrong:** Computing a simple average when weights are given.
- If Quiz (20%) = 90 and Exam (80%) = 70:
- Wrong: (90 + 70) ÷ 2 = 80
- Right: (90 × 0.20) + (70 × 0.80) = 18 + 56 = 74

### Error 5: Rounding Too Early

**Wrong:** In multi-step problems, rounding intermediate results.
- Step 1 result: 33.33% → rounded to 33%
- Step 2 uses 33% instead of 33.33% → final answer is off

**Rule:** Keep full precision until the final answer, then round.

### Error 6: Misreading Statistical Data

**Wrong:** "75% of respondents who exercise prefer morning workouts" interpreted as "75% of ALL respondents prefer morning workouts."

The error: ignoring that the percentage applies only to a subset (those who exercise), not the entire group.

### Error 7: Confusing "Percent OF" with "Percent MORE THAN"

**Wrong:** "A is 25% more than B" interpreted as "A = 25% of B"
- Right: "A is 25% more than B" means A = B + 25% of B = 1.25 × B
- "A is 25% of B" means A = 0.25 × B

These are completely different relationships.

## Exam Strategies

- **Read the question twice before computing.** Identify exactly what is being asked — the part, the rate, or the whole.
- **Underline key numbers and percentages.** This prevents mixing up values in multi-step problems.
- **Identify the base (whole) immediately.** Ask: "Percent of WHAT?" The answer to that question is your denominator.
- **Translate word problems into formulas.** Write "X% of Y = Z" and solve for the unknown.
- **Estimate first.** Use benchmark percentages (10%, 25%, 50%) to narrow down the answer before computing.
- **Watch for "of the remaining" or "of those who..."** These phrases signal that the base has changed from the original total.
- **Check that percentages in a complete distribution sum to 100%.** If they don't, either a category is missing or the problem allows multiple selections.
- **For weighted averages, verify weights sum to 100%.** If they don't, the problem may be poorly stated or you're missing a component.
- **Use elimination aggressively.** If the answer must be less than 50% and three choices are above 50%, pick the remaining one.
- **Time management:** Simple application problems should take 30-45 seconds. Multi-step problems may take 60-90 seconds. Skip and return if stuck beyond 90 seconds.

## Mini Practice Set

**1.** A student answered 45 out of 60 questions correctly. What is the percentage score?
**Answer:** 75%
**Explanation:** 45 ÷ 60 × 100 = 75%.

**2.** A barangay's population grew from 25,000 to 27,500. What is the growth rate?
**Answer:** 10%
**Explanation:** Growth = 27,500 − 25,000 = 2,500. Rate = 2,500 ÷ 25,000 × 100 = 10%.

**3.** In a survey of 400 employees, 280 prefer the new schedule. What percentage prefer it?
**Answer:** 70%
**Explanation:** 280 ÷ 400 × 100 = 70%.

**4.** A family spends 35% of their ₱40,000 income on rent. How much is the rent?
**Answer:** ₱14,000
**Explanation:** 40,000 × 0.35 = ₱14,000.

**5.** A class of 50 students has a 76% passing rate. How many students passed?
**Answer:** 38 students
**Explanation:** 50 × 0.76 = 38.

**6.** An employee's weighted grade: Attendance (10%) = 100%, Tasks (40%) = 85%, Exam (50%) = 78%. What is the final grade?
**Answer:** 83%
**Explanation:** (100 × 0.10) + (85 × 0.40) + (78 × 0.50) = 10 + 34 + 39 = 83%.

**7.** A city has 180,000 residents. If 12% are senior citizens, how many seniors are there?
**Answer:** 21,600
**Explanation:** 180,000 × 0.12 = 21,600.

**8.** A department's budget is ₱800,000. If 22% is spent on training, how much is that?
**Answer:** ₱176,000
**Explanation:** 800,000 × 0.22 = ₱176,000.

**9.** In a satisfaction survey, 45% said "Excellent" and 30% said "Good." What percentage said neither?
**Answer:** 25%
**Explanation:** 100% − 45% − 30% = 25%.

**10.** A school has 1,200 students. 65% are in the professional track. How many are NOT in the professional track?
**Answer:** 420 students
**Explanation:** Non-professional = 100% − 65% = 35%. Count = 1,200 × 0.35 = 420.

**11.** An office processed 3,000 applications. 2,250 were approved. What is the approval rate?
**Answer:** 75%
**Explanation:** 2,250 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = 75%.

**12.** A worker saves 15% of a ₱32,000 salary. How much is saved?
**Answer:** ₱4,800
**Explanation:** 32,000 × 0.15 = ₱4,800.

**13.** 40% of a company's 500 employees are female. Of the females, 30% are in management. How many females are in management?
**Answer:** 60
**Explanation:** Females = 500 × 0.40 = 200. In management = 200 × 0.30 = 60.

**14.** A town's population is 60,000. If 8% moved away, how many remain?
**Answer:** 55,200
**Explanation:** Remaining = 60,000 × (1 − 0.08) = 60,000 × 0.92 = 55,200.

**15.** A student needs 75% to pass. The exam has 80 questions. What is the minimum number of correct answers needed?
**Answer:** 60 questions
**Explanation:** 80 × 0.75 = 60.

**16.** A survey of 600 commuters: 35% use buses, 25% use jeepneys, 20% use trains. How many use other transport?
**Answer:** 120 commuters
**Explanation:** Other = 100% − 35% − 25% − 20% = 20%. Count = 600 × 0.20 = 120.

**17.** An employee earns ₱45,000. After a 12% tax deduction, what is the take-home pay?
**Answer:** ₱39,600
**Explanation:** Take-home = 45,000 × (1 − 0.12) = 45,000 × 0.88 = ₱39,600.

**18.** A municipality's 150,000 population is 55% urban. How many live in rural areas?
**Answer:** 67,500
**Explanation:** Rural = 100% − 55% = 45%. Count = 150,000 × 0.45 = 67,500.

**19.** A project is 80% complete. If 240 tasks are done, how many total tasks are there?
**Answer:** 300 tasks
**Explanation:** Total = 240 ÷ 0.80 = 300.

**20.** A government office has 120 employees. 85% attended a seminar. Of attendees, 60% rated it "Very Useful." How many rated it "Very Useful"?
**Answer:** 61 employees (rounded from 61.2)
**Explanation:** Attendees = 120 × 0.85 = 102. Very Useful = 102 × 0.60 = 61.2 ≈ 61.

## Quick Recap

| Application Area | Key Formula | Watch Out For |
|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Population** | Growth Rate = Change ÷ Original × 100 | Always divide by the ORIGINAL population |
| **Grades** | Weighted = Σ(Score × Weight) | Weights must sum to 100% |
| **Surveys** | Response % = Count ÷ Total × 100 | Identify the correct total (all respondents vs. subgroup) |
| **Financial** | Allocation = Budget × Rate | Multiple deductions are additive rates |
| **Multi-step** | Chain operations sequentially | Use result of Step N as input to Step N+1 |
| **Reverse** | Whole = Part ÷ (Rate/100) | Don't subtract percentage of the result |

**Essential reminders:**
- "Percent OF what?" — the answer is always your denominator
- Weighted averages ≠ simple averages when weights differ
- Survey percentages apply to the stated group, not necessarily the whole population
- In multi-step problems, the base changes at each step
- Estimate first, compute second, verify third
- If percentages in a distribution don't sum to 100%, a category is missing or overlapping

## Memory Aids

### The "OF = MULTIPLY" Rule
```
X% OF Y = X/100 × Y
```
Whenever you see "percent OF," it means multiply.

### The "IS OVER OF" Triangle
```
        Part (IS)
       /        \
  Rate (%)    Whole (OF)
```
- Part = Rate × Whole
- Rate = Part ÷ Whole
- Whole = Part ÷ Rate

Think: "**I**s **O**ver **O**f" — the Part IS some percent, OVER the whole (OF).

### Weighted Average Reminder
```
Don't average the averages!
Weight × Score, then ADD all products.
```

Think: "**W**eights **W**in" — always multiply by weights first.

### Survey Base Checker
Before computing a survey percentage, ask:
```
"Percent of WHO?"
- All respondents? → Use total count
- A subgroup? → Use subgroup count
```

### The "Complement Shortcut"
```
If 65% passed → 35% failed (100% − 65%)
If 80% is allocated → 20% remains
```
Think: "What's LEFT is 100 minus what's USED."

### Multi-Step Chain Rule
```
Step 1 result → becomes Step 2 input
Never go back to the original total after Step 1
(unless the problem explicitly says "of the original")
```
