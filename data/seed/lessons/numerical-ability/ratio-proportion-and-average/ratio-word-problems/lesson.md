# Ratio Word Problems

## Explanations

### Introduction

**Ratio word problems** take the abstract concept of ratios and embed them in real-world scenarios that mirror the Philippine Civil Service Examination. These problems require you to extract ratio information from prose, set up correct equations, and compute unknown values — all while avoiding the reading comprehension traps that the CSE deliberately includes.

Unlike basic ratio exercises that give you explicit numbers ("Simplify 24:36"), word problems hide the ratio inside a story. You must identify:
- Which two quantities are being compared
- In what order they are mentioned
- Which quantity is known and which is unknown
- Whether the problem involves part-to-part or part-to-whole relationships

### Why Ratio Word Problems Are Tested in the CSE

Government employees constantly interpret ratio information in reports, budgets, and statistical data. The CSE tests ratio word problems because:
- Budget officers compute fund allocations based on mandated ratios
- HR personnel determine staffing needs using employee-to-supervisor ratios
- Procurement specialists compare unit prices using ratio reasoning
- Census data uses ratios extensively (population density, dependency ratios)
- Engineers and planners use map scales and material mixing ratios daily

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Misreading the ratio order** — writing the ratio backward from what the question asks
2. **Confusing part-to-part with part-to-whole** — using the wrong quantity as the total
3. **Setting up the proportion incorrectly** — placing numbers in wrong positions
4. **Forgetting to simplify** — leaving a ratio in unsimplified form when lowest terms are expected
5. **Arithmetic errors in cross-multiplication** — multiplying the wrong pairs
6. **Unit inconsistency** — mixing kilograms and grams, minutes and hours
7. **Using the wrong operation** — adding when you should multiply, multiplying when you should divide
8. **Misidentifying direct vs. inverse relationships** — setting up a direct proportion when the problem is inverse, or vice versa

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Extract ratio relationships from word problem narratives
- Set up correct proportion equations based on the problem description
- Solve for unknown quantities using cross-multiplication and scaling
- Distinguish between part-to-part and part-to-whole ratio problems
- Apply ratio reasoning to practical CSE scenarios (budgets, workforce, recipes, maps)
- Verify ratio-based answers for reasonableness and order correctness

### 4.1 Understanding Ratio Word Problems

#### What Makes a Ratio Problem a "Word Problem"?

A ratio word problem embeds the ratio relationship inside a narrative. Instead of "What is the ratio of 15 to 25?", you get:

> "In a barangay cleanup drive, 15 volunteers showed up on Saturday and 25 on Sunday. What is the ratio of Saturday volunteers to Sunday volunteers?"

The math is the same (15:25 = 3:5), but the problem tests whether you can identify which number corresponds to which quantity.

#### The Two-Step Process

**Step 1: Identify the ratio being described.**
- What two quantities are being compared?
- In what order are they named?

**Step 2: Determine what is being asked.**
- Are you finding a missing term in an equivalent ratio?
- Are you expressing a relationship in simplest form?
- Are you computing actual quantities from ratio information?

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Separating the identification step from the computation step prevents order errors. By explicitly writing the ratio in the order given BEFORE doing any math, you build a mental checkpoint. Most ratio errors come from rushing through identification — the math itself is usually easy once the ratio is set up correctly.

#### Keyword Recognition for Ratio Problems

Certain keywords reliably signal the type of ratio problem:

| Keyword / Phrase | What It Signals | Example |
|-----------------|----------------|---------|
| "ratio of A to B" | Part-to-part ratio | "ratio of boys to girls" |
| "out of" / "of the total" | Part-to-whole ratio | "45 out of 60 passed" |
| "for every" | Either type (check context) | "for every 3 boys, 5 girls" |
| "per" / "each" / "every one" | Unit ratio or rate | "kilometers per hour" |
| "in the ratio" | Part-to-part with scaling | "divided in the ratio 2:3" |
| "same as" / "equivalent to" | Equivalent ratio check | "is 15:25 equivalent to 3:5?" |

### 4.2 Finding Missing Values in Ratios

#### The Proportion Method

When two ratios are equivalent, they form a proportion: a:b = c:d. If three of the four values are known, the fourth can be found by cross-multiplication.

```
a:b = c:d → a × d = b × c → solve for the unknown
```

#### Easy Examples

**Example 1:** If 3 notebooks cost ₱45, how much do 7 notebooks cost?
- Ratio: 3 notebooks : ₱45 = 7 notebooks : ?
- Cross-multiply: 3 × ? = 45 × 7
- 3 × ? = 315
- ? = 315 ÷ 3 = **₱105**

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Cross-multiplication works because a proportion states that two ratios are equal. If 3/45 = 7/x, then multiplying both sides by 45x gives 3x = 45×7. This is the algebraic foundation of all proportion problems — the unknown is isolated by using the inverse operation.

**Example 2:** A recipe uses 2 cups of flour for every 3 cups of sugar. How much flour is needed if 12 cups of sugar are used?
- Ratio: flour:sugar = 2:3 = ?:12
- Scaling: 3 × 4 = 12, so 2 × 4 = **8 cups of flour**

#### Medium Examples

**Example 3:** The ratio of male to female employees in an office is 5:7. If there are 35 male employees, how many female employees are there?
- male:female = 5:7 = 35:?
- 5 × 7 = 35, so 7 × 7 = **49 female employees**

**Example 4:** A map scale is 1:50,000. If two cities are 8 cm apart on the map, what is the actual distance in kilometers?
- 1:50,000 = 8:x
- x = 8 × 50,000 = 400,000 cm = **4 km**

#### Hard Examples

**Example 5:** Three numbers are in the ratio 2:5:7. Their sum is 84. What is the largest number?
- Total parts = 2 + 5 + 7 = 14
- Each part = 84 ÷ 14 = 6
- Largest = 7 × 6 = **42**

**Example 6:** The ratio of men to women in a barangay is 3:2. If there are 1,200 more men than women, how many men are there?
- Difference in parts = 3 − 2 = 1 part
- 1 part = 1,200 people
- Men = 3 × 1,200 = **3,600 men**

### 4.3 Part-to-Part Ratio Problems

In part-to-part problems, the ratio compares two distinct parts of a group. The total is the sum of the parts.

#### Finding a Missing Quantity

**Example:** In a class, the ratio of boys to girls is 4:3. If there are 21 girls, how many boys are there?
- boys:girls = 4:3 = ?:21
- 3 × 7 = 21, so 4 × 7 = **28 boys**

#### Finding Actual Quantities from the Total

**Example:** A budget of ₱1,000,000 is divided between two departments in a 2:3 ratio. How much does each get?
- Total parts = 2 + 3 = 5
- Each part = ₱1,000,000 ÷ 5 = ₱200,000
- Department A = 2 × ₱200,000 = **₱400,000**
- Department B = 3 × ₱200,000 = **₱600,000**

#### Finding Quantities from a Difference

**Example:** Two inspectors process applications in a 4:3 ratio. If Inspector A processes 50 more applications than Inspector B, how many does each process?
- Difference in parts = 4 − 3 = 1 part
- 1 part = 50 applications
- Inspector A = 4 × 50 = 200 applications
- Inspector B = 3 × 50 = 150 applications

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "In a part-to-part ratio, the whole is always the sum of the two given numbers."
>
> **Why it fails:** This is only true when the problem explicitly states that the two categories are the ONLY ones in the group. For example, if a problem says "a bag contains only red and blue marbles in a 2:3 ratio," then yes, the whole is 5 parts. But if the problem says "the ratio of boys to girls is 4:3," and doesn't specify these are the only categories, there could be teachers, staff, or other groups not mentioned.
>
> **Correct model:** Always check whether the problem states or implies that the two categories comprise the entire group. Keywords like "only," "consists of," or "all" signal that the sum of parts equals the whole. Without these, treat it as part-to-part only.

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The "value of one part" method is the universal tool for part-to-part problems. When you know the total and the ratio, dividing the total by the number of parts gives you the value of one part. This works because the ratio tells you how many equal-sized "part units" make up each quantity — if the ratio is 4:3, then Quantity A gets 4 parts and Quantity B gets 3 parts, for a total of 7 equal parts. The total must be exactly divisible into these 7 equal pieces.

### 4.4 Part-to-Whole Ratio Problems

In part-to-whole problems, the ratio compares one part to the total. The total is explicitly given or can be computed.

#### Example 1: Given the Whole

A school has 800 students. The ratio of boys to total students is 3:8. How many boys are there?
- Boys:Total = 3:8 = ?:800
- 8 × 100 = 800, so 3 × 100 = **300 boys**

#### Example 2: Finding the Whole from a Part

In a survey, 240 people preferred Candidate A. If the ratio of Candidate A supporters to total respondents is 3:5, how many total respondents were there?
- A:Total = 3:5 = 240:?
- 3 × 80 = 240, so 5 × 80 = **400 total respondents**

#### Example 3: Converting Part-to-Part to Part-to-Whole

The ratio of passed to failed examinees is 7:3. If 420 passed, what fraction of total examinees passed?
- Total parts = 7 + 3 = 10
- Passed:Total = 7:10
- Fraction passed = 7/10 = **70%**

### 4.5 Unit Ratio and Rate Problems

#### Unit Price Comparison

**Example:** Store A sells 5 kg of rice for ₱275. Store B sells 8 kg for ₱416. Which is cheaper per kg?
- Store A: ₱275 ÷ 5 = ₱55 per kg
- Store B: ₱416 ÷ 8 = ₱52 per kg
- **Store B is cheaper** by ₱3 per kg

#### Speed and Rate Problems

**Example:** A delivery truck travels 240 km in 4 hours. What is its average speed?
- Speed = 240 km ÷ 4 hours = **60 km/h**

#### Work Rate Problems

**Example:** A clerk processes 25 applications per hour. How many applications can she process in 7 hours?
- Rate = 25 applications/hour
- In 7 hours: 25 × 7 = **175 applications**

> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "To compare two rates, just compare the total outputs — the bigger number is always better."

> **Why it fails:** Total output comparisons are meaningless when the inputs differ. Store A selling 5 kg for ₱275 and Store B selling 8 kg for ₱416 cannot be compared by total price alone — ₱416 > ₱275 doesn't mean Store B is more expensive because you also get more rice. You MUST compute unit prices (cost per 1 kg) to make a fair comparison.

> **Correct model:** Always convert to unit ratios (divide both terms by the second term) before comparing. The unit ratio expresses "how much per one," which allows direct apples-to-apples comparison regardless of the original quantities.

### Exam Strategies

- **Write the ratio FIRST.** Before doing any math, write the ratio exactly as stated in the problem to confirm order.
- **Scan for unit mismatches.** If you see "hours" and "minutes" in the same problem, convert immediately.
- **Check if the question asks part-to-part or part-to-whole.** The word "total" is your signal; its absence means part-to-part.
- **Verify with mental estimation.** If 3 items cost ₱45, they're ₱15 each. If the answer for 7 items isn't around ₱105, your setup is wrong.
- **Cross-multiply your answer back.** Plug your answer into the original proportion to confirm equality holds.
- **Eliminate obviously wrong choices.** A part-to-whole ratio where the first term exceeds the second is always wrong.

### Memory Aids

- **"Write before you solve"** — Write the ratio in the order given, then calculate. Order errors are the #1 ratio mistake.
- **"Total = Sum of Parts"** — For part-to-part problems, the total is always the sum of the ratio terms.
- **"Cross and Multiply, then Divide"** — a:b = c:d → a×d = b×c → solve for the unknown.
- **"Unit Rate = Divide by the Second"** — To find per-unit cost/speed/rate, divide both terms by the second term.
- **"Same Unit or You're Done"** — If the two quantities aren't in the same unit, convert first.

### Which Method?

For each problem, identify the type and solve.

**1.** 5 kg of rice costs ₱225. Find cost of 12 kg.
- **Type:** Equivalent ratio scaling (direct proportion)
- **Answer:** ₱540
- **Why:** 225÷5=45/kg, 45×12=540. Find the unit rate, then multiply by the desired quantity.

**2.** Ratio of boys to total students is 4:9. There are 180 boys. Find total students.
- **Type:** Part-to-whole
- **Answer:** 405 students
- **Why:** 4 parts = 180, so 1 part = 45. Total = 9 × 45 = 405. The second term represents the total.

**3.** A barangay has 2,400 voters in a 5:3 male:female ratio. How many more males than females?
- **Type:** Part-to-part with difference
- **Answer:** 600
- **Why:** Total parts = 8, 1 part = 300. Difference = 5-3=2 parts = 2×300=600.

**4.** An LGU budget of ₱1,500,000 is split in ratio 3:2 between health and education. How much goes to education?
- **Type:** Part-to-part (find individual share from total)
- **Answer:** ₱600,000
- **Why:** Total parts = 5. 1 part = ₱300,000. Education = 2 × ₱300,000 = ₱600,000.

**5.** A machine fills 200 bottles in 5 hours. How many bottles in 8 hours?
- **Type:** Direct proportion (rate problem)
- **Answer:** 320 bottles
- **Why:** Rate = 200÷5 = 40 bottles/hour. In 8 hours: 40×8 = 320 bottles. The rate per hour is constant.

**6.** Two numbers are in the ratio 7:4. Their difference is 36. Find the larger number.
- **Type:** Part-to-part with difference
- **Answer:** 84
- **Why:** Difference in parts = 7-4 = 3 parts = 36. 1 part = 12. Larger = 7×12 = 84.

### Quick Recap

| Problem Type | Method | Key Check |
|---|---|---|
| Missing value in proportion | Cross-multiply: a×d = b×c | Did I place numbers in correct positions? |
| Part-to-part from total | Total ÷ sum of ratio terms = value of 1 part | Did I add the correct terms for the total? |
| Part-to-whole from part | Scale both terms by same factor | Does the first term represent the part? |
| Difference given | Difference ÷ difference in parts = value of 1 part | Did I subtract parts in the correct order? |
| Unit rate comparison | Divide first term by second term | Are both rates in the same units? |
| Direct proportion | a₁/b₁ = a₂/b₂ (ratio equality) | Do both quantities change in the same direction? |
| Inverse proportion | a₁×b₁ = a₂×b₂ (product equality) | Do quantities change in opposite directions? |
| Unit conversion | Convert to same unit first | Did I convert BOTH quantities? |

### 4.6 Advanced Ratio Word Problems

These problems combine ratio reasoning with other concepts, reflecting the hardest items on the CSE.

#### Problem Type 1: Ratios with Changing Quantities

**Example 7:** The ratio of mangoes to apples in a basket is 5:3. After 12 mangoes are eaten, the ratio becomes 1:1. How many apples are there?

- Let mangoes = 5x, apples = 3x
- After eating 12 mangoes: (5x-12):3x = 1:1
- Cross-multiply: 5x-12 = 3x
- 2x = 12, so x = 6
- Apples = 3 × 6 = **18 apples**

**Example 8:** The ratio of adults to children at a park is 3:2. After 20 more adults arrive, the ratio becomes 5:2. How many children are at the park?

- Let adults = 3x, children = 2x
- After 20 more adults: (3x+20):2x = 5:2
- Cross-multiply: 2(3x+20) = 5(2x) → 6x+40 = 10x
- 4x = 40, so x = 10
- Children = 2 × 10 = **20 children**

#### Problem Type 2: Ratios Involving Ages

**Example 9:** The ratio of Ana's age to Ben's age is 3:4. In 6 years, the ratio will be 4:5. How old is Ana now?

- Let Ana = 3x, Ben = 4x
- In 6 years: (3x+6):(4x+6) = 4:5
- Cross-multiply: 5(3x+6) = 4(4x+6) → 15x+30 = 16x+24
- x = 6
- Ana's age = 3 × 6 = **18 years old**

#### Problem Type 3: Mixture and Solution Problems

**Example 10:** A solution contains alcohol and water in the ratio 2:5. If 140 mL of water is added, the ratio becomes 1:3. What was the original volume of alcohol?

- Let alcohol = 2x, water = 5x
- After adding 140 mL water: 2x:(5x+140) = 1:3
- Cross-multiply: 3(2x) = 1(5x+140) → 6x = 5x+140
- x = 140
- Original alcohol = 2 × 140 = **280 mL**

#### Problem Type 4: Work Rate Combined Problems

**Example 11:** A completes a task in 6 hours. B completes the same task in 8 hours. How long if they work together?

- A's rate = 1/6 of the job per hour
- B's rate = 1/8 of the job per hour
- Combined rate = 1/6 + 1/8 = 4/24 + 3/24 = 7/24 of the job per hour
- Time together = 1 ÷ (7/24) = 24/7 ≈ **3.43 hours** (about 3 hours 26 minutes)

**Example 12:** Pipe A fills a tank in 4 hours. Pipe B fills it in 6 hours. Pipe C drains it in 12 hours. How long to fill with all three open?

- A fills: +1/4 per hour. B fills: +1/6 per hour. C drains: -1/12 per hour
- Net rate = 1/4 + 1/6 - 1/12 = 3/12 + 2/12 - 1/12 = 4/12 = 1/3 per hour
- Time to fill = 1 ÷ (1/3) = **3 hours**

### 4.7 Unit Conversion in Ratio Problems

Many CSE ratio problems test whether you can spot inconsistent units before setting up the ratio.

#### Common Unit Conversions

| From | To | Multiply by |
|------|----|------------|
| hours | minutes | × 60 |
| days | hours | × 24 |
| km | m | × 1,000 |
| kg | g | × 1,000 |
| years | months | × 12 |
| weeks | days | × 7 |
| liters | milliliters | × 1,000 |

#### Conversion Examples

**Example 13:** A car travels 240 km in 3 hours and a motorcycle travels 150 km in 2 hours 30 minutes. Find the ratio of their average speeds.

- Car speed = 240 ÷ 3 = 80 km/h
- Motorcycle time = 2.5 hours (convert 30 min = 0.5 hr)
- Motorcycle speed = 150 ÷ 2.5 = 60 km/h
- Ratio = 80:60 = **4:3**

**Example 14:** A tank holds 2.4 kiloliters of water and 800 liters of oil. What is the ratio of water to oil?

- Convert to same unit: 2.4 kL = 2,400 L
- Ratio = 2,400:800
- Simplify: GCF = 800 → **3:1**

**Example 15:** A runner covers 5 km in 25 minutes. Another runner covers 3 km in 12 minutes. Find the ratio of their speeds.

- Runner 1: 5 km ÷ 25 min = 0.2 km/min
- Runner 2: 3 km ÷ 12 min = 0.25 km/min
- Ratio = 0.2:0.25 = 20:25 = **4:5**

### Check Your Understanding

**1.** If the ratio A:B = 3:2 and after adding 15 to A, the ratio becomes 6:1, what was the original value of B? → **B = 10** (3x+15:2x = 6:1 → 3x+15 = 12x → x=5, B=2×5=10)

**2.** What's the first thing you check when a problem gives "2 hours and 30 minutes"? → **Convert to a single unit** (either 150 minutes or 2.5 hours — inconsistent units cause wrong ratio setup)

**3.** The ratio of savings to expenses is 2:5. If savings increase by ₱3,000 and the new ratio is 1:2, what were the original savings? → **₱12,000** (2x+3000:5x = 1:2 → 4x+6000 = 5x → x=6000, savings=2×6000=12000)

**4.** Pipe A fills in 4 hours, Pipe B fills in 6 hours. How long together? → **2.4 hours** (rates: 1/4 + 1/6 = 5/12 per hour; time = 12/5 = 2.4 hours)

### Connections

How this topic connects to other areas of the CSE:

- **Introduction to Ratios:** The foundational skills of writing ratios, simplifying, and identifying part-to-part vs. part-to-whole are directly applied in every ratio word problem
- **Types of Ratios:** Classifying a ratio as part-to-part, part-to-whole, or unit ratio is the first step in solving any ratio word problem — misclassification leads to wrong answers
- **Direct and Inverse Proportions:** Every proportion problem is a special case of a ratio word problem — direct proportion uses ratio equality, inverse proportion uses product equality
- **Percentages:** Basic percentage problems use the same part-whole relationship as ratio word problems — converting ratios to percentages is a direct application
- **Average Word Problems:** Weighted averages use ratio-based weights to determine contributions — the same proportional reasoning
