# Types of Ratios

## Explanations

### Introduction

A **ratio** is a comparison of two quantities by division. But not all ratios describe the same kind of relationship. The phrase "3 boys for every 5 girls" tells you something fundamentally different from "3 boys out of 8 students" — even though both involve the number 3 and the same classroom. The first compares one part to another part. The second compares one part to the whole. Confusing these two types is one of the most common errors on the Philippine Civil Service Examination.

**Types of Ratios** classifies the different ways ratios express relationships:
- **Part-to-Part Ratios** — comparing one category to another category
- **Part-to-Whole Ratios** — comparing one category to the total
- **Equivalent Ratios** — different expressions of the same proportional relationship
- **Unit Ratios** — ratios where the second term equals 1 (rates "per one")

Understanding ratio types matters because:
- Government budget reports use part-to-whole ratios (allocation vs. total budget)
- Workforce planning uses part-to-part ratios (employees vs. supervisors)
- Procurement uses unit ratios (cost per unit, items per box)
- Engineering and transportation use equivalent ratios (map scales, speed conversions)
- The CSE frequently tests whether examinees can distinguish between ratio types and interpret them correctly in word problems

### Why Ratio Types Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Exam tests ratio types because:
- Government employees interpret statistical reports that present data as ratios — population density, teacher-to-student ratios, budget allocation percentages
- Public servants must distinguish "3 out of 10 employees are supervisors" (part-to-whole) from "for every 3 supervisors there are 7 staff" (part-to-part)
- Procurement officers compare unit prices (₱45 per ream vs. ₱50 per ream) — a unit ratio skill
- Budget analysts scale ratios up or down (equivalent ratios) when projecting costs across departments
- Misinterpreting a ratio type leads to incorrect calculations, flawed reports, and audit findings
- Ratio-type questions appear in approximately 15–20% of the Numerical Ability section

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Confusing part-to-part with part-to-whole** — reading "ratio of boys to total students" and writing boys:girls instead
2. **Reversing the order** — the question asks for girls:boys but the examinee writes boys:girls
3. **Forgetting to compute the whole** — when given parts (3 boys, 5 girls), failing to recognize the whole is 8
4. **Incorrect simplification** — dividing only one term of the ratio instead of both
5. **Scaling errors with equivalent ratios** — multiplying the first term but forgetting the second
6. **Misidentifying unit ratios** — confusing "per one" with "per hundred" (percentage)
7. **Adding ratio terms to find totals incorrectly** — assuming a 2:3 ratio means exactly 2 and 3 items exist, rather than multiples
8. **Ignoring context clues in wording** — missing keywords like "out of," "for every," "per," "to the total"

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Identify and classify ratios as part-to-part, part-to-whole, equivalent, or unit ratios
- Distinguish part-to-part from part-to-whole ratios based on problem wording
- Generate and recognize equivalent ratios through multiplication and division
- Interpret unit ratios and apply them to rate comparisons
- Compare different ratio types and convert between them when appropriate
- Apply ratio-type knowledge to realistic CSE scenarios involving budgets, workforce, inventory, and statistics
- Solve ratio-type questions efficiently under exam time pressure

### 4.1 Overview of Ratio Types
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** A part-to-part ratio compares two disjoint subsets — neither includes the other. The two parts together may or may not equal the whole (there could be a third category). This is why adding the terms of a part-to-part ratio only gives you the total when exactly two categories cover everything. Understanding this distinction is what separates correct from incorrect answers on the CSE.


A **ratio** compares two quantities using division. The way we classify a ratio depends on *what* is being compared:

| Ratio Type | What It Compares | Example |
|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| Part-to-Part | One group to another group | boys:girls = 3:5 |
| Part-to-Whole | One group to the total | boys:total = 3:8 |
| Equivalent | Same relationship, different numbers | 3:5 = 6:10 = 9:15 |
| Unit | Any quantity to 1 | 60 km per 1 hour |

#### Why Classification Matters

Consider this scenario: A government office has 12 male employees and 18 female employees.

- **Part-to-part:** male:female = 12:18 = 2:3
- **Part-to-whole:** male:total = 12:30 = 2:5
- **Part-to-whole:** female:total = 18:30 = 3:5

If a report asks "What fraction of employees are male?" and you mistakenly use the part-to-part ratio 2:3, you'd answer 2/3 — which is wrong. The correct answer uses the part-to-whole ratio: 2/5.

This single misclassification error changes the answer entirely. The CSE exploits this confusion deliberately.

#### Identifying the Quantities Being Compared

To classify a ratio correctly, ask two questions:
1. **What are the two quantities?** (Identify both terms)
2. **Is either quantity the total of all parts?** (If yes → part-to-whole; if no → part-to-part)

**Keywords that signal part-to-part:**
- "ratio of A to B"
- "for every A there are B"
- "A compared to B"
- "A versus B"

**Keywords that signal part-to-whole:**
- "A out of the total"
- "fraction of the whole"
- "A out of all"
- "proportion of A in the group"
- "percentage of A"

**Keywords that signal unit ratios:**
- "per"
- "each"
- "for every one"
- "rate"


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "If the ratio of boys to girls is 2:3, then 2/3 of the students are boys."

> **Why it fails:** This confuses part-to-part with part-to-whole. The ratio 2:3 compares boys to girls — neither is the total. Since 2+3=5 parts total, boys represent 2/5 (40%) of the students, not 2/3 (67%). This error would overstate the boy population by 27 percentage points.

> **Correct model:** When you see a ratio written as A:B, always check whether the second term is another part or the total. Part-to-part ratios require you to ADD the parts to find the whole before computing any fraction or percentage.

### 4.2 Part-to-Part Ratios

A **part-to-part ratio** compares one subset of a group to another subset of the same group. Neither term represents the total.

#### Definition

```
Part-to-Part Ratio = Category A : Category B
```

Both A and B are parts of a larger whole, but the ratio does NOT include the whole.

#### Examples

**Classroom:**
- A class has 14 boys and 16 girls → boys:girls = 14:16 = 7:8
- This tells you: for every 7 boys, there are 8 girls

**Workplace:**
- An office has 5 supervisors and 20 staff → supervisors:staff = 5:20 = 1:4
- This tells you: for every 1 supervisor, there are 4 staff members

**Sports:**
- A basketball team won 18 games and lost 6 → wins:losses = 18:6 = 3:1
- This tells you: for every 3 wins, there is 1 loss

**Survey:**
- In a poll, 120 people agreed and 80 disagreed → agree:disagree = 120:80 = 3:2
- This tells you: for every 3 who agreed, 2 disagreed

**Inventory:**
- A warehouse has 200 boxes of paper and 50 boxes of ink → paper:ink = 200:50 = 4:1
- This tells you: for every 4 boxes of paper, there is 1 box of ink

#### Key Characteristics of Part-to-Part Ratios

1. **Neither term is the total** — both terms are subgroups
2. **The sum of the terms does NOT necessarily equal the whole** — there may be other categories not mentioned
3. **Order matters** — boys:girls ≠ girls:boys (unless they're equal)
4. **Can be simplified** — divide both terms by their GCF

#### How to Simplify Part-to-Part Ratios

Find the Greatest Common Factor (GCF) of both terms and divide:

```
Example: 24:36
GCF of 24 and 36 = 12
24 ÷ 12 = 2
36 ÷ 12 = 3
Simplified: 2:3
```

#### Visual Representation

Imagine 3 red balls and 5 blue balls:

```
🔴🔴🔴  vs  🔵🔵🔵🔵🔵

Part-to-Part: red:blue = 3:5
```

The ratio 3:5 tells you the relationship between red and blue — it says nothing about the total (which is 8).

#### Practice Interpretation

| Situation | Part-to-Part Ratio | Interpretation |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------|
| 10 cars, 6 motorcycles | cars:motorcycles = 5:3 | For every 5 cars, there are 3 motorcycles |
| 45 passed, 15 failed | passed:failed = 3:1 | For every 3 who passed, 1 failed |
| ₱8,000 food, ₱2,000 transport | food:transport = 4:1 | For every ₱4 on food, ₱1 goes to transport |


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "6:10 and 3:5 are different ratios because the numbers are different."

> **Why it fails:** A ratio is about the *relationship*, not the specific numbers. Both 6:10 and 3:5 represent the same proportional relationship — the first term is 0.6 times the second term in both cases. In a classroom, "6 boys for every 10 girls" and "3 boys for every 5 girls" describe the exact same situation at different scales.

> **Correct model:** Two ratios are equivalent if they can be reduced to the same simplified form OR if cross-multiplication produces equal products. The numbers themselves can be completely different while describing identical proportions.

### 4.3 Part-to-Whole Ratios
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Equivalent ratios preserve the proportional relationship because multiplying or dividing both terms by the same number (k) is equivalent to multiplying by k/k = 1 — you're not changing the value of the ratio, just expressing it differently. Cross-multiplication works because a:b = c:d means a/b = c/d, and multiplying both sides by b×d gives a×d = b×c.


A **part-to-whole ratio** compares one subset to the entire group (the total of all subsets combined).

#### Definition

```
Part-to-Whole Ratio = Category A : Total
where Total = Category A + Category B + Category C + ...
```

#### The Critical Step: Finding the Whole

The most common error with part-to-whole ratios is computing the "whole" incorrectly. The whole is the sum of ALL parts — not just the two mentioned.

**Example:** A class has 14 boys and 16 girls.
- Total students = 14 + 16 = 30
- Part-to-whole (boys) = 14:30 = 7:15
- Part-to-whole (girls) = 16:30 = 8:15

**Example with multiple categories:** A store sells 40 shirts, 25 pants, and 35 shoes.
- Total items = 40 + 25 + 35 = 100
- Shirts:total = 40:100 = 2:5
- Pants:total = 25:100 = 1:4
- Shoes:total = 35:100 = 7:20

#### Relationship to Fractions and Percentages

Part-to-whole ratios directly convert to fractions and percentages:

```
boys:total = 14:30 = 7:15

As a fraction: 7/15
As a percentage: 7 ÷ 15 × 100 = 46.67%
```

This is why part-to-whole ratios are so important — they are the bridge between ratios and percentages.

#### Examples by Context

**Classroom:**
- 25 students present out of 30 enrolled → present:total = 25:30 = 5:6
- Interpretation: 5 out of every 6 enrolled students are present

**Budget:**
- ₱15,000 allocated to transportation out of ₱60,000 total budget
- Transport:total = 15,000:60,000 = 1:4
- Interpretation: 1 out of every 4 pesos goes to transportation (25%)

**Population:**
- 3,500 registered voters out of 10,000 residents
- Voters:residents = 3,500:10,000 = 7:20
- Interpretation: 7 out of every 20 residents are registered voters

**Inventory:**
- 80 defective items out of 2,000 produced
- Defective:total = 80:2,000 = 1:25
- Interpretation: 1 out of every 25 items is defective

#### Comparison Table: Part-to-Part vs. Part-to-Whole

| Aspect | Part-to-Part | Part-to-Whole |
|--------|-------------|---------------|
| Compares | Group A to Group B | Group A to Total |
| Total included? | No | Yes (as second term) |
| Converts to fraction? | Not directly | Yes: A/Total |
| Sum of terms = whole? | Not necessarily | First term + others = second term |
| Example | boys:girls = 3:5 | boys:total = 3:8 |

#### Converting Between Part-to-Part and Part-to-Whole

If you know the part-to-part ratio, you can find the part-to-whole ratio:

```
Given: boys:girls = 3:5
Total parts = 3 + 5 = 8
boys:total = 3:8
girls:total = 5:8
```

If you know the part-to-whole ratio, you can find the part-to-part ratio:

```
Given: boys:total = 3:8
Girls parts = 8 - 3 = 5
boys:girls = 3:5
```

⚠️ **Warning:** This conversion only works when there are exactly TWO categories. If there are three or more categories, you need all part-to-whole ratios to reconstruct part-to-part ratios.

### 4.4 Equivalent Ratios

**Equivalent ratios** are different ratio expressions that represent the same proportional relationship. They are created by multiplying or dividing both terms by the same non-zero number.

#### Definition

```
If a:b is a ratio, then (a×k):(b×k) is an equivalent ratio for any k ≠ 0
```

#### Generating Equivalent Ratios

**By multiplication (scaling up):**
```
2:3 → multiply both by 2 → 4:6
2:3 → multiply both by 3 → 6:9
2:3 → multiply both by 5 → 10:15
2:3 → multiply both by 10 → 20:30
```

**By division (simplifying):**
```
20:30 → divide both by 2 → 10:15
20:30 → divide both by 5 → 4:6
20:30 → divide both by 10 → 2:3
```

#### How to Check if Two Ratios Are Equivalent

**Method 1: Cross-multiplication**
```
Are 3:4 and 9:12 equivalent?
3 × 12 = 36
4 × 9 = 36
36 = 36 ✓ → They are equivalent
```

**Method 2: Simplify both to lowest terms**
```
3:4 → already in lowest terms (GCF = 1)
9:12 → divide both by 3 → 3:4
Both simplify to 3:4 ✓ → They are equivalent
```

**Method 3: Check the multiplier**
```
3:4 and 9:12
9 ÷ 3 = 3 (multiplier)
12 ÷ 4 = 3 (same multiplier)
Same multiplier ✓ → They are equivalent
```

#### Ratio Tables

A ratio table shows a series of equivalent ratios:

| × | First Term | Second Term |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| ×1 | 2 | 5 |
| ×2 | 4 | 10 |
| ×3 | 6 | 15 |
| ×4 | 8 | 20 |
| ×5 | 10 | 25 |

All rows represent the same ratio: 2:5

#### Relationship to Fractions

Equivalent ratios work exactly like equivalent fractions:
```
2:5 = 4:10 = 6:15
2/5 = 4/10 = 6/15
```

This connection is critical — if you can find equivalent fractions, you can find equivalent ratios.

#### Practical Applications

**Map scales:** A map scale of 1:50,000 means 1 cm on the map = 50,000 cm (500 m) in reality. Equivalent: 2 cm = 100,000 cm (1 km).

**Recipes:** A recipe calls for flour:sugar = 3:1. To triple the recipe: 9:3 (still equivalent to 3:1).

**Workforce planning:** If the ideal teacher:student ratio is 1:25, then for 100 students you need 4 teachers (4:100 = 1:25).

#### Common Errors with Equivalent Ratios

1. **Adding instead of multiplying:** 2:3 → adding 2 to both → 4:5 ❌ (This changes the ratio!)
2. **Multiplying only one term:** 2:3 → 4:3 ❌ (Must multiply BOTH terms)
3. **Using different multipliers:** 2:3 → 4:9 ❌ (Used ×2 for first, ×3 for second)

### 4.5 Unit Ratios
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** A unit ratio normalizes the second quantity to 1 so you can compare "apples to apples." Dividing both terms by the second term's value is the same operation you use to convert any ratio to a unit ratio: a:b becomes (a÷b):1. This works because division by the same number preserves proportionality — exactly the same principle behind equivalent ratios.


A **unit ratio** is a ratio where the second term equals 1. It expresses "how much of the first quantity corresponds to exactly one of the second quantity."

#### Definition

```
Unit Ratio = a : 1
```

This is often read as "a per one" or simply "a per [unit]."

#### How to Find a Unit Ratio

Divide both terms by the second term:

```
Example: 240 km in 4 hours
Ratio: 240:4
Divide both by 4: 60:1
Unit ratio: 60 km per 1 hour (60 km/h)
```

```
Example: ₱150 for 3 notebooks
Ratio: 150:3
Divide both by 3: 50:1
Unit ratio: ₱50 per 1 notebook
```

#### Unit Ratios as Rates

When a unit ratio involves two different units of measurement, it becomes a **rate**:

| Situation | Ratio | Unit Ratio (Rate) |
|-----------|-------|-------------------|
| 300 km in 5 hours | 300:5 | 60 km per hour |
| ₱450 for 9 items | 450:9 | ₱50 per item |
| 120 pages in 4 days | 120:4 | 30 pages per day |
| 500 students for 20 teachers | 500:20 | 25 students per teacher |
| ₱36,000 salary for 12 months | 36,000:12 | ₱3,000 per month |

#### Why Unit Ratios Are Useful

Unit ratios make comparison easy:

**Which is cheaper?**
- Store A: ₱180 for 6 pens → ₱30 per pen
- Store B: ₱200 for 8 pens → ₱25 per pen
- Store B is cheaper (lower unit price)

**Which car is more fuel-efficient?**
- Car A: 480 km on 40 liters → 12 km per liter
- Car B: 350 km on 25 liters → 14 km per liter
- Car B is more efficient (more km per liter)

#### Unit Ratios in Government Work

- **Cost per beneficiary:** ₱5,000,000 program budget ÷ 2,000 beneficiaries = ₱2,500 per beneficiary
- **Processing rate:** 150 applications processed in 5 days = 30 applications per day
- **Population density:** 50,000 people in 25 sq km = 2,000 people per sq km
- **Teacher-student ratio:** 800 students with 32 teachers = 25 students per teacher

#### Relationship Between Unit Ratios and Other Types

A unit ratio is simply any ratio simplified so the second term becomes 1:
- Part-to-part: 3:5 → as unit ratio: 0.6:1
- Part-to-whole: 3:8 → as unit ratio: 0.375:1

However, unit ratios are most commonly used for rates (different units), not for part-to-part or part-to-whole comparisons.

**2.** A car travels 450 km on 30 liters. What is the unit ratio? → **15:1, or 15 km per liter (divide both terms by 30: 450÷30=15, 30÷30=1)**

**3.** What separates a unit ratio from a regular simplified ratio? → **The second term must be exactly 1 (a simplified ratio like 2:3 is not a unit ratio because the second term is not 1)**

### 4.6 Comparing Different Types of Ratios

The same real-world situation can be described using different ratio types. Recognizing which type is being asked for is the key skill tested on the CSE.

#### Same Situation, Different Ratios

**Scenario:** A department has 8 engineers and 12 administrative staff.

| Ratio Type | Expression | Simplified | Interpretation |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|----------------|
| Part-to-part | engineers:admin = 8:12 | 2:3 | For every 2 engineers, there are 3 admin staff |
| Part-to-whole (eng) | engineers:total = 8:20 | 2:5 | 2 out of every 5 employees are engineers |
| Part-to-whole (admin) | admin:total = 12:20 | 3:5 | 3 out of every 5 employees are admin staff |
| Unit (eng per admin) | 8:12 → 0.67:1 | 0.67:1 | 0.67 engineers per 1 admin staff |

#### Wording Analysis

The exam tests your ability to interpret wording precisely:

| Question Wording | Ratio Type | Answer |
|-----------------|-----------|--------|
| "What is the ratio of boys to girls?" | Part-to-part | boys:girls |
| "What fraction of students are boys?" | Part-to-whole | boys/total |
| "What is the ratio of girls to all students?" | Part-to-whole | girls:total |
| "How many boys are there per girl?" | Unit ratio | boys÷girls : 1 |
| "Express 6:10 in simplest form" | Equivalent | 3:5 |

#### Common Confusion Points

1. **"Ratio of A to B" vs. "Ratio of A to the total"**
   - "Ratio of passed to failed" → part-to-part
   - "Ratio of passed to all examinees" → part-to-whole

2. **"For every" can signal either type:**
   - "For every 3 boys there are 5 girls" → part-to-part (3:5)
   - "For every 8 students, 3 are boys" → part-to-whole (3:8)

3. **Fractions always imply part-to-whole:**
   - "What fraction are boys?" → boys/total (part-to-whole)
   - Never interpret a fraction question as part-to-part

#### Side-by-Side Drill

**Given:** 15 passed, 5 failed an exam.

| Question | Type | Answer |
|----------|------|--------|
| Ratio of passed to failed? | Part-to-part | 15:5 = 3:1 |
| Ratio of failed to passed? | Part-to-part | 5:15 = 1:3 |
| What fraction passed? | Part-to-whole | 15/20 = 3/4 |
| What fraction failed? | Part-to-whole | 5/20 = 1/4 |
| How many passed per 1 who failed? | Unit ratio | 3:1 |
| Is 15:5 equivalent to 6:2? | Equivalent check | Yes (both = 3:1) |

### 4.7 Real-Life Applications of Ratio Types

#### Classroom Statistics
- Teacher:student ratio (part-to-part) — used for class size planning
- Students who passed:total students (part-to-whole) — used for performance reports
- Books per student (unit ratio) — used for resource allocation

#### Engineering and Construction
- Cement:sand:gravel = 1:2:4 (part-to-part-to-part) — concrete mixing
- Completed work:total work = 3:5 (part-to-whole) — project progress
- Meters per second (unit ratio) — flow rates

#### Transportation
- Distance:time = 120 km:2 hours → 60 km/h (unit ratio)
- Cars:motorcycles on a road (part-to-part) — traffic analysis
- Public transport users:total commuters (part-to-whole) — modal share

#### Inventory Management
- Defective:non-defective items (part-to-part) — quality control
- Defective:total produced (part-to-whole) — defect rate
- Items per box (unit ratio) — packaging standards

#### Budget and Finance
- Salary:total expenses (part-to-whole) — budget allocation
- Income:expenses (part-to-part) — financial health
- Cost per unit (unit ratio) — procurement decisions
- Savings:income = 2:10 = 1:5 (part-to-whole) — savings rate

#### Government and Population Data
- Male:female population (part-to-part) — demographic analysis
- Employed:total working-age population (part-to-whole) — employment rate
- People per square kilometer (unit ratio) — population density
- Registered voters:total residents (part-to-whole) — voter registration rate

#### Sports Analytics
- Wins:losses (part-to-part) — team performance
- Wins:total games (part-to-whole) — win percentage
- Points per game (unit ratio) — player efficiency

### 4.9 Problem-Solving Strategies

#### Step-by-Step System for Ratio-Type Questions

**Step 1: Identify the quantities.**
Read the problem and list all quantities mentioned.

**Step 2: Determine what is being compared.**
- Are you comparing one group to another group? → Part-to-part
- Are you comparing one group to the total? → Part-to-whole
- Are you finding a "per one" value? → Unit ratio
- Are you checking if two ratios are the same? → Equivalent ratio

**Step 3: Set up the ratio in correct order.**
The order mentioned in the question is the order of the ratio. "Ratio of A to B" means A:B, not B:A.

**Step 4: Simplify if needed.**
Divide both terms by their GCF.

**Step 5: Verify your answer.**
- Does the ratio type match what was asked?
- Is the order correct?
- Is it fully simplified?
- Do the numbers make sense in context?

#### Quick Verification Methods

**For part-to-whole:** The first term must be LESS than the second term (a part cannot exceed the whole).

**For equivalent ratios:** Cross-multiply — if the products are equal, the ratios are equivalent.

**For unit ratios:** The second term must equal 1.

**For part-to-part:** Neither term needs to be larger than the other.

#### Elimination Strategies for Multiple Choice

1. **If the question asks for part-to-whole**, eliminate any choice where the first term is larger than the second
2. **If the question asks for a simplified ratio**, eliminate choices that can still be reduced
3. **If the question involves "per one"**, eliminate choices where the second term ≠ 1
4. **Check for reversed ratios** — the exam often includes the correct numbers in wrong order as a distractor

### 4.10 Estimation and Mental Math Techniques

#### Recognizing Common Equivalent Forms

Memorize these common ratio families for instant recognition:

```
1:2 = 2:4 = 3:6 = 4:8 = 5:10 = 50:100
1:3 = 2:6 = 3:9 = 4:12 = 5:15
1:4 = 2:8 = 3:12 = 4:16 = 5:20 = 25:100
1:5 = 2:10 = 3:15 = 4:20 = 5:25 = 20:100
2:3 = 4:6 = 6:9 = 8:12 = 10:15
3:4 = 6:8 = 9:12 = 12:16 = 15:20 = 75:100
3:5 = 6:10 = 9:15 = 12:20 = 15:25 = 60:100
```

#### Estimating Unit Ratios Mentally

**Trick: Round to friendly numbers first, then divide.**

- 247 km in 4 hours → round to 240 ÷ 4 = 60 km/h (actual: 61.75)
- ₱485 for 5 items → round to 500 ÷ 5 = ₱100 per item (actual: ₱97)
- 312 students with 12 teachers → 312 ÷ 12 = 26 students per teacher

**Trick: Use halving for even numbers.**

- 180:4 → halve both → 90:2 → halve again → 45:1

**Trick: Factor out 10s.**

- 350:70 → divide both by 10 → 35:7 → divide by 7 → 5:1

#### Identifying Ratio Patterns Quickly

**Pattern: If both terms end in 0, divide by 10 first.**
- 120:80 → 12:8 → 3:2

**Pattern: If both terms are even, halve repeatedly.**
- 48:36 → 24:18 → 12:9 → 4:3

**Pattern: If one term divides the other evenly, the ratio simplifies to n:1 or 1:n.**
- 75:25 → 3:1 (since 75 ÷ 25 = 3)

### 4.11 Common Errors in Types of Ratios

#### Error 1: Confusing Part-to-Part with Part-to-Whole

**Problem:** A class has 12 boys and 18 girls. What is the ratio of boys to the total?

❌ **Wrong:** 12:18 = 2:3 (This is boys:girls — part-to-part!)
✅ **Correct:** Total = 12 + 18 = 30. Boys:total = 12:30 = 2:5

#### Error 2: Reversing Ratio Order

**Problem:** What is the ratio of losses to wins if a team won 15 and lost 5?

❌ **Wrong:** 15:5 = 3:1 (This is wins:losses — reversed!)
✅ **Correct:** losses:wins = 5:15 = 1:3

#### Error 3: Identifying the Wrong "Whole"

**Problem:** In a bag of 10 red, 15 blue, and 25 green marbles, what is the ratio of red to total?

❌ **Wrong:** 10:15 (used only red and blue, forgot green)
✅ **Correct:** Total = 10 + 15 + 25 = 50. Red:total = 10:50 = 1:5

#### Error 4: Incorrect Scaling

**Problem:** If 2:3 is equivalent to ?:12, find the missing value.

❌ **Wrong:** 2 + 9 = 11 (added instead of multiplied)
✅ **Correct:** 3 × 4 = 12, so multiply first term by 4: 2 × 4 = 8. Answer: 8:12

#### Error 5: Dividing Only One Term

**Problem:** Simplify 24:36.

❌ **Wrong:** 24:36 → divide first by 6 → 4:36 (only divided one term!)
✅ **Correct:** 24 ÷ 12 = 2, 36 ÷ 12 = 3. Answer: 2:3

#### Error 6: Misunderstanding Unit Ratios

**Problem:** A car travels 180 km in 3 hours. What is the unit ratio?

❌ **Wrong:** 3:180 = 1:60 (reversed — this gives hours per km)
✅ **Correct:** 180:3 = 60:1 → 60 km per hour

### Step-by-Step Rules

### Ratio Classification Procedure

1. Read the problem and identify the two quantities being compared
2. Ask: "Is either quantity the TOTAL of all categories?"
   - YES → Part-to-whole ratio
   - NO → Continue to step 3
3. Ask: "Are both quantities subgroups of a larger set?"
   - YES → Part-to-part ratio
4. Ask: "Is the second quantity equal to 1 (or should it be converted to 1)?"
   - YES → Unit ratio
5. Ask: "Am I checking if two ratios represent the same relationship?"
   - YES → Equivalent ratio check

### Part-to-Whole Identification Method

1. Identify all categories mentioned in the problem
2. Add all categories to find the total
3. The ratio is: specified category : total
4. Simplify by dividing both terms by their GCF
5. Verify: first term must be ≤ second term

### Equivalent Ratio Procedure

**To generate:** Multiply (or divide) BOTH terms by the same number.

**To verify:** Use any of these methods:
- Cross-multiply: if a×d = b×c, then a:b = c:d
- Simplify both to lowest terms and compare
- Check that the same multiplier converts one ratio to the other

### Unit Ratio Procedure

1. Write the ratio as first quantity : second quantity
2. Divide BOTH terms by the second term
3. Result: (first ÷ second) : 1
4. Express as "[value] per [unit]"

### Answer Verification System

After solving, check:
- ✅ Did I identify the correct ratio type?
- ✅ Is the order of terms correct (matches the question)?
- ✅ Is the ratio fully simplified?
- ✅ Does the answer make logical sense?
- ✅ For part-to-whole: is the first term ≤ second term?
- ✅ For unit ratios: is the second term = 1?

### Exam Strategies

### Identifying Ratio Types Instantly

Train yourself to spot keywords:
- **"to"** between two groups → likely part-to-part
- **"out of"** or **"of the total"** → part-to-whole
- **"per"** or **"each"** or **"every one"** → unit ratio
- **"same as"** or **"equivalent to"** → equivalent ratio
- **"fraction"** or **"percentage"** → part-to-whole

### Simplifying Mentally

- Both even? Halve both.
- Both end in 0? Remove zeros.
- Both divisible by 5? Divide by 5.
- One divides the other? Result is n:1.
- Recognize common GCFs: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12

### Checking Ratio Order Carefully

The CSE loves to include the correct ratio in reversed order as a distractor. ALWAYS re-read the question to confirm which quantity comes first.

"Ratio of girls to boys" → girls:boys (NOT boys:girls)

### Eliminating Unreasonable Choices

- A part-to-whole ratio cannot have a first term > second term
- A simplified ratio cannot have both terms divisible by the same number > 1
- A unit ratio must have 1 as the second term
- Equivalent ratios must cross-multiply to equal products

### Solving Efficiently Under Time Pressure

1. Read the question ONCE carefully — identify the ratio type from keywords
2. Extract numbers — write them in ratio form immediately
3. Simplify — use the fastest method (halving, dividing by obvious GCF)
4. Match to answer choices — don't over-simplify if the choices show a specific form
5. Move on — don't second-guess if your verification checks pass

### Real CSE-Like Examples

### Easy Examples

**Example 1:**
A box contains 6 red balls and 4 blue balls. What is the ratio of red to blue balls?

- Type: Part-to-part
- Ratio: 6:4 = 3:2
- Answer: 3:2
- Explanation: We compare one group (red) to another group (blue), not to the total.

**Example 2:**
In a class of 40 students, 15 are boys. What is the ratio of boys to total students?

- Type: Part-to-whole
- Ratio: 15:40 = 3:8
- Answer: 3:8
- Explanation: We compare boys (a part) to total students (the whole).

**Example 3:**
A printer prints 120 pages in 4 minutes. What is the printing rate per minute?

- Type: Unit ratio
- Ratio: 120:4 = 30:1
- Answer: 30 pages per minute
- Explanation: We divide both terms by 4 to get the "per one" rate.

### Medium Examples

**Example 4:**
A survey of 200 employees found that 80 prefer morning shifts and 120 prefer afternoon shifts. What fraction of employees prefer morning shifts?

- Type: Part-to-whole
- Ratio: 80:200 = 2:5
- Answer: 2/5
- Explanation: "What fraction" signals part-to-whole. The whole is 200 (all employees).

**Example 5:**
If the ratio of teachers to students is 2:25, how many teachers are needed for 150 students?

- Type: Equivalent ratio
- Setup: 2:25 = ?:150
- Multiplier: 150 ÷ 25 = 6
- Answer: 2 × 6 = 12 teachers
- Explanation: We scale the ratio by the same factor to maintain equivalence.

**Example 6:**
A department has 6 managers, 18 senior staff, and 36 junior staff. What is the ratio of managers to all employees?

- Type: Part-to-whole
- Total: 6 + 18 + 36 = 60
- Ratio: 6:60 = 1:10
- Answer: 1:10
- Explanation: The "whole" includes ALL three categories, not just managers and one other group.

### Hard Examples

**Example 7:**
In a government office, the ratio of male to female employees is 5:7. If there are 84 female employees, what fraction of the total workforce is male?

- Step 1: male:female = 5:7, females = 84
- Step 2: 7 parts = 84, so 1 part = 12
- Step 3: males = 5 × 12 = 60
- Step 4: total = 60 + 84 = 144
- Step 5: male:total = 60:144 = 5:12
- Answer: 5/12
- Explanation: Convert from part-to-part to part-to-whole by finding actual values first.

**Example 8:**
Store A sells 5 kg of rice for ₱275. Store B sells 8 kg for ₱416. Which store offers the better unit price?

- Store A: ₱275 ÷ 5 = ₱55 per kg
- Store B: ₱416 ÷ 8 = ₱52 per kg
- Answer: Store B (₱52/kg < ₱55/kg)
- Explanation: Convert both to unit ratios (price per 1 kg) to compare directly.

**Example 9:**
The ratio of passed to failed examinees is 7:3. If 420 examinees passed, what is the ratio of failed examinees to the total number of examinees?

- Step 1: passed:failed = 7:3, passed = 420
- Step 2: 7 parts = 420, so 1 part = 60
- Step 3: failed = 3 × 60 = 180
- Step 4: total = 420 + 180 = 600
- Step 5: failed:total = 180:600 = 3:10
- Answer: 3:10
- Explanation: Start with part-to-part, find actual values, then express as part-to-whole.

### Which Method?

For each problem, identify the ratio type and solve.

**1.** A survey shows 120 agree and 80 disagree. What is agree:total?
- **Type:** Part-to-whole (agree compared to total respondents)
- **Answer:** 120:200 = 3:5
- **Why:** Total = 120+80 = 200. agree:total = 120:200, simplify by GCF 40 → 3:5.

**2.** Which of these is equivalent to 5:8? (a) 10:16 (b) 15:20 (c) 25:32
- **Type:** Equivalent ratio identification (test each option)
- **Answer:** (a) 10:16
- **Why:** 5×2=10, 8×2=16 ✓. (b) 15:20 → ×3 and ×2.5 — not same multiplier. (c) 25:32 → ×5 and ×4 — not same multiplier.

**3.** A printer produces 1,440 pages in 12 minutes. What is the printing rate per minute?
- **Type:** Unit ratio (rate per one minute)
- **Answer:** 120 pages per minute
- **Why:** 1,440:12 → divide both by 12 → 120:1. Unit ratio = 120 pages per minute.

**4.** In a barangay of 500 residents, the ratio of registered to unregistered voters is 3:2. How many are registered?
- **Type:** Part-to-part with scaling (find actual values from ratio)
- **Answer:** 300 registered voters
- **Why:** Total parts = 5. 1 part = 500÷5 = 100. Registered = 3×100 = 300.

**5.** A recipe uses 3 cups of flour for every 2 cups of sugar. If you use 12 cups of flour, how much sugar?
- **Type:** Equivalent ratio (scaling up from given ratio)
- **Answer:** 8 cups
- **Why:** 3:2 = 12:? → multiplier = 12÷3 = 4 → sugar = 2×4 = 8.

**6.** The ratio of boys to total students is 2:5. If there are 30 boys, how many are NOT boys?
- **Type:** Part-to-whole with reverse calculation
- **Answer:** 45 non-boys (girls + others)
- **Why:** 2 parts = 30, so 1 part = 15. Total = 5×15 = 75. Non-boys = 75-30 = 45.

### Quick Recap

| Ratio Type | Definition | Key Signal Words | Example |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| Part-to-Part | Compares one group to another group | "A to B," "for every A there are B" | boys:girls = 3:5 |
| Part-to-Whole | Compares one group to the total | "out of," "fraction of," "of the total" | boys:total = 3:8 |
| Equivalent | Same relationship, different numbers | "same as," "equivalent to," "simplify" | 6:10 = 3:5 |
| Unit | Quantity per one unit | "per," "each," "rate" | 60 km per hour |

**Key Relationships:**
- Part-to-part can be converted to part-to-whole by adding terms for the total
- Part-to-whole ratios directly convert to fractions and percentages
- Equivalent ratios are generated by multiplying/dividing both terms equally
- Unit ratios are found by dividing both terms by the second term

### Memory Aids

### Ratio Identification Tricks

**"P-P-P" Rule:** Part-to-Part compares **P**iece to **P**iece (neither is the total)

**"P-W = Part of Whole":** If you see "out of" or "total" — it's Part-to-Whole

**"U = 1":** Unit ratios always have **1** as the second term — think "**U**no"

**"E = Equal scaling":** Equivalent ratios use **E**qual multipliers on both terms

### Part vs. Whole Reminders

- If the second term is the SUM of all groups → Part-to-Whole
- If the second term is just ONE other group → Part-to-Part
- When in doubt, ask: "Can I add the two terms to get a meaningful total?"
  - If YES and that total makes sense as "all items" → you might be looking at part-to-part terms that sum to the whole
  - If the second term already IS the total → Part-to-Whole

### Equivalent Ratio Shortcuts

- **Double-check rule:** Whatever you do to one term, do to the other
- **Cross-multiply test:** a:b = c:d if and only if a×d = b×c
- **GCF shortcut:** Both terms divisible by the same number? Divide both. Repeat until you can't.

### Unit Ratio Memory Hacks

- "Per" = "÷ by the second number"
- ₱ per item = total cost ÷ number of items
- km per hour = total distance ÷ total time
- students per teacher = total students ÷ total teachers

### Exam-Time Reminders

1. READ the question twice — what type of ratio is being asked?
2. ORDER matters — "A to B" means A:B, not B:A
3. TOTAL = sum of ALL parts (don't forget hidden categories)
4. SIMPLIFY — always reduce to lowest terms unless told otherwise
5. CHECK — does your answer type match the question type?
