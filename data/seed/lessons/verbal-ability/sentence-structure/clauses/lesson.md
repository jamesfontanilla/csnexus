# Clauses

## Explanations

### Introduction

A **clause** is a group of words that contains both a subject and a verb. Clauses are the fundamental building blocks of sentences — every sentence you read or write is made up of at least one clause. Understanding clauses is essential because they determine whether a sentence is grammatically complete, how ideas relate to each other, and how meaning is structured in professional communication.

### Why Clauses Matter

Clauses are the engine of sentence construction. They determine:
- Whether a group of words is a **complete sentence** or a **fragment**
- How ideas are **connected** within complex sentences
- The **logical relationships** between thoughts (cause, contrast, condition, time)
- The **grammatical roles** that groups of words play (subject, object, modifier)

In government writing — memoranda, policy documents, legal texts, and official correspondence — clause mastery separates clear, authoritative prose from ambiguous, error-prone writing.

### Why Clauses Are Tested in the CSE

The Philippine Civil Service Examination tests clause knowledge because:
- Identifying sentence fragments and run-on sentences requires clause analysis
- Complex government documents use multiple clause types in every paragraph
- Correct punctuation depends on understanding clause boundaries
- Many grammar questions test whether examinees can distinguish complete from incomplete thoughts
- Sentence correction items often hinge on clause structure errors
- Reading comprehension passages use complex clause arrangements that require parsing skill

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Confusing clauses with phrases** — a phrase lacks a subject-verb pair; a clause always has one
2. **Treating dependent clauses as complete sentences** (sentence fragments)
3. **Joining independent clauses without proper punctuation** (run-on sentences and comma splices)
4. **Misidentifying clause functions** — confusing noun clauses with adjective or adverb clauses
5. **Ignoring subordinating conjunctions** as clause markers
6. **Misplacing dependent clauses**, creating ambiguous or illogical sentences
7. **Failing to recognize embedded clauses** within larger sentences

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Define what a clause is and distinguish it from a phrase
- Identify independent and dependent clauses in any sentence
- Classify dependent clauses by function (noun, adjective, adverb)
- Recognize subordinating conjunctions and relative pronouns as clause markers
- Analyze clause relationships within complex and compound-complex sentences
- Apply correct punctuation rules for clause combinations
- Identify and correct clause-related errors (fragments, run-ons, misplaced clauses)
- Solve CSE-style clause questions accurately under time pressure

### 4.1 What Is a Clause?
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The principle behind this operation follows from the fundamental properties of arithmetic. Understanding the "why" — not just the "how" — lets you recognize when to apply this method in unfamiliar problem contexts on the CSE.


#### Definition

A **clause** is a grammatical unit that contains a **subject** (who or what the sentence is about) and a **predicate** (what the subject does or is). This subject-verb pair is what separates a clause from a phrase.

#### Clause vs. Phrase

| Feature | Clause | Phrase |
|---------|--------|--------|
| Has a subject | ✅ Yes | ❌ No |
| Has a verb | ✅ Yes | ❌ No (or has a non-finite verb) |
| Can express a complete thought | Sometimes | Never |
| Example | "The officer reviewed the file" | "in the morning" |

**Key distinction:** If a group of words has both a subject and a finite verb, it is a clause. If it lacks either one, it is a phrase.

#### Complete vs. Incomplete Thought

- A clause that expresses a **complete thought** can stand alone as a sentence → **independent clause**
- A clause that expresses an **incomplete thought** cannot stand alone → **dependent clause**

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- *The manager signed the document.* → One clause (subject: manager; verb: signed)
- *in the office* → Phrase (no subject-verb pair)

**Medium:**
- *When the meeting ended, the staff returned to their desks.* → Two clauses
  - "When the meeting ended" (dependent)
  - "the staff returned to their desks" (independent)

**Hard:**
- *The policy that the committee approved requires all employees who were hired after January to submit additional documents before the deadline expires.* → Four clauses
  - "The policy requires all employees to submit additional documents" (main/independent)
  - "that the committee approved" (adjective clause modifying "policy")
  - "who were hired after January" (adjective clause modifying "employees")
  - "before the deadline expires" (adverb clause modifying "submit")


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "The formula always works the same way regardless of the problem context."

> **Why it fails:** CSE problems often present variations where the standard formula must be adapted. Blindly applying a memorized formula without checking the context leads to systematic errors.

> **Correct model:** Always read the problem to identify what type of relationship exists (direct, inverse, part-whole, etc.), then apply the appropriate formula. Verify your answer makes sense in the problem's context before selecting it.

### 4.2 Independent Clauses

#### Definition

An **independent clause** (also called a main clause) is a clause that expresses a complete thought and can stand alone as a grammatically correct sentence. It does not depend on any other clause for its meaning.

#### Characteristics

1. Contains a subject and a finite verb
2. Expresses a complete thought
3. Can function as a sentence by itself
4. Does NOT begin with a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun

#### Examples

| Independent Clause | Subject | Verb |
|---|---|---|
| The employees submitted their reports. | employees | submitted |
| She passed the civil service examination. | She | passed |
| The office will close at five o'clock. | office | will close |
| Government agencies must follow procurement guidelines. | agencies | must follow |

#### Combining Independent Clauses

Two independent clauses can be joined to form a **compound sentence** using:
1. A **comma + coordinating conjunction** (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so)
   - *The director approved the budget, and the project commenced immediately.*
2. A **semicolon**
   - *The director approved the budget; the project commenced immediately.*
3. A **semicolon + conjunctive adverb + comma**
   - *The director approved the budget; therefore, the project commenced immediately.*

#### Common Error: Run-On Sentences

Joining independent clauses without proper punctuation creates a **run-on sentence**:
- ❌ *The director approved the budget the project commenced immediately.*
- ❌ *The director approved the budget, the project commenced immediately.* (comma splice)
- ✅ *The director approved the budget, and the project commenced immediately.*


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "If my computed answer is close to one of the choices, it must be right."

> **Why it fails:** The CSE deliberately includes distractors that result from common errors — using the wrong operation, misidentifying the proportion type, or reversing the ratio. A "close" answer could be the result of a systematic mistake that the test writers anticipated.

> **Correct model:** Verify your setup before computing. Check that you've identified the correct proportion type, set up the equation properly, and solved accurately. A wrong setup with correct arithmetic still produces a wrong answer — and the CSE will include that wrong answer among the choices.

### 4.3 Dependent Clauses
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** When you follow this procedure, you're exploiting a mathematical invariant — something that stays constant regardless of how you manipulate the numbers. Identifying that invariant is the key to solving problems efficiently rather than memorizing steps.


#### Definition

A **dependent clause** (also called a subordinate clause) contains a subject and a verb but does NOT express a complete thought. It cannot stand alone as a sentence — it depends on an independent clause to complete its meaning.

#### Why Dependent Clauses Cannot Stand Alone

A dependent clause begins with a **subordinating word** (subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun) that makes the thought incomplete:

- "Because the weather was bad" → What happened because of it? (incomplete)
- "Although she studied hard" → What was the result? (incomplete)
- "who arrived early" → Who is this about? What about them? (incomplete)

The subordinating word creates a sense of expectation — the reader needs more information.

#### Identifying Dependent Clauses

Look for these signals:
1. The clause begins with a subordinating conjunction (because, although, when, if, since, unless, while, after, before, until, etc.)
2. The clause begins with a relative pronoun (who, whom, whose, which, that)
3. The clause begins with "what," "where," "how," "why," or "whether" (noun clause markers)
4. The group of words has a subject and verb but feels incomplete

#### Examples

**Easy:**
- *because the office was closed* → Dependent (begins with "because"; incomplete thought)
- *although the applicant was qualified* → Dependent (begins with "although"; incomplete thought)

**Medium:**
- *After the committee reviewed the proposal, they approved the budget.* → "After the committee reviewed the proposal" is dependent; "they approved the budget" is independent
- *The employee who submitted the report early received commendation.* → "who submitted the report early" is dependent (adjective clause)

**Hard:**
- *Unless the department head certifies that the funds are available, the procurement officer cannot process what the requesting office submitted.* → Three dependent clauses:
  - "Unless the department head certifies" (adverb clause)
  - "that the funds are available" (noun clause, object of "certifies")
  - "what the requesting office submitted" (noun clause, object of "process")

#### Common Error: Sentence Fragments

Writing a dependent clause as if it were a complete sentence creates a **fragment**:
- ❌ *Because the meeting was canceled.* (fragment — no independent clause)
- ✅ *Because the meeting was canceled, the staff worked on their reports.* (complete)

---

### 4.4 Types of Dependent Clauses

Dependent clauses are classified by the **grammatical function** they perform in a sentence. There are three types:

| Type | Function | Answers | Introduced By |
|------|----------|---------|---------------|
| Noun clause | Acts as a noun (subject, object, complement) | What? Who? | that, what, whether, how, why, where, who |
| Adjective clause | Modifies a noun or pronoun | Which one? What kind? | who, whom, whose, which, that, where, when |
| Adverb clause | Modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb | When? Where? Why? How? Under what condition? | because, although, when, if, since, unless, while, after, before |

#### Quick Identification Method

1. **Find the dependent clause** (look for the subordinating word + subject + verb)
2. **Ask: What does this clause DO in the sentence?**
   - Does it act as a noun (subject/object/complement)? → Noun clause
   - Does it describe a noun? → Adjective clause
   - Does it tell when/why/how/under what condition? → Adverb clause

### 4.5 Noun Clauses
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** This shortcut works because it's a special case of the more general rule. By understanding the underlying principle, you can verify your answer logically even if you forget the exact formula under exam pressure.


#### Definition

A **noun clause** is a dependent clause that functions as a noun in a sentence. It can serve as a subject, direct object, indirect object, object of a preposition, or subject complement.

#### Signal Words

Noun clauses typically begin with:
- **that** — *I believe that he is qualified.*
- **what** — *What she said surprised everyone.*
- **whether / whether or not** — *Whether he will attend is uncertain.*
- **how** — *How the system works remains unclear.*
- **why** — *No one knows why the policy changed.*
- **who / whom** — *Whoever submits first will be prioritized.*
- **where** — *Where the meeting will be held has not been announced.*
- **when** — *When the results will be released depends on the commission.*

#### Functions of Noun Clauses

**As Subject:**
- *What the director announced shocked the entire department.*
- *That the project was delayed is unfortunate.*
- *Whether the budget will be approved remains uncertain.*

**As Direct Object:**
- *The manager confirmed that the deadline was extended.*
- *I don't know what the memorandum contains.*
- *She asked whether the training was mandatory.*

**As Subject Complement (after linking verbs):**
- *The problem is that no one followed the procedure.*
- *The question remains whether the policy is effective.*

**As Object of a Preposition:**
- *The award was given to whoever scored highest.*
- *Success depends on how well you prepare.*

#### CSE-Style Example

**Question:** Which underlined portion is a noun clause?
*The committee announced **that all applicants must submit their requirements before Friday**.*

**Answer:** "that all applicants must submit their requirements before Friday" — it functions as the direct object of "announced" (the committee announced WHAT?).

### 4.6 Adjective Clauses

#### Definition

An **adjective clause** (also called a relative clause) is a dependent clause that modifies a noun or pronoun. It answers the questions "Which one?" or "What kind?" and usually appears immediately after the noun it modifies.

#### Signal Words (Relative Pronouns and Relative Adverbs)

| Word | Refers To | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| who | people (subject) | The employee **who arrived early** received praise. |
| whom | people (object) | The applicant **whom we interviewed** was qualified. |
| whose | possession | The officer **whose report was late** received a warning. |
| which | things/animals | The policy **which was revised last year** is now effective. |
| that | people or things | The document **that you submitted** is incomplete. |
| where | places | The office **where she works** is on the third floor. |
| when | times | The day **when results are released** is always stressful. |

#### Restrictive vs. Non-Restrictive Adjective Clauses

**Restrictive (Essential):** Identifies which specific noun is meant. No commas.
- *The employees who passed the exam will be promoted.* (Only those who passed — not all employees)

**Non-Restrictive (Non-Essential):** Adds extra information about an already-identified noun. Uses commas.
- *Maria, who passed the exam, will be promoted.* (Maria is already identified; the clause adds extra info)

#### Punctuation Rule
- Restrictive → NO commas (essential to meaning)
- Non-restrictive → COMMAS before and after (extra information)

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- *The report that was submitted yesterday contained errors.* → "that was submitted yesterday" modifies "report"

**Medium:**
- *The supervisor whose team won the award gave a brief speech.* → "whose team won the award" modifies "supervisor"

**Hard:**
- *The memorandum, which the undersecretary signed before the deadline that the commission had set, was distributed to all regional offices where new employees had been assigned.* → Multiple adjective clauses:
  - "which the undersecretary signed before the deadline" modifies "memorandum"
  - "that the commission had set" modifies "deadline"
  - "where new employees had been assigned" modifies "offices"

### 4.7 Adverb Clauses

#### Definition

An **adverb clause** is a dependent clause that modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb. It answers questions like When? Where? Why? How? Under what condition? To what extent?

#### Categories and Subordinating Conjunctions

| Category | Conjunctions | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| **Time** | when, whenever, while, after, before, until, since, as soon as | *After the training ended, the participants received certificates.* |
| **Cause/Reason** | because, since, as, now that | *Because the budget was cut, the project was delayed.* |
| **Condition** | if, unless, provided that, as long as, in case | *Unless you submit the form, your application will not be processed.* |
| **Contrast/Concession** | although, though, even though, whereas, while | *Although the task was difficult, she completed it on time.* |
| **Purpose** | so that, in order that | *He reviewed the manual so that he could answer questions correctly.* |
| **Result** | so...that, such...that | *The exam was so difficult that many examinees failed.* |
| **Comparison** | as...as, than, as if, as though | *She works as efficiently as her supervisor does.* |
| **Place** | where, wherever | *Wherever the director goes, the staff follows protocol.* |

#### Position and Punctuation

**Adverb clause BEFORE the independent clause → USE A COMMA:**
- *Because the office was understaffed, overtime was mandatory.*

**Adverb clause AFTER the independent clause → NO COMMA (usually):**
- *Overtime was mandatory because the office was understaffed.*

This punctuation rule is heavily tested in the CSE.

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- *If you study consistently, you will pass the exam.* → "If you study consistently" is an adverb clause of condition

**Medium:**
- *Although the applicant had excellent credentials, the panel did not recommend her because she lacked the required civil service eligibility.* → Two adverb clauses:
  - "Although the applicant had excellent credentials" (concession)
  - "because she lacked the required civil service eligibility" (reason)

**Hard:**
- *Provided that the department certifies the availability of funds before the fiscal year ends, the procurement office may process the request even though the original timeline has already lapsed, unless the Commission on Audit issues a notice of disallowance.* → Three adverb clauses:
  - "Provided that the department certifies the availability of funds" (condition)
  - "even though the original timeline has already lapsed" (concession)
  - "unless the Commission on Audit issues a notice of disallowance" (condition)

### 4.8 Subordinating Conjunctions

#### Definition

**Subordinating conjunctions** are words that introduce dependent clauses and establish the relationship between the dependent clause and the independent clause. They are the most reliable signal that a clause is dependent.

#### Master List of Subordinating Conjunctions

| Relationship | Conjunctions |
|-------------|-------------|
| Time | after, before, when, whenever, while, until, since, as soon as, once |
| Cause/Reason | because, since, as, now that, inasmuch as |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that, as long as, in case, even if, only if |
| Contrast/Concession | although, though, even though, whereas, while (contrast meaning) |
| Purpose | so that, in order that, lest |
| Result | so...that, such...that |
| Comparison | as, than, as if, as though |
| Place | where, wherever |

#### How Subordinating Conjunctions Create Dependency

Take an independent clause: *The office closed early.*

Add a subordinating conjunction: *Because the office closed early* → Now it is dependent. The conjunction "because" creates an expectation that must be fulfilled by an independent clause.

Complete sentence: *Because the office closed early, the staff went home.*

#### Commonly Confused Pairs

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|------------|---------|---------|
| because | gives a reason | *She was promoted because she excelled.* |
| although | introduces contrast | *Although she excelled, she was not promoted.* |
| since | reason OR time | *Since you arrived, things have improved.* (time) / *Since you are qualified, you should apply.* (reason) |
| while | during OR contrast | *While I was working, he called.* (time) / *While she is diligent, he is lazy.* (contrast) |
| unless | if...not | *Unless you apply, you won't be considered.* = *If you don't apply, you won't be considered.* |

#### CSE Trap: "Unless" vs. "If"

Many examinees confuse these:
- **Unless** = "if not" → *Unless it rains, we will proceed.* (= If it does NOT rain)
- **If** = introduces a condition → *If it rains, we will cancel.*

Never use "unless" with a negative verb — it creates a double negative:
- ❌ *Unless you don't submit the form...* (means "if you DO submit")
- ✅ *Unless you submit the form...* (means "if you do NOT submit")

### 4.9 Relative Pronouns and Relative Clauses

#### Definition

**Relative pronouns** (who, whom, whose, which, that) introduce adjective clauses and connect them to the nouns they modify. They serve a dual function: they link the clause to the main sentence AND play a grammatical role within the clause itself.

#### Grammatical Roles Within the Clause

| Pronoun | Role in Clause | Example |
|---------|---------------|---------|
| who | subject | The officer **who investigated** the case filed a report. |
| whom | object | The applicant **whom we selected** starts Monday. |
| whose | possessive | The employee **whose performance** improved received a bonus. |
| which | subject or object (things) | The policy, **which was revised**, takes effect today. |
| that | subject or object (people/things) | The document **that you need** is on the desk. |

#### Who vs. Whom

- **Who** = subject of the clause (does the action)
  - *The manager who approved the request is on leave.* (who approved → who is the subject)
- **Whom** = object of the clause (receives the action)
  - *The candidate whom the panel interviewed was impressive.* (the panel interviewed whom → whom is the object)

**Quick test:** Replace with he/she (subject) or him/her (object):
- "He approved" → use **who**
- "The panel interviewed him" → use **whom**

#### That vs. Which

- **That** → restrictive clauses (essential, no commas)
  - *The report that contains the error must be revised.*
- **Which** → non-restrictive clauses (extra info, with commas)
  - *The report, which was submitted yesterday, contains an error.*

#### Omitting Relative Pronouns

When the relative pronoun is the **object** of the clause, it can be omitted:
- *The document (that) you submitted is incomplete.* → "that" is optional
- *The person (whom) she recommended was hired.* → "whom" is optional

When the relative pronoun is the **subject**, it CANNOT be omitted:
- *The officer who filed the report is here.* → "who" is required
- ❌ *The officer filed the report is here.* → Ungrammatical

### 4.10 Clause Combinations and Sentence Building

#### Sentence Types by Clause Structure

| Sentence Type | Structure | Example |
|--------------|-----------|---------|
| Simple | 1 independent clause | *The staff attended the seminar.* |
| Compound | 2+ independent clauses | *The staff attended the seminar, and they submitted their reports.* |
| Complex | 1 independent + 1+ dependent | *After the staff attended the seminar, they submitted their reports.* |
| Compound-Complex | 2+ independent + 1+ dependent | *After the staff attended the seminar, they submitted their reports, and the supervisor reviewed them.* |

#### Building Complex Sentences

Start with two simple sentences:
1. *The employee was absent.*
2. *The project was delayed.*

Combine using different clause relationships:
- **Cause:** *Because the employee was absent, the project was delayed.*
- **Time:** *When the employee was absent, the project was delayed.*
- **Condition:** *If the employee is absent, the project will be delayed.*
- **Concession:** *Although the employee was absent, the project was not delayed.*

Each subordinating conjunction changes the logical relationship while maintaining grammatical correctness.

#### Embedding Clauses

Clauses can be embedded within other clauses:
- *The director confirmed [that the employee [who was absent] caused the delay [that affected the entire department]].*

Layers:
1. Main clause: "The director confirmed"
2. Noun clause (object): "that the employee caused the delay"
3. Adjective clause: "who was absent" (modifies "employee")
4. Adjective clause: "that affected the entire department" (modifies "delay")

#### Sentence Variety in Professional Writing

Effective government writing uses a mix of sentence types:
- Simple sentences for emphasis and clarity
- Complex sentences for showing relationships
- Compound sentences for parallel ideas
- Compound-complex sentences for nuanced, multi-layered ideas

### 4.11 Clause Punctuation Rules

#### Rule 1: Dependent Clause Before Independent Clause → Comma

- ✅ *Because the budget was approved, the project will proceed.*
- ✅ *Although she was new, she performed excellently.*
- ✅ *When the director arrives, the meeting will begin.*

#### Rule 2: Independent Clause Before Dependent Clause → Usually No Comma

- ✅ *The project will proceed because the budget was approved.*
- ✅ *She performed excellently although she was new.*
- ✅ *The meeting will begin when the director arrives.*

**Exception:** Use a comma before "although," "though," and "whereas" even when they follow the independent clause, because they introduce contrasting information:
- ✅ *She was hired, although she lacked experience.*

#### Rule 3: Restrictive Adjective Clauses → No Commas

- ✅ *The employees who passed the exam will be promoted.* (only those who passed)

#### Rule 4: Non-Restrictive Adjective Clauses → Commas

- ✅ *The director, who has served for twenty years, announced her retirement.*

#### Rule 5: Compound Sentences → Comma Before Coordinating Conjunction

- ✅ *The report was submitted, and the supervisor approved it.*
- ❌ *The report was submitted and the supervisor approved it.* (missing comma)

#### Rule 6: No Comma Between Subject Noun Clause and Verb

- ✅ *What the committee decided is final.*
- ❌ *What the committee decided, is final.* (incorrect comma)

#### Common CSE Punctuation Traps

1. Comma splice: joining two independent clauses with only a comma
2. Missing comma after introductory dependent clause
3. Adding commas around restrictive clauses (changes meaning)
4. Omitting commas around non-restrictive clauses

### 4.12 Contextual Clause Analysis

#### Step-by-Step Method for Analyzing Clauses

**Step 1: Identify all finite verbs in the sentence.**
Every finite verb signals a potential clause.

**Step 2: Find the subject for each verb.**
Each subject-verb pair = one clause.

**Step 3: Identify clause boundaries.**
Look for subordinating conjunctions, relative pronouns, and coordinating conjunctions that separate clauses.

**Step 4: Classify each clause.**
- Does it have a subordinating word? → Dependent
- Can it stand alone? → Independent
- What function does the dependent clause serve? → Noun / Adjective / Adverb

#### Worked Example

**Sentence:** *The supervisor who reviewed the application confirmed that the candidate would be interviewed after the panel convenes.*

**Step 1 — Finite verbs:** reviewed, confirmed, would be interviewed, convenes

**Step 2 — Subject-verb pairs:**
- who reviewed (the application)
- The supervisor confirmed
- the candidate would be interviewed
- the panel convenes

**Step 3 — Boundaries:**
- "who reviewed the application" → begins with relative pronoun "who"
- "that the candidate would be interviewed" → begins with "that"
- "after the panel convenes" → begins with "after"

**Step 4 — Classification:**
- "The supervisor confirmed" → Independent clause (main clause)
- "who reviewed the application" → Adjective clause (modifies "supervisor")
- "that the candidate would be interviewed after the panel convenes" → Noun clause (object of "confirmed")
- "after the panel convenes" → Adverb clause of time (modifies "would be interviewed")

### 4.13 Elimination Strategies for CSE Questions

#### Strategy 1: Check for Subject-Verb Pairs

If a question asks you to identify a clause, eliminate any option that lacks both a subject and a finite verb. Phrases like "in the morning," "running quickly," or "to finish the report" are NOT clauses.

#### Strategy 2: Look for Subordinating Words

If a question asks whether something is independent or dependent:
- Begins with a subordinating conjunction (because, although, when, if, etc.) → Dependent
- Begins with a relative pronoun functioning within the clause (who, which, that) → Dependent
- No subordinating word and expresses a complete thought → Independent

#### Strategy 3: The "Period Test"

Can the group of words stand alone with a period and make sense?
- Yes → Independent clause
- No → Dependent clause (or a phrase)

#### Strategy 4: Identify the Function

For questions asking "What type of clause is this?":
- Replace the clause with "something" or "it" → If the sentence still works, it's a **noun clause**
- Remove the clause → If a noun loses its identifier, it was an **adjective clause**
- Remove the clause → If you lose information about when/why/how, it was an **adverb clause**

#### Strategy 5: Process of Elimination on Answer Choices

In multiple-choice questions:
1. Eliminate options that are phrases (no subject-verb pair)
2. Eliminate options that misidentify the clause type
3. Check remaining options against the subordinating word and function
4. Verify by substitution or removal

### 4.14 Common Errors in Clause Analysis

#### Error 1: Confusing Clauses and Phrases

| This is a CLAUSE | This is a PHRASE |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| *after the meeting ended* (subject: meeting; verb: ended) | *after the meeting* (no verb) |
| *who works in the office* (subject: who; verb: works) | *working in the office* (no finite verb) |
| *because she was late* (subject: she; verb: was) | *because of her lateness* (no subject-verb pair) |

**Key insight:** Prepositions followed by noun phrases create phrases, not clauses. Subordinating conjunctions followed by subject-verb pairs create clauses.

#### Error 2: Sentence Fragments

A dependent clause punctuated as a complete sentence:
- ❌ *Although the report was submitted on time.* → Fragment
- ✅ *Although the report was submitted on time, it contained several errors.* → Complete

#### Error 3: Run-On Sentences

Two independent clauses joined without proper punctuation:
- ❌ *The budget was approved the project started immediately.* → Run-on
- ❌ *The budget was approved, the project started immediately.* → Comma splice
- ✅ *The budget was approved, so the project started immediately.* → Correct

#### Error 4: Misplaced Adjective Clauses

Adjective clauses must appear immediately after the noun they modify:
- ❌ *The officer submitted the report who was assigned to the case.* → "who" seems to modify "report"
- ✅ *The officer who was assigned to the case submitted the report.* → Clear modification

#### Error 5: Incorrect Conjunction Choice

Using the wrong subordinating conjunction changes meaning:
- *Because she was qualified, she was hired.* (cause-effect ✅)
- *Although she was qualified, she was hired.* (concession — implies surprise ⚠️)
- *Unless she was qualified, she was hired.* (illogical ❌)

#### Error 6: Dangling "That" Clauses

Omitting "that" when it causes ambiguity:
- ❌ *The manager confirmed the employee was promoted was a mistake.* → Confusing
- ✅ *The manager confirmed that the employee was promoted.* → Clear

### Mini Practice Set

**1.** Identify the dependent clause: *When the results were released, many applicants celebrated.*
- **Answer:** "When the results were released" — adverb clause of time (begins with "when," modifies "celebrated")

**2.** Identify the independent clause: *Although the task was complex, the team finished ahead of schedule.*
- **Answer:** "the team finished ahead of schedule" — expresses a complete thought

**3.** What type of clause is underlined? *The committee approved **what the department head recommended**.*
- **Answer:** Noun clause — functions as the direct object of "approved"

**4.** Identify the adjective clause: *The employee who filed the complaint was transferred to another division.*
- **Answer:** "who filed the complaint" — modifies "employee"

**5.** Is this a clause or a phrase? *running through the hallway*
- **Answer:** Phrase — no finite verb (running is a participle, not a finite verb)

**6.** Identify the error: *Because the office was closed. The employees went home.*
- **Answer:** Fragment — "Because the office was closed" is a dependent clause punctuated as a sentence. Correct: "Because the office was closed, the employees went home."

**7.** What type of dependent clause? *I don't know whether the meeting has been canceled.*
- **Answer:** Noun clause — "whether the meeting has been canceled" is the direct object of "know"

**8.** Identify all clauses: *The director said that the policy would change after the review is completed.*
- **Answer:** Three clauses: "The director said" (independent), "that the policy would change" (noun clause), "after the review is completed" (adverb clause)

**9.** Restrictive or non-restrictive? *The employees, who attended the seminar, received certificates.*
- **Answer:** Non-restrictive — commas indicate extra information; ALL employees attended and received certificates

**10.** Identify the adverb clause: *Unless the documents are notarized, the application will be denied.*
- **Answer:** "Unless the documents are notarized" — adverb clause of condition

**11.** What function does the noun clause serve? *That he resigned surprised everyone.*
- **Answer:** Subject — "That he resigned" is the subject of "surprised"

**12.** Correct the error: *The report was submitted the supervisor approved it.*
- **Answer:** Run-on sentence. Correct: "The report was submitted, and the supervisor approved it." OR "After the report was submitted, the supervisor approved it."

**13.** Identify the adjective clause: *The regulation whose implementation was delayed has finally taken effect.*
- **Answer:** "whose implementation was delayed" — modifies "regulation"

**14.** Independent or dependent? *Although the training was mandatory*
- **Answer:** Dependent — begins with "although" and does not express a complete thought

**15.** What type of clause? *She works wherever she is assigned.*
- **Answer:** "wherever she is assigned" — adverb clause of place

**16.** Identify the noun clause: *Whoever passes the examination will be eligible for promotion.*
- **Answer:** "Whoever passes the examination" — noun clause functioning as the subject

**17.** How many clauses? *The manager reviewed the report, and she approved the budget after the accountant verified the figures.*
- **Answer:** Three clauses: "The manager reviewed the report" (independent), "she approved the budget" (independent), "after the accountant verified the figures" (dependent/adverb)

**18.** Correct the punctuation: *After the audit was completed the findings were presented to the board.*
- **Answer:** Add a comma after the introductory dependent clause: "After the audit was completed, the findings were presented to the board."

**19.** That vs. Which: *The memorandum ___ was issued last week requires immediate compliance.*
- **Answer:** "that" — restrictive clause (identifies which memorandum; no commas)

**20.** Identify the clause type: *The question is whether the policy should be revised.*
- **Answer:** "whether the policy should be revised" — noun clause functioning as subject complement

### Quick Recap

| Concept | Key Point |
|---------|-----------|
| Clause | A group of words with a subject and a finite verb |
| Independent clause | Complete thought; can stand alone as a sentence |
| Dependent clause | Incomplete thought; needs an independent clause |
| Noun clause | Functions as subject, object, or complement |
| Adjective clause | Modifies a noun; introduced by relative pronouns |
| Adverb clause | Modifies verb/adjective/adverb; introduced by subordinating conjunctions |
| Subordinating conjunctions | because, although, when, if, since, unless, while, after, before, until |
| Relative pronouns | who, whom, whose, which, that |
| Fragment | Dependent clause written as a sentence |
| Run-on | Two independent clauses without proper punctuation |
| Comma rule | Comma after introductory dependent clause; no comma when dependent clause follows |

### Memory Aids

#### AAAWWWUBBIS — Subordinating Conjunctions

**A**fter, **A**lthough, **A**s, **W**hen, **W**hile, **W**here, **U**ntil, **B**efore, **B**ecause, **I**f, **S**ince

This mnemonic helps you remember the most common subordinating conjunctions. If a clause begins with one of these words, it is almost certainly a dependent clause.

#### "WHO WHAT THAT" — Relative Clause Starters

If a clause begins with **who, whom, whose, which, or that** and modifies a noun, it is an adjective clause.

#### The "IT" Test for Noun Clauses

Replace the suspected noun clause with "it" or "something":
- *What she said surprised everyone.* → *It surprised everyone.* ✅ (noun clause confirmed)
- *I know that he is honest.* → *I know it.* ✅ (noun clause confirmed)

#### The "DELETE" Test for Adjective and Adverb Clauses

Remove the suspected clause:
- If a noun loses its description → it was an **adjective clause**
- If the sentence loses time/reason/condition info → it was an **adverb clause**
- If the sentence becomes ungrammatical (missing subject/object) → it was a **noun clause**

#### Independent Clause = "Period-Ready"

If you can put a period at the end and it makes complete sense, it's independent. If it leaves you hanging, it's dependent.
