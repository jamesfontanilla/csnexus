# Types of Sentences by Structure

## Explanations

### Introduction

**Sentence structure** refers to how clauses are arranged and connected within a sentence. English sentences are classified into four structural types — simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex — based on the number and kind of clauses they contain.

This classification matters because:
- Government writing demands variety and precision; using only simple sentences sounds choppy, while overusing compound-complex sentences creates confusion
- The CSE Verbal Ability section frequently asks examinees to identify sentence types, combine sentences, or fix structural errors
- Understanding clause relationships is the foundation for recognizing run-on sentences, comma splices, and fragments
- Sentence structure questions test whether you can analyze HOW ideas connect — not just WHAT they say

### Why Sentence Structure Classification Is Tested in the CSE

The Philippine Civil Service Examination tests sentence structure because:
- Administrative writing requires clear, varied sentence construction
- Many items ask you to identify the type of a given sentence or to combine sentences correctly
- Clause analysis skills help you answer grammar questions about punctuation, conjunctions, and parallelism
- Understanding structure helps you eliminate wrong answers in sentence-correction items
- It tests higher-order grammar knowledge — beyond vocabulary into syntax

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. Confusing **compound** and **complex** sentences (both have multiple clauses, but the clause types differ)
2. Thinking a sentence with a compound subject or compound verb is automatically a compound sentence
3. Failing to recognize **dependent clauses** that begin with subordinating conjunctions
4. Misidentifying **relative clauses** (who, which, that) as independent clauses
5. Overlooking semicolons as clause connectors in compound sentences
6. Assuming long sentences are compound-complex and short sentences are simple
7. Confusing coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) with subordinating conjunctions (because, although, since)
8. Not recognizing that a comma + FANBOYS between independent clauses signals a compound structure

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Identify the four major sentence structures (simple, compound, complex, compound-complex)
- Distinguish independent clauses from dependent clauses
- Recognize coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) and their role in compound sentences
- Recognize subordinating conjunctions and their role in complex sentences
- Analyze sentences with multiple clauses to determine structure type
- Combine simple sentences into compound, complex, or compound-complex structures
- Apply correct punctuation for each sentence type
- Solve CSE-style sentence structure questions accurately under time pressure

## 4.1 What Are Types of Sentences by Structure?

### Definition

Sentences are classified by structure based on the **number and type of clauses** they contain. A clause is a group of words with both a subject and a verb. The two types of clauses are:

- **Independent clause** — expresses a complete thought; can stand alone as a sentence
- **Dependent (subordinate) clause** — does NOT express a complete thought; cannot stand alone

The combination of these clause types determines the sentence's structural classification.

### The Four Sentence Structures

| Structure | Independent Clauses | Dependent Clauses |
|-----------|-------------------|------------------|
| Simple | 1 | 0 |
| Compound | 2+ | 0 |
| Complex | 1 | 1+ |
| Compound-Complex | 2+ | 1+ |

### Why Clauses Determine Structure

The key insight: **count the clauses, identify their types, and the structure names itself.**

- If you find only ONE independent clause and NO dependent clause → Simple
- If you find TWO OR MORE independent clauses and NO dependent clause → Compound
- If you find ONE independent clause and ONE OR MORE dependent clauses → Complex
- If you find TWO OR MORE independent clauses AND at least ONE dependent clause → Compound-Complex

### Conjunction Signals

Conjunctions are your primary clue for identifying sentence structure:

| Conjunction Type | Examples | Signals |
|-----------------|----------|---------|
| Coordinating (FANBOYS) | for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so | Compound structure (joins independent clauses) |
| Subordinating | because, although, since, while, if, unless, after, before, when, until | Complex structure (creates dependent clause) |
| Correlative | both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also | May signal compound elements within a clause |

### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- "The clerk filed the documents." → Simple (one independent clause)
- "The clerk filed the documents, and the supervisor signed the report." → Compound (two independent clauses joined by "and")

**Medium:**
- "Because the deadline was approaching, the team worked overtime." → Complex (one dependent clause + one independent clause)
- "The manager approved the budget, but the director requested revisions because the figures were outdated." → Compound-Complex (two independent clauses + one dependent clause)

**Hard:**
- "Although the policy was controversial, the committee endorsed it, and the secretary issued the memorandum before the session ended." → Compound-Complex (one dependent clause + two independent clauses + one dependent clause)

## 4.2 Simple Sentences

### Definition

A **simple sentence** contains exactly ONE independent clause and NO dependent clauses. It expresses a single complete thought.

### Key Characteristics

- Has one subject-verb pair (or compound subjects/verbs sharing the same clause)
- Expresses one complete thought
- May contain phrases (prepositional, participial, infinitive) but NO subordinate clauses
- Can be short OR long — length does not determine structure

### Important Distinction

A simple sentence can have:
- **Compound subject:** "The manager *and* the supervisor attended the meeting."
- **Compound verb:** "The employee *filed* the report *and submitted* the form."
- **Both:** "John *and* Maria *studied* together *and passed* the exam."

These are still SIMPLE sentences because there is only ONE independent clause. The subjects and verbs share the same clause structure.

### How to Identify a Simple Sentence

1. Find all subject-verb pairs
2. Check if there is only ONE clause (one complete thought)
3. Confirm there are NO subordinating conjunctions creating dependent clauses
4. Confirm there are NO coordinating conjunctions joining separate independent clauses

### Examples

**Basic:**
- "The teacher explained the lesson."
- "She passed the civil service exam."
- "The government office closes at five."

**With compound subject:**
- "The director and the assistant reviewed the proposal."
- "Neither the applicant nor the interviewer arrived on time."

**With compound verb:**
- "The secretary typed the letter and mailed it immediately."
- "The committee reviewed, discussed, and approved the budget."

**With phrases (still simple):**
- "The employee in the accounting department submitted the quarterly report on time."
- "Running late for the meeting, the supervisor took a shortcut through the lobby."
- "To qualify for the position, applicants must pass the civil service examination."

### Common Trap

> "The employee in the accounting department submitted the quarterly report to the director of finance on the last day of the fiscal year."

This sentence is LONG but still SIMPLE — it has one subject ("employee"), one verb ("submitted"), and multiple prepositional phrases. Length ≠ complexity.

## 4.3 Compound Sentences

### Definition

A **compound sentence** contains TWO OR MORE independent clauses and NO dependent clauses. Each clause could stand alone as a complete sentence.

### Key Characteristics

- Contains at least two subject-verb pairs in separate independent clauses
- Clauses are joined by coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS), semicolons, or semicolons with transitional expressions
- Each clause expresses a complete thought independently
- No subordinating conjunctions creating dependent clauses

### Three Ways to Form Compound Sentences

**1. Comma + Coordinating Conjunction (FANBOYS)**
- "She studied hard, **and** she passed the exam."
- "The office was closed, **but** the guard was still on duty."
- "He must submit the form today, **or** his application will be denied."

**2. Semicolon Alone**
- "The meeting ended early**;** everyone returned to their desks."
- "The budget was approved**;** the project can now proceed."

**3. Semicolon + Transitional Expression + Comma**
- "The proposal was rejected**; however,** the team revised and resubmitted it."
- "She completed the training**; therefore,** she qualified for the promotion."

### FANBOYS — The Coordinating Conjunctions

| Conjunction | Relationship | Example |
|-------------|-------------|---------|
| **F**or | Reason/cause | "He was promoted, for he exceeded all targets." |
| **A**nd | Addition | "She filed the report, and he submitted the budget." |
| **N**or | Negative addition | "He did not complain, nor did he request a transfer." |
| **B**ut | Contrast | "The task was difficult, but she completed it on time." |
| **O**r | Alternative | "Submit the form today, or you will miss the deadline." |
| **Y**et | Contrast (stronger) | "The evidence was clear, yet the committee hesitated." |
| **S**o | Result/effect | "The deadline passed, so the application was denied." |

### Punctuation Rule

When a coordinating conjunction joins two **independent clauses**, place a comma BEFORE the conjunction:
- ✅ "The clerk filed the report**,** and the supervisor signed it."
- ❌ "The clerk filed the report and the supervisor signed it." (missing comma)

When a coordinating conjunction joins **compound elements within one clause** (not two independent clauses), do NOT use a comma:
- ✅ "The clerk filed the report and signed the form." (compound verb, one clause — no comma)

### Examples

**Easy:**
- "The rain stopped, and the ceremony began."
- "She applied for the position, but she was not qualified."

**Medium:**
- "The committee reviewed the proposal; however, they requested additional data."
- "He did not attend the seminar, nor did he submit an excuse letter."

**Hard:**
- "The department exceeded its quarterly targets, so the director recommended a commendation; yet the central office delayed the approval."

## 4.4 Complex Sentences

### Definition

A **complex sentence** contains exactly ONE independent clause and ONE OR MORE dependent (subordinate) clauses.

### Key Characteristics

- Contains one independent clause (the main idea)
- Contains at least one dependent clause (provides additional information)
- The dependent clause is introduced by a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun
- The dependent clause CANNOT stand alone as a sentence
- The relationship between clauses is unequal — one idea is primary, the other is secondary

### Types of Dependent Clauses

**1. Adverbial Clauses** (modify the verb — tell when, why, how, under what condition)
- Introduced by subordinating conjunctions: because, although, since, while, if, unless, when, before, after, until, as, even though, so that, in order that
- "**Because the budget was cut**, the project was delayed."
- "She will be promoted **if she passes the examination**."

**2. Adjective (Relative) Clauses** (modify a noun — tell which one)
- Introduced by relative pronouns: who, whom, whose, which, that
- "The employee **who submitted the report** received a commendation."
- "The policy **that was implemented last year** improved efficiency."

**3. Noun Clauses** (function as a noun — subject, object, or complement)
- Introduced by: that, what, whatever, who, whoever, how, whether, why
- "**What the director announced** surprised everyone."
- "The committee decided **that the proposal needed revision**."

### Punctuation Rules for Complex Sentences

**When the dependent clause comes FIRST** → use a comma after it:
- ✅ "**Although the task was difficult,** she completed it on time."
- ✅ "**Before the meeting started,** the secretary distributed the agenda."

**When the dependent clause comes LAST** → generally NO comma:
- ✅ "She completed the task on time **although it was difficult**."
- ✅ "The secretary distributed the agenda **before the meeting started**."

### Subordinating Conjunctions — Complete Reference

| Category | Conjunctions |
|----------|-------------|
| Time | after, before, when, while, until, since, as soon as, once |
| Cause/Reason | because, since, as, now that |
| Contrast/Concession | although, though, even though, whereas, while |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that, as long as, in case |
| Purpose | so that, in order that |
| Result | so...that, such...that |
| Comparison | as, than, as...as |

### Examples

**Easy:**
- "Because it was raining, classes were suspended."
- "She left early because she felt ill."

**Medium:**
- "Although he was the most qualified candidate, the panel chose someone with more experience."
- "The employee who was hired last month already received a commendation."

**Hard:**
- "Unless the department submits the compliance report before the deadline that the commission set, the agency's accreditation will be revoked."

## 4.5 Compound-Complex Sentences

### Definition

A **compound-complex sentence** contains TWO OR MORE independent clauses AND at least ONE dependent clause. It combines the features of both compound and complex sentences.

### Key Characteristics

- Has at least two independent clauses (like a compound sentence)
- Has at least one dependent clause (like a complex sentence)
- Uses both coordinating and subordinating conjunctions
- Expresses multiple related ideas with at least one subordinate relationship
- Most structurally complex sentence type

### How to Identify

Look for BOTH:
1. A coordinating conjunction or semicolon joining independent clauses (compound signal)
2. A subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun creating a dependent clause (complex signal)

If both are present → Compound-Complex.

### Examples

**Basic pattern:**
- "**Although the task was difficult** [dependent], she completed it [independent], **and** her supervisor praised her [independent]."

**Analysis:**
- Dependent clause: "Although the task was difficult" (subordinating conjunction "although")
- Independent clause 1: "she completed it"
- Independent clause 2: "her supervisor praised her"
- Coordinating conjunction: "and" (joins the two independent clauses)

**More examples:**

- "**When the alarm sounded** [dependent], the employees evacuated the building [independent], **but** some remained on the upper floors [independent]."

- "The director approved the budget [independent], **and** the team began the project [independent] **after the contracts were signed** [dependent]."

- "**Because the deadline was extended** [dependent], the staff had more time to prepare [independent]**;** however, the quality of submissions did not improve [independent]."

### CSE-Style Example

> "Although the committee recommended approval, the director withheld his signature, and the project remained unfunded until the next fiscal year."

**Clause breakdown:**
- "Although the committee recommended approval" → dependent (subordinating conjunction "although")
- "the director withheld his signature" → independent
- "the project remained unfunded until the next fiscal year" → independent (joined by "and")
- "until the next fiscal year" → prepositional phrase (NOT a clause — no subject-verb pair)

**Answer:** Compound-Complex

## 4.6 Independent vs. Dependent Clauses

### The Critical Distinction

This is the MOST IMPORTANT skill for sentence structure classification. If you can accurately identify clause types, you can classify any sentence.

### Independent Clause

An **independent clause**:
- Has a subject and a verb
- Expresses a complete thought
- Can stand alone as a sentence
- Does NOT begin with a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun

**Examples:**
- "The manager approved the request." ✅ Complete thought
- "She will attend the conference." ✅ Complete thought
- "The report was submitted on time." ✅ Complete thought

### Dependent Clause

A **dependent (subordinate) clause**:
- Has a subject and a verb
- Does NOT express a complete thought
- CANNOT stand alone as a sentence
- Begins with a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun

**Examples:**
- "Because the manager approved the request" ❌ Incomplete — what happened?
- "Although she will attend the conference" ❌ Incomplete — so what?
- "Who submitted the report on time" ❌ Incomplete — what about that person?

### Side-by-Side Comparison

| Feature | Independent Clause | Dependent Clause |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Subject + Verb | ✅ Yes | ✅ Yes |
| Complete thought | ✅ Yes | ❌ No |
| Can stand alone | ✅ Yes | ❌ No |
| Starts with subordinator | ❌ No | ✅ Yes |
| Function | Main idea | Supporting detail |

### The "Stand Alone" Test

Read the clause by itself. Ask: "Does this make sense as a complete sentence?"

- "The office closed early." → ✅ Makes sense alone → Independent
- "Because the office closed early" → ❌ Leaves you waiting → Dependent
- "She received a promotion." → ✅ Makes sense alone → Independent
- "Who received a promotion" → ❌ Feels like a question fragment → Dependent

### Tricky Cases

**Phrases vs. Clauses:**
- "in the morning" → phrase (no subject-verb pair) — NOT a clause
- "when morning came" → clause (subject: "morning," verb: "came") — dependent clause

**"That" clauses:**
- "The director announced **that the policy would change**." → "that the policy would change" is a dependent noun clause

**Relative clauses embedded in independent clauses:**
- "The employee **who arrived late** was reprimanded." → The entire sentence is ONE independent clause containing an embedded relative clause. This makes it a COMPLEX sentence.

## 4.7 Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS)

### The Seven Coordinating Conjunctions

Use the mnemonic **FANBOYS** to remember all seven:

| Letter | Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|--------|------------|---------|---------|
| F | for | because/reason | "He left early, for he was feeling ill." |
| A | and | addition | "She filed the report, and he signed it." |
| N | nor | negative addition | "He did not call, nor did he write." |
| B | but | contrast | "The test was hard, but she passed." |
| O | or | alternative/choice | "Study now, or you will fail." |
| Y | yet | contrast (unexpected) | "He trained daily, yet he lost the race." |
| S | so | result/consequence | "It rained hard, so the event was canceled." |

### Role in Sentence Structure

Coordinating conjunctions join **equal grammatical elements**:
- Word + Word: "fast **and** efficient"
- Phrase + Phrase: "in the office **or** at home"
- Clause + Clause: "She applied, **but** she was rejected."

**For sentence structure classification**, coordinating conjunctions matter when they join **independent clauses** — this creates a compound sentence.

### Punctuation with FANBOYS

**Between independent clauses** → comma BEFORE the conjunction:
- "The budget was approved**,** and the project began."

**Between words or phrases** → NO comma:
- "The budget was approved and implemented." (compound verb, not compound sentence)

### "Nor" — Special Inversion Rule

When "nor" joins independent clauses, the second clause uses **inverted word order** (auxiliary verb before subject):
- "She did not complain, **nor did she** request a transfer."
- "The report was not filed, **nor was it** discussed in the meeting."

## 4.8 Subordinating Conjunctions

### Definition

**Subordinating conjunctions** introduce dependent clauses and show the relationship between the dependent clause and the independent clause. They "subordinate" one idea to another — making it less important grammatically.

### Complete List by Category

**Time:**
- after, before, when, whenever, while, until, since, as, as soon as, once, by the time

**Cause/Reason:**
- because, since, as, now that, inasmuch as

**Contrast/Concession:**
- although, though, even though, whereas, while (when meaning "although"), even if

**Condition:**
- if, unless, provided that, as long as, in case, on condition that, whether or not

**Purpose:**
- so that, in order that, lest

**Result:**
- so...that, such...that

**Comparison:**
- as, than, as if, as though

**Place:**
- where, wherever

### How Subordinating Conjunctions Create Dependent Clauses

Take any independent clause and add a subordinating conjunction to the front — it immediately becomes dependent:

- "She passed the exam." → Independent ✅
- "**Because** she passed the exam" → Dependent ❌ (needs more information)
- "**Although** she passed the exam" → Dependent ❌ (needs more information)
- "**When** she passed the exam" → Dependent ❌ (needs more information)

### Position Flexibility

Dependent clauses can appear at the beginning, middle, or end of a sentence:

**Beginning (comma required):**
- "**Although the evidence was strong,** the jury acquitted the defendant."

**End (no comma usually):**
- "The jury acquitted the defendant **although the evidence was strong**."

**Middle (commas if non-essential):**
- "The defendant, **although the evidence was strong,** was acquitted by the jury."

### Distinguishing from Prepositions

Some words function as BOTH subordinating conjunctions and prepositions:
- **Before/After/Since/Until:**
  - Preposition (+ noun): "She left **before** the meeting." (no clause)
  - Conjunction (+ clause): "She left **before** the meeting started." (dependent clause)

The test: Does a subject-verb pair follow the word? If yes → conjunction. If no → preposition.

## 4.9 Sentence Combining and Transformation

### Why This Matters for the CSE

The exam frequently asks you to:
- Combine two simple sentences into one compound, complex, or compound-complex sentence
- Identify the BEST way to combine sentences
- Transform sentence structures while preserving meaning

### Combining into Compound Sentences

Take two simple sentences and join them with a coordinating conjunction:
- "The clerk filed the report." + "The supervisor signed it."
- → "The clerk filed the report**,** **and** the supervisor signed it."

### Combining into Complex Sentences

Take two simple sentences and subordinate one:
- "She studied hard." + "She passed the exam."
- → "**Because** she studied hard**,** she passed the exam."
- → "She passed the exam **because** she studied hard."

### Combining into Compound-Complex Sentences

Combine three or more ideas using both coordination and subordination:
- "The deadline was extended." + "The staff had more time." + "The quality did not improve."
- → "**Although** the deadline was extended**,** the staff had more time**,** **but** the quality did not improve."

### Transformation Practice

**Simple → Complex:**
- Original: "The experienced employee trained the new hires."
- Transformed: "The employee **who was experienced** trained the new hires."

**Two Simple → Compound:**
- Original: "He applied for the position." + "He was not selected."
- Transformed: "He applied for the position**, but** he was not selected."

**Compound → Complex:**
- Original: "She was tired, but she finished the report."
- Transformed: "**Although** she was tired**,** she finished the report."

## 4.10 Punctuation in Sentence Structure

### Punctuation Rules by Sentence Type

**Simple Sentence:**
- No internal clause-separating punctuation needed
- May use commas for lists, appositives, or introductory phrases
- "The director, a veteran administrator, approved the request."

**Compound Sentence:**
- Comma + FANBOYS between independent clauses
- OR semicolon between independent clauses
- OR semicolon + transitional word + comma
- ✅ "She applied, and she was hired."
- ✅ "She applied; she was hired."
- ✅ "She applied; consequently, she was hired."

**Complex Sentence:**
- Comma after a dependent clause that comes FIRST
- No comma when the dependent clause comes LAST (usually)
- ✅ "Because she was qualified, she was hired."
- ✅ "She was hired because she was qualified."

**Compound-Complex Sentence:**
- Follows BOTH compound and complex punctuation rules
- ✅ "Although she was nervous, she performed well, and the panel was impressed."

### Common Punctuation Errors

**Comma Splice** — joining two independent clauses with ONLY a comma (no conjunction):
- ❌ "She studied hard, she passed the exam."
- ✅ "She studied hard, **and** she passed the exam."
- ✅ "She studied hard**;** she passed the exam."

**Run-On Sentence** — joining two independent clauses with NO punctuation and NO conjunction:
- ❌ "She studied hard she passed the exam."
- ✅ "She studied hard, and she passed the exam."

**Unnecessary Comma** — placing a comma before a conjunction that joins compound elements (not clauses):
- ❌ "She studied hard**,** and passed the exam." (compound verb, not compound sentence)
- ✅ "She studied hard and passed the exam."

## 4.11 Elimination Strategies for CSE Questions

### Step-by-Step Classification Method

When a CSE question asks "What type of sentence is this?":

**Step 1: Find all verbs (conjugated verbs, not infinitives or participles)**

**Step 2: Match each verb to its subject — this gives you your clauses**

**Step 3: Classify each clause as independent or dependent**
- Does it start with a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun? → Dependent
- Can it stand alone? → Independent

**Step 4: Count and classify**
- 1 independent, 0 dependent → Simple
- 2+ independent, 0 dependent → Compound
- 1 independent, 1+ dependent → Complex
- 2+ independent, 1+ dependent → Compound-Complex

### Quick Identification Shortcuts

| If you see... | It's likely... |
|---------------|---------------|
| No conjunctions, one subject-verb pair | Simple |
| FANBOYS between clauses with subjects on both sides | Compound |
| Semicolon between complete thoughts | Compound |
| Subordinating conjunction (because, although, if, when...) | Complex (at minimum) |
| Relative pronoun (who, which, that) modifying a noun | Complex (at minimum) |
| BOTH a subordinating conjunction AND a FANBOYS/semicolon | Compound-Complex |

### Trap Recognition

**Trap 1: Compound subject/verb ≠ Compound sentence**
- "The manager and the clerk reviewed the document." → SIMPLE (compound subject, one clause)

**Trap 2: Long sentence ≠ Complex sentence**
- "The senior administrative officer of the Department of Budget and Management in the regional office submitted the comprehensive quarterly financial report to the central office." → SIMPLE (one clause with many phrases)

**Trap 3: "And" doesn't always signal compound**
- "She studied and passed." → SIMPLE (compound verb)
- "She studied, and she passed." → COMPOUND (two independent clauses)

**Trap 4: Prepositional phrases ≠ Dependent clauses**
- "After the meeting" → phrase (no verb) — does NOT make it complex
- "After the meeting ended" → clause (subject + verb) — DOES make it complex

## 4.12 Common Errors in Sentence Structure

### Run-On Sentences

Two or more independent clauses joined without proper punctuation or conjunction:
- ❌ "The report was due Monday the team submitted it on Friday."
- ✅ "The report was due Monday, but the team submitted it on Friday."
- ✅ "The report was due Monday; however, the team submitted it on Friday."

### Comma Splices

Two independent clauses joined by only a comma:
- ❌ "The policy was effective, it reduced complaints by 40%."
- ✅ "The policy was effective, and it reduced complaints by 40%."
- ✅ "The policy was effective; it reduced complaints by 40%."
- ✅ "Because the policy was effective, it reduced complaints by 40%."

### Sentence Fragments

Dependent clauses or phrases presented as complete sentences:
- ❌ "Because the deadline was extended."
- ✅ "Because the deadline was extended, the team had more time."
- ❌ "Although she was the most qualified candidate."
- ✅ "Although she was the most qualified candidate, she was not selected."

### Misuse of Conjunctions

Using a subordinating conjunction when coordination is needed (or vice versa):
- ❌ "She was qualified, because she was not hired." (illogical — "because" implies cause)
- ✅ "She was qualified, but she was not hired." (contrast makes sense)

### Incorrect Clause Identification

Treating a phrase as a clause:
- "In the morning" → NOT a clause (no subject-verb pair)
- "When morning came" → IS a clause (subject: morning, verb: came)

## Step-by-Step Rules for Sentence Classification

### The 4-Step Method

1. **Identify all finite verbs** (conjugated verbs — not infinitives like "to go" or participles like "running" used as adjectives)
2. **Match each verb to its subject** — each subject-verb pair = one clause
3. **Classify each clause** — look for subordinating conjunctions/relative pronouns at the start
4. **Apply the formula:**
   - 1 IC + 0 DC = Simple
   - 2+ IC + 0 DC = Compound
   - 1 IC + 1+ DC = Complex
   - 2+ IC + 1+ DC = Compound-Complex

### Worked Example

> "Although the committee recommended the proposal, the director rejected it, and the project was shelved until funding became available."

**Step 1 — Find verbs:** recommended, rejected, was shelved, became

**Step 2 — Match subjects:**
- committee recommended
- director rejected
- project was shelved
- funding became

**Step 3 — Classify clauses:**
- "Although the committee recommended the proposal" → starts with "although" → DEPENDENT
- "the director rejected it" → no subordinator → INDEPENDENT
- "the project was shelved" → no subordinator → INDEPENDENT (joined by "and")
- "until funding became available" → starts with "until" → DEPENDENT

**Step 4 — Count:** 2 independent + 2 dependent = **Compound-Complex**

## Exam Strategies

### Time Management

- Sentence structure questions should take 30–45 seconds each
- Use the shortcut table first — look for conjunction signals
- Only do full clause analysis if the shortcuts don't give a clear answer

### Process of Elimination

If unsure of the exact type:
1. **Is there more than one clause?** If NO → Simple. Done.
2. **Is there a dependent clause?** If NO → Compound. Done.
3. **Are there two or more independent clauses?** If NO → Complex. If YES → Compound-Complex.

### Red Flags for Wrong Answers

- If the sentence has NO conjunctions and NO semicolons → almost certainly Simple
- If you see "although," "because," "if," "when" → it CANNOT be Simple or Compound
- If you see a comma + FANBOYS between clauses → it CANNOT be Simple or Complex (alone)

## Real CSE-Like Examples with Full Analysis

### Example 1 (Easy)
> "The government employee submitted the report before the deadline."

**Analysis:** One subject-verb pair (employee submitted). "Before the deadline" is a prepositional phrase, NOT a clause (no verb).
**Answer:** Simple

### Example 2 (Easy)
> "The manager reviewed the proposal, and the committee approved it."

**Analysis:** Two subject-verb pairs (manager reviewed, committee approved). Both are independent clauses joined by ", and."
**Answer:** Compound

### Example 3 (Medium)
> "Because the applicant lacked experience, the hiring committee rejected her application."

**Analysis:** Two clauses. "Because the applicant lacked experience" → dependent (starts with "because"). "The hiring committee rejected her application" → independent.
**Answer:** Complex

### Example 4 (Medium)
> "The employee who was assigned to the project completed it ahead of schedule."

**Analysis:** "Who was assigned to the project" is a relative clause (dependent) embedded within the independent clause "The employee completed it ahead of schedule."
**Answer:** Complex

### Example 5 (Hard)
> "Although the budget was limited, the team delivered quality results, and the client expressed satisfaction."

**Analysis:**
- "Although the budget was limited" → dependent (although)
- "the team delivered quality results" → independent
- "the client expressed satisfaction" → independent (joined by ", and")
- Count: 2 independent + 1 dependent
**Answer:** Compound-Complex

### Example 6 (Hard)
> "The director signed the memorandum, and the staff implemented the new policy after the training was completed."

**Analysis:**
- "The director signed the memorandum" → independent
- "the staff implemented the new policy" → independent (joined by ", and")
- "after the training was completed" → dependent (after + subject + verb)
- Count: 2 independent + 1 dependent
**Answer:** Compound-Complex

## Quick Recap

| Sentence Type | Formula | Signal Words |
|---------------|---------|-------------|
| **Simple** | 1 IC + 0 DC | No clause-joining conjunctions |
| **Compound** | 2+ IC + 0 DC | FANBOYS, semicolons |
| **Complex** | 1 IC + 1+ DC | Subordinating conjunctions, relative pronouns |
| **Compound-Complex** | 2+ IC + 1+ DC | Both FANBOYS/semicolons AND subordinators |

**Key reminders:**
- Compound subjects/verbs do NOT make a compound sentence
- Length does NOT determine structure — a 30-word sentence can be simple
- Prepositional phrases are NOT clauses (they lack subject-verb pairs)
- The "stand alone" test is your best tool for classifying clauses
- Punctuation provides clues: comma + FANBOYS = compound signal; subordinator + comma = complex signal

## Memory Aids

### FANBOYS Mnemonic
**F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o — the ONLY coordinating conjunctions in English.

### "SIC" Method for Classification
- **S**ubordinator present? → Complex element exists
- **I**ndependent clauses — how many?
- **C**ount and classify using the formula

### Clause Test Shortcut
Add "I believe that..." before the clause:
- "I believe that she passed the exam." → Makes sense → Independent
- "I believe that because she studied hard." → Doesn't make sense → Dependent

### The "Period Test"
Can you put a period after the clause and have it make sense?
- "She passed the exam." → ✅ Period works → Independent
- "Because she studied hard." → ❌ Period feels wrong → Dependent
