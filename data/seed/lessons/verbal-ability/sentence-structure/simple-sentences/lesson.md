# Simple Sentences

## Explanations

### Introduction

A simple sentence is a sentence with one independent clause and no dependent clauses. That means it has a subject, a verb, and a complete thought. It may be short or long, plain or heavily modified, but it still counts as simple if it contains only one independent clause.

### Why Simple Sentences Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination checks whether you can read sentence structure carefully and quickly. Simple sentences are tested because they help you:

- identify one complete thought without getting distracted by extra words
- distinguish simple sentences from compound and complex sentences
- avoid fragment and run-on errors
- locate the true subject and main verb in a sentence
- handle punctuation confidently in sentence-structure items

### Common Mistakes

Candidates often make these mistakes:

- thinking every short sentence is simple
- thinking every long sentence must be compound or complex
- confusing a phrase with a sentence
- missing the true subject because of modifiers
- assuming punctuation alone tells the sentence type

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify a simple sentence by its clause count
- tell a simple sentence from a fragment, compound sentence, or complex sentence
- find the subject, predicate, and main verb in a simple sentence
- recognize when modifiers do not change the clause count
- repair incomplete sentences into simple sentences

## What Makes a Sentence Simple

### Overview

A sentence is simple when it contains one independent clause and no dependent clauses. The clause may include phrases and modifiers, but those extra words do not create a new clause.

### Core Principle

Count clauses, not length.

If a sentence has only one subject-verb structure that can stand alone as a complete thought, it is simple.

### Visualization

| Sentence type | Clause pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| simple sentence | 1 independent clause | `The clerk filed the memo.` |
| compound sentence | 2 independent clauses | `The clerk filed the memo, and the manager approved it.` |
| complex sentence | 1 independent + 1 dependent clause | `Because the memo was late, the clerk apologized.` |
| compound-complex sentence | 2 independent + 1 dependent clause | `Because the memo was late, the clerk apologized, and the manager helped.` |

### Common Mistakes

Do not confuse a sentence with only a few words for a simple sentence. A simple sentence can be long if it still has only one independent clause.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Is `The supervisor called the applicant on Monday.` a simple sentence?

Reasoning:

- It has one subject and one main verb.
- It has no dependent clause.
- The extra phrase `on Monday` is only a modifier.

Answer: **Yes. It is a simple sentence.**

### Key Insight

Simple means one independent clause, not necessarily short.

### Quick Check

Question: Which option is the simple sentence?
Choices:
- The clerk filed the memo.
- The clerk filed the memo, and the manager approved it.
- Because the memo was late, the clerk apologized.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo.
Rationale: It has one independent clause and no dependent clause.

Question: Which sentence is simple even though it is longer?
Choices:
- The team waited quietly in the lobby.
- The team waited quietly in the lobby, and the trainer arrived later.
- When the team waited quietly in the lobby, the trainer arrived later.
Answer: The team waited quietly in the lobby.
Rationale: The extra adverb phrase does not add another clause.

Question: Which choice has one independent clause and no dependent clauses?
Choices:
- The office reopened after lunch.
- The office reopened after lunch, and the staff returned.
- After lunch, the office reopened, so the staff returned.
Answer: The office reopened after lunch.
Rationale: It contains only one independent clause.

## Subject and Complete Predicate

### Overview

Every simple sentence has a subject and a complete predicate. The subject tells who or what the sentence is about. The complete predicate tells what the subject does, is, or experiences.

### Core Principle

Find the true subject first, then read the complete predicate.

Prepositional phrases and other modifiers may sit between the subject and the verb, but they do not become the subject.

### Visualization

| Part | Example | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| subject | `The clerk` | who performs the action |
| complete predicate | `filed the memo in the cabinet` | what the subject does |
| main verb | `filed` | the action word |

### Common Mistakes

Do not stop at the first noun phrase you see. The real subject may be hidden by a phrase after it.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: In `The stack of reports on the desk was missing.`, what is the subject?

Reasoning:

- The phrase `of reports on the desk` modifies `stack`.
- The head noun `stack` controls the verb.
- So the subject is `The stack`.

Answer: **The stack**

### Key Insight

Modifiers may make the sentence longer, but they do not change the basic subject-verb core.

### Quick Check

Question: What is the subject in `The auditor checked the files on the table.`?
Choices:
- The auditor
- checked the files on the table
- on the table
Answer: The auditor
Rationale: The subject is the noun phrase that controls the verb.

Question: What is the complete predicate in `The manager approved the report before noon.`?
Choices:
- The manager
- approved the report before noon
- the report before noon
Answer: approved the report before noon
Rationale: The complete predicate includes the verb and everything that follows it.

Question: What is the main verb in `The supervisor called the applicant on Monday.`?
Choices:
- applicant
- called
- on Monday
Answer: called
Rationale: `Called` is the action word that carries the sentence.

## Simple Sentences With Modifiers

### Overview

Simple sentences often include modifiers such as prepositional phrases, introductory phrases, appositives, and absolute phrases. Those additions give detail, but they do not create a new clause by themselves.

### Core Principle

If the sentence still has only one independent clause, it is still simple.

### Visualization

| Modifier type | Example | Why it still stays simple |
| --- | --- | --- |
| prepositional phrase | `The clerk filed the memo in the cabinet.` | no new subject-verb pair |
| introductory phrase | `Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo.` | still only one clause |
| appositive | `Maya, the branch coordinator, filed the memo.` | extra noun detail only |
| absolute phrase | `Her hands full, the clerk answered the phone.` | adds background, not a clause |

### Common Mistakes

Do not let commas trick you into thinking a sentence has two clauses. Punctuation may separate phrases, not just clauses.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Why is `Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo.` still a simple sentence?

Reasoning:

- `Before the meeting` is only an introductory phrase.
- The sentence has one independent clause.
- No dependent clause is added.

Answer: **It still has only one independent clause.**

### Key Insight

A modifier can add detail without adding a new clause.

### Quick Check

Question: Which sentence is still simple even though it has a fronted phrase?
Choices:
- Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo.
- Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo, and the manager approved it.
- Because the meeting was late, the clerk filed the memo.
Answer: Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo.
Rationale: The opening phrase is only a modifier.

Question: Which sentence is still simple even though it has an appositive?
Choices:
- Maya, the branch coordinator, filed the memo.
- Maya, the branch coordinator, filed the memo, and the manager approved it.
- Because Maya was the branch coordinator, she filed the memo.
Answer: Maya, the branch coordinator, filed the memo.
Rationale: The appositive adds information but does not create another clause.

Question: Which sentence is still simple even though it has an absolute phrase?
Choices:
- Her hands full, the clerk answered the phone.
- Her hands full, the clerk answered the phone, and the supervisor waited.
- Since her hands were full, the clerk answered the phone.
Answer: Her hands full, the clerk answered the phone.
Rationale: The absolute phrase adds background detail, but the sentence still has one main clause.

## Simple Sentences vs Fragments and Other Sentence Types

### Overview

A fragment is incomplete. A compound sentence has two independent clauses. A complex sentence has one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. A simple sentence has only one independent clause.

### Core Principle

Ask these questions:

1. Does the group have a subject and a verb?
2. Does it express a complete thought?
3. Does it contain only one independent clause?

If the answer is yes to all three, it is simple.

### Visualization

| Type | What it contains | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| simple sentence | one independent clause | `The office reopened after lunch.` |
| fragment | incomplete thought | `After lunch in the office.` |
| compound sentence | two independent clauses | `The office reopened, and the staff returned.` |
| complex sentence | one independent + one dependent clause | `After lunch, the office reopened.` |

### Common Mistakes

Do not call a sentence simple just because it is not long. Also, do not call a fragment a sentence just because it starts with a capital letter.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which group is a fragment?

`After lunch in the office`

Reasoning:

- It does not contain a complete subject-verb structure.
- It does not express a complete thought.
- It is a fragment.

Answer: **After lunch in the office**

### Key Insight

Simple sentences are complete, but not all complete-looking groups are simple sentences.

### Quick Check

Question: Which option is a simple sentence and not a fragment?
Choices:
- The courier delivered the package by noon.
- By noon in the lobby.
- Because the package was late
Answer: The courier delivered the package by noon.
Rationale: It is a complete independent clause.

Question: Which option is a simple sentence and not a compound sentence?
Choices:
- The branch closed early on Friday.
- The branch closed early on Friday, and the staff left.
- The branch closed early on Friday, but the staff stayed.
Answer: The branch closed early on Friday.
Rationale: It has only one independent clause.

Question: Which option is a simple sentence and not a complex sentence?
Choices:
- The children played outside during recess.
- When the children played outside during recess, the bell rang.
- Because the children played outside during recess, the bell rang.
Answer: The children played outside during recess.
Rationale: It has one independent clause and no dependent clause.

## Punctuation and Repair

### Overview

Simple sentences usually end with a period, question mark, or exclamation mark. If a sentence has an introductory clause or a nonessential appositive, it may need commas, but the sentence is still simple as long as it has only one independent clause.

### Core Principle

Punctuation helps you see structure, but it does not create structure by itself.

Fragments are repaired by adding the missing subject, verb, or complete thought. Run-ons are repaired by splitting the clauses or joining them correctly.

### Visualization

| Pattern | Correct form |
| --- | --- |
| simple sentence | `The manager approved the report.` |
| introductory phrase | `Before noon, the manager approved the report.` |
| appositive | `Maya, the branch coordinator, filed the memo.` |
| repaired fragment | `The clerk filed the memo in the cabinet.` |

### Common Mistakes

Do not assume that a comma always means there are two clauses. A comma can also separate an introductory phrase or an appositive.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: How do you fix `Before the meeting` into a simple sentence?

Reasoning:

- The original group is only a phrase.
- It needs a subject and a verb.
- Adding a subject and verb creates a complete simple sentence.

Answer: **Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo.**

### Key Insight

To repair a fragment into a simple sentence, add whatever is missing so the sentence has one complete independent clause.

### Quick Check

Question: Which revision correctly punctuates the introductory phrase?
Choices:
- Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo.
- Before the meeting the clerk filed the memo.
- Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo, and the manager approved it.
Answer: Before the meeting, the clerk filed the memo.
Rationale: The introductory phrase needs a comma, and the result is still a simple sentence.

Question: Which revision correctly punctuates the appositive?
Choices:
- Maya, the branch coordinator, filed the memo.
- Maya the branch coordinator filed the memo.
- Maya, the branch coordinator filed the memo.
Answer: Maya, the branch coordinator, filed the memo.
Rationale: The nonessential appositive should be set off by commas.

Question: Which revision turns the fragment into a simple sentence?
Choices:
- The clerk filed the memo in the cabinet.
- In the cabinet.
- Because the clerk filed the memo.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo in the cabinet.
Rationale: It adds a subject and a verb to make one complete independent clause.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

`The office reopened after lunch.` is a simple sentence because it has one independent clause. The phrase `after lunch` adds time, but it does not add another clause.

### Example 2

`The supervisor, the branch coordinator, signed the notice.` is still a simple sentence. The appositive adds information about the supervisor, but the sentence still has only one main clause.

### Example 3

`Because the office was closed, the clerk went home.` is not a simple sentence. It is a complex sentence because it has one dependent clause and one independent clause.

### Example 4

`The clerk filed the memo, and the manager approved it.` is a compound sentence. It has two independent clauses joined correctly.

## Key Takeaways

- A simple sentence has one independent clause and no dependent clauses.
- A simple sentence can be short or long.
- Modifiers do not change the clause count.
- Find the true subject and the complete predicate before deciding.
- Fragments are incomplete; run-ons join independent clauses incorrectly.
- Commas can separate introductory phrases or appositives without changing a sentence into compound or complex.

## Summary

Simple sentences are one of the most important building blocks in English sentence structure. In the Civil Service Examination, they are useful because they train you to count clauses carefully, identify the true subject and predicate, and avoid confusing modifiers with extra clauses. If you can spot a simple sentence quickly, you can also spot fragments, run-ons, and longer sentence types with much more confidence.
