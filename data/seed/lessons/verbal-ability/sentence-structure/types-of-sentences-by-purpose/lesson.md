# Types of Sentences by Purpose

## Explanations

### Introduction

Every sentence you speak or write has a **communicative purpose** — it either states something, asks something, commands something, or expresses strong emotion. Understanding sentence purpose is one of the most fundamental grammar skills tested in the Philippine Civil Service Examination because it reveals whether you can analyze how language functions in real communication.

Sentence purpose matters because:
- Government communication relies on precise sentence types: directives use imperatives, memoranda use declaratives, inquiries use interrogatives
- The CSE Verbal Ability section frequently asks you to classify sentences or identify their communicative function
- Misidentifying sentence purpose leads to misunderstanding tone, intent, and appropriate responses in professional settings
- Punctuation alone does not always determine sentence type — context and intention are equally important

### Why Sentence Classification Is Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Exam tests sentence types by purpose because:
- Public servants must draft clear communications where sentence purpose matches intent
- Classification questions test multiple skills simultaneously: grammar awareness, punctuation knowledge, and contextual analysis
- It is a foundational concept that supports more advanced grammar topics (reported speech, sentence combining, paragraph coherence)
- Filipino examinees commonly confuse polite requests (imperative) with questions (interrogative) due to cross-linguistic influence

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. Classifying any sentence ending with a period as declarative (imperative sentences also end with periods)
2. Assuming all sentences with question marks are interrogative (rhetorical questions may function as exclamatory or declarative)
3. Confusing polite requests ("Could you please close the door?") with genuine questions
4. Overlooking the implied subject "you" in imperative sentences
5. Classifying exclamatory sentences as declarative because they state something emphatically
6. Relying solely on punctuation instead of analyzing communicative intent
7. Misidentifying indirect questions as interrogative sentences
8. Confusing strong commands with exclamatory sentences

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Identify the four major sentence types by purpose (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory)
- Distinguish between sentence types based on communicative function, not just punctuation
- Analyze punctuation and tone to determine sentence purpose accurately
- Recognize imperative sentences even when they appear polite or question-like
- Classify sentences in formal, professional, and conversational contexts
- Apply elimination strategies to solve CSE-style sentence classification questions efficiently
- Transform sentences from one type to another while preserving meaning
- Identify common traps and misleading sentence structures in exam questions

### 4.1 What Are Types of Sentences by Purpose?
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The principle behind this operation follows from the fundamental properties of arithmetic. Understanding the "why" — not just the "how" — lets you recognize when to apply this method in unfamiliar problem contexts on the CSE.


**Sentences classified by purpose** are grouped according to their **communicative function** — what the speaker or writer intends to accomplish with the sentence.

There are **four types**:

| Type | Purpose | Typical Punctuation |
|------|---------|-------------------|
| Declarative | Makes a statement or expresses a fact/opinion | Period (.) |
| Interrogative | Asks a question | Question mark (?) |
| Imperative | Gives a command, request, or instruction | Period (.) or exclamation mark (!) |
| Exclamatory | Expresses strong emotion or surprise | Exclamation mark (!) |

#### The Relationship Between Purpose and Punctuation

Punctuation is a **clue**, not a **rule**. Consider:
- "Please sit down." — ends with a period but is **imperative** (a request)
- "What a beautiful day." — could end with a period but is **exclamatory** (strong feeling)
- "You're leaving already?" — ends with a question mark and is **interrogative**, but could also express surprise

The key principle: **Always analyze the speaker's intent first, then confirm with punctuation.**

#### Why Speakers Use Different Sentence Types

Language is purposeful. We choose sentence types based on:
- **Information sharing** → declarative ("The office closes at 5 PM.")
- **Information seeking** → interrogative ("What time does the office close?")
- **Action directing** → imperative ("Close the office at 5 PM.")
- **Emotion expressing** → exclamatory ("The office is already closed!")

In professional communication, choosing the wrong sentence type can change the tone entirely. A command phrased as a question sounds tentative; a statement phrased as an exclamation sounds unprofessional.


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "The formula always works the same way regardless of the problem context."

> **Why it fails:** CSE problems often present variations where the standard formula must be adapted. Blindly applying a memorized formula without checking the context leads to systematic errors.

> **Correct model:** Always read the problem to identify what type of relationship exists (direct, inverse, part-whole, etc.), then apply the appropriate formula. Verify your answer makes sense in the problem's context before selecting it.

### 4.2 Declarative Sentences

#### Definition

A **declarative sentence** makes a statement. It declares a fact, expresses an opinion, provides information, or describes something. It is the most common sentence type in both spoken and written English.

#### Key Characteristics
- States information (facts, opinions, descriptions, explanations)
- Ends with a **period** (.)
- Uses standard subject-verb word order
- Tone is neutral or informative
- Does NOT request action, ask for information, or express overwhelming emotion

#### Punctuation Rule
Declarative sentences always end with a period.

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- "The meeting starts at 8:00 AM."
- "Manila is the capital of the Philippines."
- "She works in the Department of Education."

**Medium:**
- "The committee believes that the proposal requires further review."
- "According to the latest census, the population has increased by 1.7 percent."
- "Employees who fail to submit their reports on time will receive a written warning."

**Hard (CSE-style):**
- "The memorandum states that all division chiefs must attend the quarterly planning session." (Declarative — it reports what the memo states; it does not command attendance)
- "Whether or not the budget is approved remains uncertain." (Declarative — it states a condition of uncertainty)
- "I wonder if the director has signed the document." (Declarative — despite containing "wonder" and an indirect question, it makes a statement about the speaker's mental state)

#### Common Trap: Indirect Questions

An **indirect question** is a declarative sentence that contains a question within it but does NOT ask a question directly.

- "I asked whether the report was ready." → **Declarative** (states what I asked)
- "She wants to know if you are coming." → **Declarative** (states what she wants)
- "The supervisor inquired about the deadline." → **Declarative** (reports an inquiry)

These end with periods and make statements — they are NOT interrogative.


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "If my computed answer is close to one of the choices, it must be right."

> **Why it fails:** The CSE deliberately includes distractors that result from common errors — using the wrong operation, misidentifying the proportion type, or reversing the ratio. A "close" answer could be the result of a systematic mistake that the test writers anticipated.

> **Correct model:** Verify your setup before computing. Check that you've identified the correct proportion type, set up the equation properly, and solved accurately. A wrong setup with correct arithmetic still produces a wrong answer — and the CSE will include that wrong answer among the choices.

### 4.3 Interrogative Sentences
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** When you follow this procedure, you're exploiting a mathematical invariant — something that stays constant regardless of how you manipulate the numbers. Identifying that invariant is the key to solving problems efficiently rather than memorizing steps.


#### Definition

An **interrogative sentence** asks a question. Its primary purpose is to **seek information, confirmation, or clarification** from the listener or reader.

#### Key Characteristics
- Asks a question directly
- Ends with a **question mark** (?)
- Often uses inverted word order (auxiliary/verb before subject)
- May begin with a WH-word (who, what, where, when, why, how)
- Expects a response from the listener

#### Types of Interrogative Sentences

**1. Yes/No Questions** — expect "yes" or "no" as an answer
- "Did you submit the report?"
- "Is the director available?"
- "Have the documents been signed?"

**2. WH-Questions** — seek specific information
- "Where is the conference room?"
- "When does the training start?"
- "Who approved this requisition?"
- "How many copies do we need?"

**3. Alternative Questions** — offer choices
- "Would you prefer the morning session or the afternoon session?"
- "Shall we meet on Monday or Tuesday?"

**4. Tag Questions** — seek confirmation
- "The deadline is Friday, isn't it?"
- "You've completed the form, haven't you?"

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- "What is your name?"
- "Are you a government employee?"
- "Where do you work?"

**Medium:**
- "Has the committee reached a consensus on the proposed amendments?"
- "To whom should the appeal be addressed?"
- "Under which section of the Civil Service Law does this provision fall?"

**Hard (CSE-style):**
- "Could you explain why the disbursement was delayed?" (Interrogative — despite the polite tone, it directly asks for an explanation)
- "Who among the applicants meets all the qualifications listed in the job posting?" (Interrogative — asks for identification)

#### Common Trap: Rhetorical Questions

A **rhetorical question** is phrased as a question but does NOT expect an answer. It makes a point or expresses emotion.

- "Who doesn't want a promotion?" → The speaker is not seeking names; they are asserting that everyone wants a promotion.
- "Isn't it obvious that we need more staff?" → The speaker is asserting that the need is obvious.

For CSE purposes, rhetorical questions are still classified as **interrogative** because they use question structure and punctuation. However, some exam questions may test whether you recognize that the *function* is closer to declarative or exclamatory.

### 4.4 Imperative Sentences

#### Definition

An **imperative sentence** gives a command, makes a request, offers an instruction, or provides advice. It directs someone to do (or not do) something.

#### Key Characteristics
- Directs action (commands, requests, instructions, advice, invitations)
- The subject is usually **implied "you"** (not stated)
- Can end with a **period** (.) for calm commands/requests or an **exclamation mark** (!) for urgent commands
- Tone ranges from polite to forceful
- Does NOT ask a question or merely state a fact

#### The Implied Subject "You"

In imperative sentences, the subject "you" is understood but not written:
- "[You] Submit the report by Friday."
- "[You] Please take a seat."
- "[You] Do not use your phone during the meeting."

This is the most reliable way to identify an imperative sentence: if you can insert "you" as the subject and the sentence still makes sense as a direction, it is imperative.

#### Tone Variations

| Tone | Example |
|------|---------|
| Polite request | "Please forward the email to all staff." |
| Neutral instruction | "Complete Form 212 in triplicate." |
| Strong command | "Report to the office immediately." |
| Urgent command | "Evacuate the building now!" |
| Advice | "Consider applying for the position." |
| Invitation | "Join us for the orientation on Monday." |

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- "Close the door."
- "Please sit down."
- "Write your name on the form."

**Medium:**
- "Submit all supporting documents to the Human Resource Division before the deadline."
- "Do not disclose confidential information to unauthorized personnel."
- "Kindly coordinate with the Records Section regarding the release of your certificate."

**Hard (CSE-style):**
- "Let us review the minutes of the last meeting." (Imperative — "let us" is a first-person inclusive command/suggestion)
- "Please be advised that the office will be closed on Monday." (Imperative — despite sounding informational, "be advised" is a directive to the reader)
- "Never assume that verbal instructions replace written memoranda." (Imperative — "never assume" is a command with implied "you")

#### Common Trap: Polite Requests That Look Like Questions

Some sentences use question form but function as imperatives:
- "Could you please close the window?" → Functionally imperative (a polite request), but grammatically interrogative
- "Would you mind submitting the report today?" → Polite request

**CSE Rule of Thumb:** If the sentence uses question structure AND a question mark, classify it as **interrogative** unless the exam specifically asks about communicative function. When in doubt on the CSE, follow the punctuation.

However, sentences like "Please close the window." are clearly imperative — no question structure, no question mark.

### 4.5 Exclamatory Sentences
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** This shortcut works because it's a special case of the more general rule. By understanding the underlying principle, you can verify your answer logically even if you forget the exact formula under exam pressure.


#### Definition

An **exclamatory sentence** expresses strong emotion — surprise, excitement, anger, admiration, disbelief, or any intense feeling. It conveys emphasis beyond what a simple declarative statement would.

#### Key Characteristics
- Expresses strong emotion or emphasis
- Ends with an **exclamation mark** (!)
- Often begins with "What" or "How" (but not as a question)
- Tone is emotional, emphatic, or intense
- Differs from declarative by the degree of feeling expressed

#### Structure Patterns

**Pattern 1: "What + noun phrase + !"**
- "What a wonderful opportunity!"
- "What an incredible achievement!"
- "What a disaster!"

**Pattern 2: "How + adjective/adverb + !"**
- "How beautiful the sunset is!"
- "How quickly time passes!"
- "How unfortunate!"

**Pattern 3: Statement with strong emotion + !**
- "I can't believe we won!"
- "That was absolutely brilliant!"
- "This is outrageous!"

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- "What a beautiful day!"
- "How amazing!"
- "That's incredible!"

**Medium:**
- "What an outstanding performance the team delivered at the national competition!"
- "How disappointing that the project was cancelled after months of preparation!"
- "Never in my career have I seen such dedication!"

**Hard (CSE-style):**
- "What a remarkable turnaround the agency achieved in just one fiscal year!" (Exclamatory — "What a" + noun phrase expressing admiration)
- "How irresponsible of the committee to release the findings without verification!" (Exclamatory — "How" + adjective expressing disapproval)

#### Common Trap: Exclamatory vs. Imperative with Exclamation Mark

Not every sentence with an exclamation mark is exclamatory:
- "Stop right there!" → **Imperative** (commands action, implied "you")
- "What a surprise!" → **Exclamatory** (expresses emotion, no action directed)
- "Run!" → **Imperative** (commands action)
- "We won!" → **Exclamatory** (expresses excitement)

**Test:** Can you insert "you" as the subject directing action? → Imperative. Does it express emotion without directing action? → Exclamatory.

### 4.6 Punctuation and Sentence Purpose

#### The Punctuation-Purpose Connection

| Punctuation | Primary Association | But Also Used For |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Period (.) | Declarative | Imperative (calm commands), indirect questions |
| Question mark (?) | Interrogative | Rhetorical questions, polite requests |
| Exclamation mark (!) | Exclamatory | Urgent imperatives, emphatic declarations |

#### When Punctuation Misleads

**Scenario 1: Period ≠ Always Declarative**
- "Hand me the file." → Imperative (command)
- "Please have a seat." → Imperative (polite request)
- "Let's begin." → Imperative (suggestion/command)

**Scenario 2: Question Mark ≠ Always a Genuine Question**
- "Isn't this the most beautiful view?" → Could be exclamatory in function
- "Would you kindly stop talking?" → Polite imperative in function
- "Who cares?" → Rhetorical (no answer expected)

**Scenario 3: Exclamation Mark ≠ Always Exclamatory**
- "Get out!" → Imperative (urgent command)
- "Don't touch that!" → Imperative (prohibition)
- "Attention!" → Imperative (calling to order)

#### Punctuation Analysis Strategy for the CSE

1. **Read the sentence completely** — do not jump to conclusions from the end punctuation alone
2. **Identify the speaker's intent** — is the speaker stating, asking, directing, or emoting?
3. **Check for implied "you"** — if present, likely imperative
4. **Check for question structure** — inverted word order or WH-word at the start
5. **Assess emotional intensity** — strong feeling without directing action suggests exclamatory
6. **Confirm with punctuation** — use punctuation as the final confirmation, not the first filter

### 4.7 Contextual Analysis of Sentence Purpose

#### Analyzing Sentence Intention

Sometimes the same words can serve different purposes depending on context:

**"The door is open."**
- Declarative: Stating a fact about the door's state
- Could function as an imperative hint: "Close the door" (indirect speech act)

**"It's cold in here."**
- Declarative: Stating a temperature observation
- Could function as an imperative hint: "Turn on the heater" or "Close the window"

For CSE purposes, classify based on the **grammatical structure and punctuation**, not the implied meaning, unless the question specifically asks about communicative function.

#### Step-by-Step Contextual Analysis

1. **Read the sentence aloud mentally** — hear the natural intonation
2. **Identify the grammatical subject** — is it stated or implied?
3. **Determine the verb mood** — indicative (stating), interrogative (asking), or imperative (directing)?
4. **Check the end punctuation** — does it match your analysis?
5. **Consider the context** — workplace, academic, conversational?
6. **Make your classification** — declarative, interrogative, imperative, or exclamatory

### 4.8 Formal and Professional Communication

#### Sentence Types in Workplace Writing

| Document Type | Dominant Sentence Type | Example |
|---------------|----------------------|---------|
| Memoranda | Declarative + Imperative | "All employees shall submit..." / "Submit your forms by..." |
| Official letters | Declarative | "We wish to inform you that..." |
| Policy manuals | Imperative | "Employees must report to..." |
| Meeting minutes | Declarative | "The committee agreed that..." |
| Email inquiries | Interrogative | "Could you confirm whether..." |
| Performance reviews | Declarative | "The employee demonstrated..." |

#### Professional Tone and Sentence Purpose

In formal writing:
- **Declarative sentences** dominate because they convey information objectively
- **Imperative sentences** appear in directives, policies, and instructions — often softened with "please," "kindly," or "shall"
- **Interrogative sentences** appear in correspondence seeking information
- **Exclamatory sentences** are rare in formal writing — they suggest emotional bias

#### Examples in Government Communication

- "The Civil Service Commission hereby announces the schedule of examinations." (Declarative)
- "All applicants must submit their requirements on or before the deadline." (Imperative — "must submit" directs action)
- "When is the next examination date?" (Interrogative)
- "What an honor it is to serve the Filipino people!" (Exclamatory — appropriate only in speeches, not memos)

### 4.9 Tone and Emotional Expression

#### How Tone Relates to Sentence Type

| Tone | Likely Sentence Type | Signal Words |
|------|---------------------|-------------|
| Neutral/informative | Declarative | states, reports, describes |
| Curious/seeking | Interrogative | who, what, where, when, why, how |
| Directive/authoritative | Imperative | do, submit, report, ensure, avoid |
| Polite/requesting | Imperative | please, kindly, let us |
| Emotional/emphatic | Exclamatory | what a, how, incredible, amazing |

#### Emotional Intensity Scale

1. **Low intensity** → Declarative: "The results were good."
2. **Moderate intensity** → Declarative with emphasis: "The results were remarkably good."
3. **High intensity** → Exclamatory: "What incredible results!"

#### Respectful Commands vs. Harsh Commands

- "Please submit your report." → Polite imperative (period)
- "Submit your report immediately." → Firm imperative (period)
- "Submit your report NOW!" → Urgent imperative (exclamation mark)

All three are imperative regardless of tone — the purpose (directing action) remains the same.

### 4.10 Sentence Transformation

#### Converting Between Sentence Types

Understanding how to transform sentences between types deepens your grasp of sentence purpose and is tested in the CSE.

#### Declarative → Interrogative

| Declarative | Interrogative |
|-------------|---------------|
| "She completed the training." | "Did she complete the training?" |
| "The office is closed today." | "Is the office closed today?" |
| "They approved the budget." | "Did they approve the budget?" |

**Method:** Move the auxiliary verb before the subject. If no auxiliary exists, add "do/does/did."

#### Declarative → Imperative

| Declarative | Imperative |
|-------------|-----------|
| "You should submit the form." | "Submit the form." |
| "Employees must wear their IDs." | "Wear your IDs." |
| "You need to attend the meeting." | "Attend the meeting." |

**Method:** Remove the subject "you" and any modal verbs; begin with the base verb.

#### Declarative → Exclamatory

| Declarative | Exclamatory |
|-------------|-------------|
| "The view is beautiful." | "What a beautiful view!" / "How beautiful the view is!" |
| "She is talented." | "How talented she is!" |
| "That was a great performance." | "What a great performance that was!" |

**Method:** Add "What a/an" before a noun phrase or "How" before an adjective/adverb; add exclamation mark.

#### Interrogative → Declarative

| Interrogative | Declarative |
|---------------|-------------|
| "Is the report ready?" | "The report is ready." |
| "Did he pass the exam?" | "He passed the exam." |
| "Where is the office?" | "The office is on the third floor." |

**Method:** Restore standard subject-verb order; replace question mark with period.

#### Imperative → Interrogative (Polite Request)

| Imperative | Polite Interrogative |
|-----------|---------------------|
| "Close the window." | "Could you close the window?" |
| "Submit the report." | "Would you submit the report?" |
| "Help me with this." | "Can you help me with this?" |

**Method:** Add a modal verb (could, would, can) + subject "you"; add question mark.

### 4.11 Elimination Strategies for the CSE

#### Strategy 1: Check for Implied "You"

If you can logically insert "you" as the doer of the action → **Imperative**
- "[You] File the documents alphabetically." ✓ → Imperative
- "The documents are filed alphabetically." ✗ cannot insert "you" as doer → Declarative

#### Strategy 2: Check for Question Structure

If the sentence has inverted word order (verb/auxiliary before subject) AND a question mark → **Interrogative**
- "Has the meeting been rescheduled?" → verb "has" before subject "meeting" → Interrogative

#### Strategy 3: Check for Emotional Markers

If the sentence uses "What a/an..." or "How..." + adjective with an exclamation mark → **Exclamatory**
- "What a remarkable achievement!" → Exclamatory
- "How unfortunate!" → Exclamatory

#### Strategy 4: Default to Declarative

If none of the above apply and the sentence simply states information with a period → **Declarative**

This is your safest fallback because declarative is the most common sentence type.

#### Strategy 5: Eliminate Wrong Answers

On the CSE, if you are unsure:
1. Can you insert "you" as the subject directing action? If yes → Imperative (eliminate others)
2. Does it end with "?" and ask something? If yes → Interrogative (eliminate others)
3. Does it express strong emotion with "!" and no directed action? If yes → Exclamatory (eliminate others)
4. If none of the above → Declarative

### 4.12 Common Errors in Sentence Classification

#### Error 1: Confusing Interrogative and Declarative Forms

**Indirect questions are declarative, not interrogative:**
- "I wonder where she went." → Declarative (states what I wonder)
- "He asked if the meeting was cancelled." → Declarative (reports what he asked)
- "The question is whether we can afford it." → Declarative (states what the question is)

**Test:** Does the sentence itself ask a question? Or does it merely *report* or *contain* a question? If it reports → Declarative.

#### Error 2: Misunderstanding Polite Commands

**Sentences with "please" or "kindly" are imperative, not declarative:**
- "Please submit your application." → Imperative (directs action)
- "Kindly refrain from using your phone." → Imperative (directs action)

**Test:** Remove "please/kindly" — does it still direct someone to do something? If yes → Imperative.

#### Error 3: Overlooking Emotional Tone

**Emphatic statements with exclamation marks are exclamatory:**
- "I absolutely love this job!" → Exclamatory (strong positive emotion)
- "That was the worst presentation ever!" → Exclamatory (strong negative emotion)

**But:** "Stop talking!" → Imperative (directs action, even with exclamation mark)

#### Error 4: Relying Only on Punctuation

**The same sentence can change type with different punctuation:**
- "You're leaving." → Declarative (states a fact)
- "You're leaving?" → Interrogative (asks for confirmation)
- "You're leaving!" → Exclamatory (expresses surprise)

This proves that punctuation alone is insufficient — you must analyze intent.

#### Error 5: Misclassifying Rhetorical Questions

**Rhetorical questions are grammatically interrogative:**
- "Who wouldn't want to work here?" → Interrogative (question structure + question mark)
- Even though the speaker doesn't expect an answer, the grammatical form is interrogative

For CSE purposes: classify by **grammatical form** unless the question explicitly asks about **communicative function**.

### Step-by-Step Classification Method

Use this systematic approach for every CSE sentence classification question:

**Step 1:** Read the entire sentence carefully.
**Step 2:** Identify the end punctuation (., ?, !)
**Step 3:** Determine the speaker's primary intent:
- Stating/informing → Declarative
- Asking/seeking → Interrogative
- Directing/commanding → Imperative
- Emoting/exclaiming → Exclamatory
**Step 4:** Check for structural markers:
- Implied "you" + base verb → Imperative
- Inverted word order + ? → Interrogative
- "What a/How" + emotion + ! → Exclamatory
- Subject + verb + information + . → Declarative
**Step 5:** Confirm your answer matches both intent and structure.
**Step 6:** If structure and intent conflict, prioritize **grammatical structure** on the CSE.

### Real CSE-Like Examples with Analysis

#### Easy Examples

**1.** "The Civil Service Examination is held twice a year."
- **Answer:** Declarative
- **Analysis:** States a fact about exam frequency. Standard subject-verb order. Ends with period.

**2.** "What is the passing score for the Professional level?"
- **Answer:** Interrogative
- **Analysis:** WH-question seeking specific information. Ends with question mark.

**3.** "Submit your application before the deadline."
- **Answer:** Imperative
- **Analysis:** Directs action. Implied subject "you." Begins with base verb. Ends with period.

**4.** "What an honor to serve the public!"
- **Answer:** Exclamatory
- **Analysis:** "What an" + noun phrase expressing strong admiration. Ends with exclamation mark.

#### Medium Examples

**5.** "All government employees are required to file their Statement of Assets, Liabilities, and Net Worth annually."
- **Answer:** Declarative
- **Analysis:** States a requirement as information. Does not direct the reader to do it — it reports what is required.

**6.** "Do not disclose any information regarding the examination content."
- **Answer:** Imperative
- **Analysis:** Negative command. "Do not" + base verb. Implied subject "you." Directs the reader to refrain from action.

**7.** "How many items are included in the Verbal Ability subtest?"
- **Answer:** Interrogative
- **Analysis:** WH-question (how many) seeking numerical information. Inverted word order. Question mark.

**8.** "How inspiring it is to see young professionals dedicated to public service!"
- **Answer:** Exclamatory
- **Analysis:** "How" + adjective expressing admiration. Exclamation mark. No action directed.

#### Hard Examples

**9.** "I wonder whether the results have been released."
- **Answer:** Declarative
- **Analysis:** Indirect question. The sentence states what the speaker wonders — it does not ask a question directly. Ends with period.

**10.** "Let all applicants be informed of the revised schedule."
- **Answer:** Imperative
- **Analysis:** "Let" + noun + "be informed" is a formal command structure. It directs that applicants should be informed.

**11.** "Never have I witnessed such blatant disregard for protocol!"
- **Answer:** Exclamatory
- **Analysis:** Despite inverted word order (which might suggest interrogative), the sentence expresses strong emotion (disbelief/outrage). Exclamation mark confirms.

**12.** "The question remains: will the agency meet its targets this quarter?"
- **Answer:** Declarative
- **Analysis:** The main clause "The question remains" is declarative — it states that a question exists. The embedded question is subordinate. The sentence as a whole makes a statement.

### Quick Recap

| Type | What It Does | Key Signal | End Punctuation |
|------|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Declarative** | States facts, opinions, information | Subject + verb + info | Period (.) |
| **Interrogative** | Asks questions | Inverted order / WH-word | Question mark (?) |
| **Imperative** | Commands, requests, instructs | Implied "you" + base verb | Period (.) or (!) |
| **Exclamatory** | Expresses strong emotion | "What a/How" + emotion | Exclamation mark (!) |

**Key Traps to Remember:**
- Indirect questions → Declarative (not interrogative)
- "Please/Kindly" + action → Imperative (not declarative)
- Exclamation mark + directed action → Imperative (not exclamatory)
- Rhetorical questions → Still interrogative grammatically
- "Let us/Let all" → Imperative

### Memory Aids

#### Mnemonic: D-I-I-E

**D**eclare → **I**nquire → **I**nstruct → **E**xclaim

Think of it as the four things you can do with a sentence:
- **D**eclare a fact
- **I**nquire for information
- **I**nstruct someone to act
- **E**xclaim with emotion

#### Punctuation Memory Trick: "PIE"

- **P**eriod → Declarative or Imperative (two types share this!)
- **I**nterrogation mark → Interrogative
- **E**xclamation mark → Exclamatory or urgent Imperative (two types share this!)

Since periods and exclamation marks are shared, you MUST look beyond punctuation.

#### The "You" Test for Imperatives

If you can naturally insert "you" at the beginning as the person being directed:
- "[You] Submit the form." ✓ → Imperative
- "[You] The form was submitted." ✗ → Not imperative (Declarative)

#### The "Answer" Test for Interrogatives

If the sentence expects a verbal response (yes/no, a name, a time, a place):
- "Where is the office?" → Expects "On the third floor" → Interrogative ✓
- "I wonder where the office is." → Does not expect a response → Declarative

#### The "Feeling" Test for Exclamatory

If the primary purpose is to express how the speaker FEELS (not to inform, ask, or direct):
- "What a disaster!" → Expresses feeling → Exclamatory ✓
- "That was a disaster." → Informs about what happened → Declarative

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Sentence purpose (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory) is determined by the speaker's communicative intent, which is signaled by word order and punctuation. Declarative uses subject-verb order and a period; interrogative inverts the auxiliary and subject (or uses a question word) and ends with a question mark. These structural signals let readers identify purpose instantly without analyzing meaning.
