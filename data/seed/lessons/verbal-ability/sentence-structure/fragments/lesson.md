# Fragments

## Explanations

### Introduction

A fragment is a group of words that looks like a sentence but is not complete. It may be missing a subject, a verb, or a complete thought. In CSE items, fragments often hide inside long openings, dependent clauses, and phrase-heavy choices.

### Why Fragments Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination checks whether you can recognize incomplete structures quickly and repair them correctly. Fragments are tested because they show whether you can:

- spot a missing subject, verb, or main clause
- tell a phrase from a clause
- avoid choosing a group of words that only looks complete
- repair a fragment without changing the intended meaning
- read carefully under time pressure

### Common Mistakes

Candidates often make these mistakes:

- calling a dependent clause a complete sentence
- confusing a phrase with a clause
- missing the true subject when the sentence starts with a modifier
- thinking a period or comma fixes an incomplete idea
- assuming a long phrase is automatically a sentence

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify sentence fragments quickly
- classify fragments by what is missing
- distinguish subjectless, verbless, dependent-clause, and phrase fragments
- repair fragments into complete sentences
- separate fragments from complete sentences and clauses

## What Makes a Fragment Incomplete

### Overview

A fragment is incomplete because it does not express a full thought. It may be missing a subject, missing a verb, or missing the complete idea needed for a sentence to stand alone.

### Core Principle

Count structure, not appearance.

If the words do not form one complete independent clause, the group is a fragment.

### Visualization

| Type | Example | Why it is incomplete |
| --- | --- | --- |
| complete sentence | `The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.` | has a subject, verb, and complete thought |
| subjectless fragment | `Reviewed the memo before lunch.` | missing the subject |
| verbless fragment | `The memo for the audit.` | missing the verb |
| dependent-clause fragment | `Because the office closed early.` | missing a complete thought |

### Common Mistakes

Do not assume that a capital letter and a period make a sentence complete. A fragment can still look polished on the surface.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Why is `Because the office closed early.` a fragment?

Reasoning:

- It has a subject and a verb.
- It begins with `because`, so it is dependent.
- It cannot stand alone as a complete thought.

Answer: **It is a dependent-clause fragment.**

### Key Insight

A fragment is incomplete because the sentence idea is unfinished, not because it is short.

### Quick Check

Question: Which group is a fragment?
Choices:
- The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
- Because the office closed early.
- The memo for the audit.
Answer: Because the office closed early.
Rationale: It is a dependent clause that does not express a complete thought.

Question: Which group can stand alone as a complete sentence?
Choices:
- Reviewed the memo before lunch.
- The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
- Before the meeting in the lobby.
Answer: The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
Rationale: It has one complete independent clause.

Question: Which description best fits a fragment?
Choices:
- A complete thought with one subject and one verb
- A group of words that cannot stand alone
- Two independent clauses joined correctly
Answer: A group of words that cannot stand alone
Rationale: A fragment is incomplete and needs more structure.

## Missing Subject or Missing Verb

### Overview

Some fragments are incomplete because they lack a subject or a verb. A subjectless fragment has a verb but no doer. A verbless fragment has a subject-like noun phrase but no finite verb.

### Core Principle

A sentence needs both a subject and a verb.

If one part is missing, the structure cannot stand alone.

### Visualization

| Type | Example | Missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| subjectless fragment | `Reviewed the memo before lunch.` | subject |
| verbless fragment | `The memo for the audit.` | verb |
| complete sentence | `The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.` | nothing |

### Common Mistakes

Do not stop after spotting a verb or a noun phrase. Ask whether the group has both parts of a sentence.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Why is `Reviewed the memo before lunch.` incomplete?

Reasoning:

- It has a verb.
- It has an object and a time phrase.
- It has no subject telling who did the action.

Answer: **It is missing a subject.**

### Key Insight

A fragment can look active and detailed while still missing one of the two basic sentence parts.

### Quick Check

Question: Which fragment is missing a subject?
Choices:
- Reviewed the memo before lunch.
- The memo for the audit.
- The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
Answer: Reviewed the memo before lunch.
Rationale: The action is present, but the doer is missing.

Question: Which fragment is missing a verb?
Choices:
- Sent the notice after the briefing.
- The memo for the audit.
- Because the office closed early.
Answer: The memo for the audit.
Rationale: It has a noun phrase, but no verb tells what happens.

Question: Which sentence is complete?
Choices:
- The report for the audit.
- Because the report was late.
- The report for the audit was ready.
Answer: The report for the audit was ready.
Rationale: It has a subject, a verb, and a complete thought.

## Dependent-Clause Fragments

### Overview

A dependent clause has a subject and a verb, but it cannot stand alone. It usually starts with a dependent marker such as because, when, although, if, since, while, before, after, or until.

### Core Principle

If a clause depends on another clause to finish the thought, it is incomplete by itself.

### Visualization

| Marker | Example | Why it is incomplete |
| --- | --- | --- |
| because | `Because the office closed early` | no complete main clause |
| when | `When the supervisor arrived` | no complete main clause |
| although | `Although the report was ready` | no complete main clause |
| if | `If the printer jams` | no complete main clause |

### Common Mistakes

Do not assume that a clause is complete just because it has a subject and a verb. If the opening word makes it dependent, the thought is still unfinished.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Why is `When the supervisor arrived.` a fragment?

Reasoning:

- It has a subject and a verb.
- It starts with `when`, which makes it dependent.
- It needs a main clause to complete the thought.

Answer: **It is a dependent-clause fragment.**

### Key Insight

A dependent clause is grammatically built, but it still cannot stand alone.

### Quick Check

Question: Which group is a dependent-clause fragment?
Choices:
- When the supervisor arrived.
- The supervisor arrived.
- The supervisor arrived, and the meeting began.
Answer: When the supervisor arrived.
Rationale: It starts with a dependent marker and needs a main clause.

Question: Which revision turns the fragment into a complete sentence?
Choices:
- When the supervisor arrived, the meeting began.
- When the supervisor arrived.
- The meeting began.
Answer: When the supervisor arrived, the meeting began.
Rationale: The dependent clause is attached to a complete main clause.

Question: Which word makes the clause dependent?
Choices:
- supervisor
- arrived
- when
Answer: when
Rationale: The dependent marker prevents the clause from standing alone.

## Phrase Fragments

### Overview

A phrase fragment is a group of words that has no subject-verb pair. It may be a prepositional phrase, an infinitive phrase, or a participial phrase, but it still cannot stand alone as a sentence.

### Core Principle

A phrase can add detail, but it cannot replace a sentence.

### Visualization

| Phrase type | Example | Why it is incomplete |
| --- | --- | --- |
| prepositional phrase | `Before the meeting in the lobby` | no subject or verb |
| infinitive phrase | `To finish the report by noon` | no subject or verb |
| participial phrase | `After lunch near the window` | no subject or verb |
| prepositional phrase | `With the folder on the desk` | no subject or verb |

### Common Mistakes

Do not let a long phrase fool you. Length does not make a phrase into a sentence.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Why is `To finish the report by noon.` a fragment?

Reasoning:

- It is an infinitive phrase.
- It has no subject and no finite verb.
- It cannot stand alone as a complete thought.

Answer: **It is a phrase fragment.**

### Key Insight

If there is no subject-verb core, there is no sentence.

### Quick Check

Question: Which group is only a phrase?
Choices:
- Before the meeting in the lobby.
- The clerk waited before the meeting in the lobby.
- Because the meeting was delayed.
Answer: Before the meeting in the lobby.
Rationale: It is a phrase, not a complete clause.

Question: Which revision repairs the phrase fragment?
Choices:
- The clerk waited before the meeting in the lobby.
- Before the meeting in the lobby.
- Because the meeting was delayed.
Answer: The clerk waited before the meeting in the lobby.
Rationale: It adds a subject and a verb to make one complete clause.

Question: Which option is not a clause?
Choices:
- Because the office closed early
- The office closed early.
- The office closed early, and the staff went home.
Answer: Because the office closed early
Rationale: It is a dependent clause, not a phrase, but it still cannot stand alone.

## Repairing Fragments

### Overview

Fragments are repaired by adding whatever is missing. A subjectless fragment needs a subject. A verbless fragment needs a verb. A dependent-clause fragment needs a main clause. A phrase fragment needs a complete clause.

### Core Principle

Fix the smallest missing piece that creates one complete independent clause.

### Visualization

| Fragment type | Repair pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| subjectless | add a subject | `Reviewed the memo before lunch.` -> `The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.` |
| verbless | add a verb | `The memo for the audit.` -> `The memo for the audit was ready.` |
| dependent clause | attach a main clause | `Because the office closed early.` -> `Because the office closed early, the staff went home.` |
| phrase | add a subject and verb | `Before the meeting in the lobby.` -> `The clerk waited before the meeting in the lobby.` |

### Common Mistakes

Do not overcorrect. The best repair is usually the one that makes the sentence complete while keeping the original meaning close to the fragment.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: How do you fix `Before the meeting in the lobby.`?

Reasoning:

- The group is only a phrase.
- It needs a subject and a verb.
- A complete clause must be added.

Answer: **The clerk waited before the meeting in the lobby.**

### Key Insight

The right repair depends on what is missing, not on how long the fragment looks.

### Quick Check

Question: Which revision best turns the fragment into a complete sentence?
Choices:
- The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
- Reviewed the memo before lunch.
- Before the meeting in the lobby.
Answer: The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
Rationale: It adds the missing subject and forms one complete clause.

Question: Which revision correctly repairs the dependent-clause fragment?
Choices:
- Because the office closed early, the staff went home.
- Because the office closed early.
- The office closed early.
Answer: Because the office closed early, the staff went home.
Rationale: The dependent clause now connects to a complete main clause.

Question: Which revision correctly repairs the phrase fragment?
Choices:
- The clerk waited before the meeting in the lobby.
- Before the meeting in the lobby.
- Because the meeting was delayed.
Answer: The clerk waited before the meeting in the lobby.
Rationale: The phrase is expanded into a full sentence with a subject and verb.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

`Reviewed the memo before lunch.` is a fragment because it has a verb but no subject.

### Example 2

`The memo for the audit.` is a fragment because it has a noun phrase but no verb.

### Example 3

`Because the office closed early.` is a fragment because it is a dependent clause that does not finish the thought.

### Example 4

`The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.` is a complete sentence because it has one independent clause.

## Key Takeaways

- A fragment is incomplete and cannot stand alone.
- A fragment may be missing a subject, a verb, or a complete thought.
- Dependent clauses are not complete sentences by themselves.
- Phrases are not clauses, so they cannot stand alone.
- The best repair depends on what the fragment is missing.

## Summary

Sentence fragments are a high-value grammar skill in the Civil Service Examination because they test whether you can tell a complete idea from an incomplete one. If you can quickly identify what is missing, you can recognize the fragment, choose the correct repair, and avoid traps built from phrases, dependent clauses, and long opening modifiers.
