# Basic Components of a Sentence

## Explanations

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Every English sentence is built from the same two required components: a subject (what/who the sentence is about) and a predicate (what the subject does/is). This binary structure is universal — no matter how long or complex a sentence becomes, it always reduces to "something does/is something." Identifying these core components lets you parse any sentence by finding its structural skeleton first, then analyzing the modifiers and complements attached to it.

### Introduction

**Sentence structure** is the arrangement of words, phrases, and clauses that form a grammatically complete thought. Understanding how sentences are built — what parts they require, how those parts relate, and what makes a sentence complete — is foundational to every grammar skill tested on the Philippine Civil Service Examination.

Why this matters:
- Government communication demands clarity; unclear sentence structure causes misinterpretation of policies, memoranda, and directives
- The CSE Verbal Ability section tests your ability to identify subjects, predicates, objects, and complements — often indirectly through error-identification and sentence-completion items
- Sentence structure knowledge underpins every other grammar topic: subject-verb agreement, parallelism, pronoun reference, and modifier placement all depend on recognizing sentence components

### Why Sentence Structure Is Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Exam tests sentence structure because:
- Administrative writing requires complete, well-formed sentences — fragments and run-ons signal incompetence in official documents
- Many CSE items require you to identify what is missing or misplaced in a sentence, which demands component-level analysis
- Understanding sentence patterns helps you eliminate wrong answers faster in grammar, correct usage, and paragraph organization questions
- It is the grammar foundation: every other rule (agreement, tense, voice) operates on sentence components

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. Confusing the subject with a noun in a prepositional phrase ("The box *of chocolates* is" — subject is "box," not "chocolates")
2. Failing to identify the complete predicate vs. the simple predicate (verb alone)
3. Mistaking a direct object for a subject complement
4. Treating dependent clauses as complete sentences (sentence fragments)
5. Overlooking implied subjects in imperative sentences
6. Confusing linking verbs with action verbs, leading to misidentification of complements vs. objects
7. Not recognizing verb phrases (helping verb + main verb) as a single predicate unit
8. Joining two independent clauses without proper punctuation (run-on sentences / comma splices)

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Identify the basic components of a sentence (subject, predicate, object, complement)
- Distinguish between simple and complete subjects
- Distinguish between simple and complete predicates
- Identify direct objects, indirect objects, and complements
- Differentiate between phrases and clauses
- Recognize independent and dependent clauses
- Determine whether a group of words is a complete sentence, a fragment, or a run-on
- Analyze basic sentence patterns (S-V, S-V-O, S-LV-C)
- Apply elimination strategies to CSE-style sentence structure questions
- Solve sentence component identification questions accurately under time pressure

## 4.1 What Is a Sentence?

A **sentence** is a group of words that expresses a **complete thought** and contains at least a **subject** and a **predicate**. It begins with a capital letter and ends with terminal punctuation (period, question mark, or exclamation point).

### Requirements for a Complete Sentence

A grammatically complete sentence must have:
1. **A subject** — who or what the sentence is about
2. **A predicate** — what the subject does or is
3. **A complete thought** — the sentence makes sense on its own; it does not leave the reader waiting for more information

### Why This Matters

If any of these three elements is missing, the word group is a **fragment**, not a sentence. The CSE frequently tests your ability to distinguish complete sentences from fragments.

### Examples

**Complete sentences:**
- The employee submitted the report. *(subject: The employee; predicate: submitted the report; complete thought: yes)*
- Rain fell heavily throughout the night. *(subject: Rain; predicate: fell heavily throughout the night)*

**Fragments (NOT sentences):**
- Because the meeting was cancelled. *(dependent clause — no complete thought)*
- The new policy regarding overtime. *(no predicate — what about the policy?)*
- Running quickly through the hallway. *(no subject — who is running?)*

### CSE-Style Application

In the exam, you may see items like:

> Which of the following is a complete sentence?
> A. Although the director approved the budget.
> B. The memorandum on the table.
> C. The staff completed the inventory before noon.
> D. Walking to the office every morning.

**Answer: C** — It has a subject (The staff), a predicate (completed the inventory before noon), and expresses a complete thought.

## 4.2 Subject of a Sentence

The **subject** tells **who** or **what** the sentence is about. It is the noun or pronoun (and its modifiers) that performs the action or is described by the predicate.

### Simple Subject vs. Complete Subject

| Term | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Simple subject** | The core noun or pronoun alone | *student* |
| **Complete subject** | The simple subject plus all its modifiers | *The diligent student from Quezon City* |

### How to Find the Subject

1. Find the verb (predicate) first
2. Ask: **Who** or **what** performs this action (or is described)?
3. The answer is the subject

### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- **Dogs** bark at strangers. *(Simple subject: Dogs)*
- **She** passed the exam. *(Simple subject: She)*

**Medium:**
- **The newly hired employees in the accounting department** attended the orientation. *(Simple subject: employees; Complete subject: The newly hired employees in the accounting department)*
- **Several boxes of documents** were delivered this morning. *(Simple subject: boxes — NOT documents; "of documents" is a prepositional phrase modifying "boxes")*

**Hard:**
- **What the committee decided** remains confidential. *(Subject is the entire noun clause "What the committee decided")*
- **Neither the manager nor the supervisors** were informed of the change. *(Compound subject joined by "neither…nor")*

### Implied (You-Understood) Subject

In imperative sentences (commands/requests), the subject is **you**, even though it does not appear:
- (You) Submit the report by Friday.
- (You) Please close the door.

### Common Trap: Prepositional Phrase Confusion

The subject is NEVER inside a prepositional phrase. Examinees often mistake the object of a preposition for the subject:

- "The cost **of the materials** has increased." → Subject: **cost** (not materials)
- "One **of the applicants** was selected." → Subject: **One** (not applicants)
- "The results **of the investigation** were released." → Subject: **results** (not investigation)

## 4.3 Predicate of a Sentence

The **predicate** tells what the subject **does**, **is**, or **has**. It always contains a verb and may include objects, complements, and modifiers.

### Simple Predicate vs. Complete Predicate

| Term | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Simple predicate** | The verb or verb phrase alone | *explained* |
| **Complete predicate** | The verb plus all its complements, objects, and modifiers | *explained the new policy to the staff clearly* |

### How to Find the Predicate

1. Identify the subject
2. Everything else in the sentence (the verb and all words that complete or modify the verb's meaning) is the complete predicate

### Examples

**Easy:**
- The teacher **explained the lesson**. *(Simple predicate: explained; Complete predicate: explained the lesson)*
- Birds **fly**. *(Simple predicate: fly; Complete predicate: fly)*

**Medium:**
- The committee **has been reviewing the applications since Monday**. *(Simple predicate: has been reviewing; Complete predicate: has been reviewing the applications since Monday)*
- The report **was submitted late by the intern**. *(Simple predicate: was submitted; Complete predicate: was submitted late by the intern)*

**Hard:**
- The director **not only approved the budget but also allocated additional funds for training**. *(Compound predicate with correlative conjunction)*
- The policy **that was implemented last year has significantly reduced employee turnover**. *(Simple predicate of the main clause: has reduced)*

## 4.4 Verbs and Verb Phrases

The verb is the **heart of the predicate**. It expresses action, occurrence, or a state of being.

### Types of Verbs

#### Action Verbs
Express physical or mental action:
- Physical: run, write, submit, deliver, build
- Mental: think, believe, consider, understand, remember

**Examples:**
- The clerk **filed** the documents. *(physical action)*
- The manager **considered** the proposal carefully. *(mental action)*

#### Linking Verbs
Connect the subject to a word that describes or renames it. They do NOT express action.

Common linking verbs: *is, am, are, was, were, be, been, being, seem, appear, become, remain, feel, look, sound, taste, smell, grow, turn, stay*

**Examples:**
- The soup **smells** delicious. *(links "soup" to "delicious")*
- She **became** the department head. *(links "She" to "department head")*
- The results **are** encouraging. *(links "results" to "encouraging")*

#### Helping (Auxiliary) Verbs
Combine with a main verb to form a **verb phrase**. They add meaning related to tense, mood, or voice.

Common helping verbs: *is, am, are, was, were, be, been, being, has, have, had, do, does, did, will, would, shall, should, can, could, may, might, must*

**Examples:**
- The report **has been submitted**. *(helping verbs: has, been; main verb: submitted)*
- The employees **will be attending** the seminar. *(helping verbs: will, be; main verb: attending)*
- She **could have finished** earlier. *(helping verbs: could, have; main verb: finished)*

### Verb Phrases

A **verb phrase** = helping verb(s) + main verb. The entire phrase functions as the simple predicate.

| Verb Phrase | Helping Verb(s) | Main Verb |
|-------------|----------------|-----------|
| is working | is | working |
| has been approved | has, been | approved |
| will have completed | will, have | completed |
| should be reviewed | should, be | reviewed |
| might have been delayed | might, have, been | delayed |

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** English verb phrases stack helpers in a fixed order — modal → perfect (have) → progressive (be) → passive (be) → main verb — because each helper governs the form of the word that follows it. Modals require a base form ("can go"), "have" requires a past participle ("has gone"), and "be" requires either a present participle for progressive ("is going") or a past participle for passive ("is taken"). This chain of form-governance is why you can predict the correct verb phrase structure by working left to right through the helpers.

## 4.5 Direct Objects

A **direct object** receives the action of the verb. It answers the question **"What?"** or **"Whom?"** after an action verb.

### How to Find the Direct Object

1. Find the action verb
2. Ask: The subject [verb] **what** or **whom**?
3. The answer is the direct object

### Important Rule

Only **action verbs** (transitive verbs) take direct objects. Linking verbs NEVER have direct objects — they have complements instead.

### Examples

**Easy:**
- Maria wrote **a letter**. *(wrote what? → a letter)*
- The supervisor called **the employee**. *(called whom? → the employee)*

**Medium:**
- The committee approved **the revised budget for the fiscal year**. *(approved what? → the revised budget for the fiscal year)*
- The director has not yet signed **the memorandum**. *(signed what? → the memorandum)*

**Hard:**
- The board announced **that the policy would take effect immediately**. *(announced what? → the noun clause "that the policy would take effect immediately")*
- She gave the report **a thorough review**. *(Wait — this has both an indirect object and a direct object. See section 4.6.)*

### Verbs That Do NOT Take Direct Objects (Intransitive Verbs)

Some action verbs are **intransitive** — they do not transfer action to an object:
- The baby **slept** peacefully. *(slept what? — no answer; "peacefully" is an adverb, not an object)*
- The audience **laughed**. *(laughed what? — no answer)*
- The temperature **rose** quickly. *(rose what? — no answer)*

## 4.6 Indirect Objects

An **indirect object** tells **to whom** or **for whom** the action is done. It appears between the verb and the direct object.

### How to Find the Indirect Object

1. Find the action verb and the direct object
2. Ask: The subject [verb] [direct object] **to/for whom**?
3. If the answer appears between the verb and the direct object (without "to" or "for"), it is an indirect object

### Structure

Subject + Verb + **Indirect Object** + Direct Object

### Examples

**Easy:**
- John gave **his friend** a gift. *(gave a gift to whom? → his friend)*
- The teacher showed **the class** a video. *(showed a video to whom? → the class)*

**Medium:**
- The manager sent **all department heads** a memorandum regarding the new policy. *(sent a memorandum to whom? → all department heads)*
- The government offered **displaced workers** financial assistance. *(offered assistance to whom? → displaced workers)*

**Hard:**
- The committee awarded **the top-performing employee** a certificate of recognition and a cash incentive. *(awarded what to whom? → indirect object: the top-performing employee; direct objects: a certificate… and a cash incentive)*

### Indirect Object vs. Prepositional Phrase

These two sentences mean the same thing, but only the first has an indirect object:
- She gave **him** the book. *(indirect object: him)*
- She gave the book **to him**. *(prepositional phrase: to him — NOT an indirect object)*

The indirect object form places the recipient BEFORE the direct object WITHOUT a preposition.

## 4.7 Complements

A **complement** completes the meaning of a subject or object. Complements follow **linking verbs** (subject complements) or rename/describe an object (object complements).

### Types of Complements

#### 1. Subject Complement (Predicate Nominative)

A noun or pronoun that **renames** the subject. It follows a linking verb.

- She is **a doctor**. *(doctor = She)*
- The winner was **he**. *(he = winner)*
- Mr. Santos became **the department manager**. *(department manager = Mr. Santos)*

#### 2. Subject Complement (Predicate Adjective)

An adjective that **describes** the subject. It follows a linking verb.

- The soup smells **delicious**. *(delicious describes soup)*
- The employees seemed **exhausted**. *(exhausted describes employees)*
- The instructions are **unclear**. *(unclear describes instructions)*

#### 3. Object Complement

A noun or adjective that **renames or describes the direct object**. It follows the direct object.

- The board elected her **chairperson**. *(chairperson renames her)*
- The news made everyone **happy**. *(happy describes everyone)*
- They painted the office **blue**. *(blue describes office)*

### How to Distinguish: Direct Object vs. Subject Complement

| After an action verb → | Direct Object |
|------------------------|---------------|
| After a linking verb → | Subject Complement |

**Test:** Replace the verb with an equals sign (=). If the sentence still makes sense, it's a linking verb + complement:
- She is a doctor. → She = a doctor ✓ (complement)
- She called a doctor. → She = a doctor ✗ (direct object)

## 4.8 Phrases and Clauses

Understanding phrases and clauses is critical for analyzing sentence structure and identifying fragments.

### Phrases

A **phrase** is a group of related words that does NOT contain both a subject and a verb working together. It functions as a single part of speech.

#### Types of Phrases

| Type | Structure | Example | Function |
|------|-----------|---------|----------|
| **Prepositional** | preposition + object | *in the office* | adjective or adverb |
| **Verb** | helping verb(s) + main verb | *has been working* | predicate |
| **Noun** | noun + modifiers | *the new employee* | subject, object, complement |
| **Participial** | participle + modifiers | *running quickly* | adjective |
| **Gerund** | gerund + modifiers | *filing reports* | noun |
| **Infinitive** | to + verb + modifiers | *to finish the task* | noun, adjective, adverb |
| **Appositive** | noun that renames another | *Maria, the supervisor,* | noun |

**Key point:** A phrase CANNOT stand alone as a sentence because it lacks a subject-verb pair.

### Clauses

A **clause** is a group of words that DOES contain a subject and a verb.

#### Independent Clause

An independent clause expresses a **complete thought** and can stand alone as a sentence.

- *The manager approved the request.* ✓ (complete sentence)
- *She will attend the meeting.* ✓ (complete sentence)

#### Dependent (Subordinate) Clause

A dependent clause has a subject and verb but does NOT express a complete thought. It begins with a **subordinating conjunction** or a **relative pronoun** and cannot stand alone.

**Subordinating conjunctions:** because, although, when, while, if, since, after, before, unless, until, even though, so that, wherever, whenever

**Relative pronouns:** who, whom, whose, which, that

**Examples:**
- *because the meeting was cancelled* (Why? What happened? — incomplete)
- *who submitted the report late* (What about this person? — incomplete)
- *although the budget was approved* (What's the contrast? — incomplete)

### Phrase vs. Clause — Quick Comparison

| Feature | Phrase | Clause |
|---------|--------|--------|
| Has a subject? | No | Yes |
| Has a verb? | May have a verbal (participle, gerund, infinitive) but not a finite verb | Yes (finite verb) |
| Can be a sentence? | Never | Only if independent |

## 4.9 Complete vs. Incomplete Sentences

The CSE frequently tests whether you can distinguish a grammatically complete sentence from a fragment or run-on.

### Sentence Fragments

A **fragment** is a group of words punctuated as a sentence but missing one or more requirements (subject, predicate, or complete thought).

#### Common Fragment Types

| Fragment Type | Example | Problem | Correction |
|--------------|---------|---------|-----------|
| Missing subject | *Submitted the report on time.* | Who submitted? | *The clerk submitted the report on time.* |
| Missing predicate | *The new policy regarding overtime.* | What about it? | *The new policy regarding overtime was announced today.* |
| Dependent clause alone | *Because the office was closed.* | Incomplete thought | *Because the office was closed, the meeting was postponed.* |
| Participial phrase alone | *Running late for the meeting.* | No subject or finite verb | *Running late for the meeting, she took a taxi.* |
| Appositive alone | *A dedicated public servant.* | No predicate | *He is a dedicated public servant.* |

### Run-On Sentences

A **run-on** joins two or more independent clauses without proper punctuation or conjunction.

#### Types of Run-Ons

**Fused sentence** (no punctuation between clauses):
- ✗ *The report is due tomorrow the staff is working overtime.*

**Comma splice** (only a comma between clauses):
- ✗ *The report is due tomorrow, the staff is working overtime.*

#### How to Fix Run-Ons

1. **Period:** The report is due tomorrow. The staff is working overtime.
2. **Semicolon:** The report is due tomorrow; the staff is working overtime.
3. **Comma + coordinating conjunction:** The report is due tomorrow, so the staff is working overtime.
4. **Subordination:** Because the report is due tomorrow, the staff is working overtime.

### Complete Sentence Checklist

Ask yourself:
- ✅ Does it have a subject? (Who or what?)
- ✅ Does it have a predicate with a finite verb? (What does/is the subject?)
- ✅ Does it express a complete thought? (Does it make sense alone?)

If all three are YES → complete sentence.

## 4.10 Sentence Patterns

English sentences follow predictable patterns. Recognizing these patterns helps you identify components quickly.

### Pattern 1: Subject + Verb (S-V)

The simplest pattern. An intransitive verb needs no object or complement.

- Birds **fly**.
- The baby **cried**.
- The meeting **ended** abruptly.

### Pattern 2: Subject + Verb + Direct Object (S-V-DO)

The verb transfers action to a receiver.

- The secretary **typed** *the letter*.
- The committee **reviewed** *all applications*.
- She **completed** *the training program*.

### Pattern 3: Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object (S-V-IO-DO)

The verb has both a recipient and a receiver of action.

- The director **gave** *the staff* *a bonus*.
- She **sent** *her supervisor* *an email*.
- The government **offered** *the victims* *financial aid*.

### Pattern 4: Subject + Linking Verb + Subject Complement (S-LV-SC)

The linking verb connects the subject to a description or renaming.

- The policy **is** *effective*.
- She **became** *the team leader*.
- The results **seem** *promising*.

### Pattern 5: Subject + Verb + Direct Object + Object Complement (S-V-DO-OC)

The object complement renames or describes the direct object.

- They **elected** *her* *president*.
- The committee **found** *the proposal* *acceptable*.
- The paint **made** *the room* *brighter*.

### Pattern Recognition Strategy for the CSE

1. Find the verb first
2. Determine if it's action or linking
3. If action → look for objects (what/whom)
4. If linking → look for complement (= subject)
5. Check for indirect objects (to/for whom, before the DO)

## 4.11 Contextual Sentence Analysis

In the CSE, you won't just label parts in isolation — you'll analyze sentences in context. Here's how to approach complex sentences systematically.

### Step-by-Step Analysis Method

**Example:** "The experienced engineer who designed the bridge has received an award from the mayor."

1. **Find the main verb:** has received
2. **Find the subject:** Ask "Who has received?" → The experienced engineer (the clause "who designed the bridge" is a dependent clause modifying "engineer")
3. **Find the direct object:** Ask "has received what?" → an award
4. **Identify modifiers:** "from the mayor" is a prepositional phrase modifying "award" or "received"; "who designed the bridge" is a relative clause modifying "engineer"

### Analyzing Compound and Complex Sentences

**Compound sentence** (two independent clauses):
- *The director approved the budget, and the project began immediately.*
  - Clause 1: The director approved the budget (S-V-DO)
  - Clause 2: the project began immediately (S-V)
  - Joined by: coordinating conjunction "and"

**Complex sentence** (independent + dependent clause):
- *Although the deadline was extended, most employees submitted their reports early.*
  - Dependent clause: Although the deadline was extended
  - Independent clause: most employees submitted their reports early (S-V-DO)

### Identifying Sentence Functions in Context

When the CSE asks "What is the function of X in the sentence?", use this framework:

| If X answers... | It functions as... |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Who/what before the verb | Subject |
| What action is performed | Verb/Predicate |
| Verb + what/whom? | Direct object |
| Verb + DO + to/for whom? | Indirect object |
| = subject (after linking verb) | Subject complement |
| Describes the DO | Object complement |
| When/where/how/why | Adverbial modifier |
| Which one/what kind | Adjectival modifier |

## 4.12 Elimination Strategies

When facing CSE sentence structure questions, use these strategies to narrow down answers quickly.

### Strategy 1: Check for Completeness

If the question asks which option is a complete sentence:
- Eliminate any choice that starts with a subordinating conjunction and has no independent clause
- Eliminate any choice that lacks a finite verb (watch for -ing words without helpers)
- Eliminate any choice that has no subject

### Strategy 2: Identify the Core

Strip away modifiers to find the skeleton:
- "The recently hired employees in the finance department" → employees (subject)
- Cross out prepositional phrases, participial phrases, and appositives to reveal the core S-V-O structure

### Strategy 3: Verb Type Determines What Follows

- Action verb → expect an object (or nothing if intransitive)
- Linking verb → expect a complement (noun or adjective = subject)
- If a choice puts an object after a linking verb, eliminate it
- If a choice puts a complement after an action verb, eliminate it

### Strategy 4: Subject-Verb Agreement as a Clue

If the question involves choosing the correct sentence:
- Identify the true subject (ignore prepositional phrases)
- Check if the verb agrees in number
- Eliminate choices with agreement errors

### Strategy 5: Spot Structural Red Flags

Immediately eliminate choices that contain:
- Two independent clauses joined only by a comma (comma splice)
- A dependent clause standing alone (fragment)
- A missing subject or verb
- Double subjects ("The manager he approved…")

## 4.13 Common Sentence Structure Errors

### 1. Sentence Fragments

**Error:** Punctuating an incomplete thought as a sentence.
- ✗ *Because the office was renovated.*
- ✓ *Because the office was renovated, the staff worked from home.*

### 2. Run-On Sentences

**Error:** Joining independent clauses without proper punctuation.
- ✗ *The deadline passed the report was not submitted.*
- ✓ *The deadline passed, but the report was not submitted.*

### 3. Comma Splices

**Error:** Using only a comma between independent clauses.
- ✗ *The policy is effective, it reduced complaints by 40%.*
- ✓ *The policy is effective; it reduced complaints by 40%.*

### 4. Missing Subjects

**Error:** Omitting the subject (outside of imperative sentences).
- ✗ *Went to the office early this morning.*
- ✓ *She went to the office early this morning.*

### 5. Unclear or Missing Predicates

**Error:** A noun phrase with no verb.
- ✗ *The new regulation about employee benefits.*
- ✓ *The new regulation about employee benefits takes effect next month.*

### 6. Misplaced Objects

**Error:** Placing the object where it creates ambiguity.
- ✗ *The manager told to submit the report the employee.* (garbled word order)
- ✓ *The manager told the employee to submit the report.*

### 7. Double Subjects

**Error:** Using a pronoun that repeats the subject unnecessarily.
- ✗ *The director he approved the budget.*
- ✓ *The director approved the budget.*

### 8. Incomplete Comparisons

**Error:** Starting a comparison without finishing it.
- ✗ *This policy is more effective.*  (more effective than what?)
- ✓ *This policy is more effective than the previous one.*

## Step-by-Step Rules for Sentence Analysis

### Rule 1: Find the Verb First
The verb is the engine of the sentence. Once you find it, everything else falls into place.

### Rule 2: Ask "Who/What?" Before the Verb
The answer is your subject.

### Rule 3: Ask "What/Whom?" After an Action Verb
The answer is your direct object.

### Rule 4: Check for Linking Verbs
If the verb can be replaced by "=" and the sentence still makes sense, it's a linking verb. What follows is a complement, not an object.

### Rule 5: Look for "To/For Whom" Between Verb and Object
If a noun appears between the verb and the direct object without a preposition, it's an indirect object.

### Rule 6: Strip Modifiers to Find the Core
Remove prepositional phrases, adjective clauses, and adverbs to reveal the basic S-V pattern.

### Rule 7: Check for Completeness
Every sentence needs: subject + finite verb + complete thought. If any is missing, it's a fragment.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Fix |
|---------|---------------|-----------|
| Calling a prepositional object the subject | The object of "of/in/for" is close to the verb | Cross out prepositional phrases first |
| Missing the helping verb | Students focus only on the main verb | Look for the full verb phrase |
| Confusing DO with SC | Both follow the verb | Check: is the verb action or linking? |
| Treating dependent clauses as sentences | They have subjects and verbs | Check: does it start with a subordinator? |
| Ignoring implied subjects | Imperatives look subjectless | Remember: commands have "you" understood |

## Exam Strategies

### Time Management
- Spend no more than 30–45 seconds per sentence structure item
- If you can't identify the answer in 30 seconds, eliminate two choices and guess between the remaining two

### Quick Identification Technique
1. **Scan for the verb** — this anchors your analysis
2. **Look left** — the subject is usually to the left of the verb
3. **Look right** — objects and complements are to the right
4. **Check boundaries** — commas, conjunctions, and subordinators mark clause boundaries

### Pattern Recognition Under Pressure
- If you see a linking verb (is, was, seems, became), expect a complement — not an object
- If you see a transitive verb (gave, sent, showed) with two nouns after it, think IO + DO
- If a sentence starts with "Although," "Because," "When," etc., the first clause is dependent — look for the independent clause

### Red Flags That Signal Wrong Answers
- A "sentence" that begins with "because" or "although" and has no second clause
- A word group with only an -ing verb and no helping verb (fragment)
- Two complete sentences joined by just a comma
- A sentence with no verb at all

## Real CSE-Like Examples

### Easy

**Question:** What is the simple subject in "The hardworking employees of the finance department submitted their reports on time"?
**Answer:** employees
**Explanation:** Strip away modifiers: "The hardworking" modifies employees; "of the finance department" is a prepositional phrase. The core noun performing the action is "employees."

### Medium

**Question:** Identify the direct object in "The committee has awarded the outstanding teacher a plaque of recognition."
**Answer:** a plaque of recognition
**Explanation:** The verb is "has awarded." Ask "awarded what?" → a plaque of recognition. "The outstanding teacher" is the indirect object (awarded to whom?).

### Hard

**Question:** Which sentence component does the underlined portion serve? "The fact *that the policy was revised* surprised everyone."
**Answer:** The underlined clause is a noun clause functioning as part of the subject (appositive to "fact").
**Explanation:** "That the policy was revised" is a noun clause in apposition to "The fact." Together, "The fact that the policy was revised" is the complete subject of "surprised."

## Mini Practice Set

**1.** What is the simple subject? "The results of the investigation were released yesterday."
**Answer:** results
**Explanation:** "Of the investigation" is a prepositional phrase — the subject is never inside a prepositional phrase.

**2.** Identify the complete predicate: "The newly appointed director addressed the staff during the assembly."
**Answer:** addressed the staff during the assembly
**Explanation:** Everything after the subject ("The newly appointed director") is the complete predicate.

**3.** What is the direct object? "The secretary prepared the agenda for tomorrow's meeting."
**Answer:** the agenda
**Explanation:** Prepared what? → the agenda. "For tomorrow's meeting" is a prepositional phrase modifying "agenda."

**4.** What is the indirect object? "The principal gave the teachers new guidelines."
**Answer:** the teachers
**Explanation:** Gave what? → new guidelines (DO). Gave to whom? → the teachers (IO, positioned between verb and DO).

**5.** Is this a complete sentence? "Although the training was mandatory for all new hires."
**Answer:** No — it is a fragment.
**Explanation:** It begins with "Although" (subordinating conjunction), making it a dependent clause with no independent clause to complete the thought.

**6.** Identify the linking verb: "The new policy seems unfair to many employees."
**Answer:** seems
**Explanation:** "Seems" connects the subject "policy" to the adjective "unfair." You can test: policy = unfair (makes sense).

**7.** What is the subject complement? "Maria became the head of the department."
**Answer:** the head of the department
**Explanation:** "Became" is a linking verb. "The head of the department" renames Maria (predicate nominative).

**8.** What type of error is this? "The report was submitted late, the supervisor was not pleased."
**Answer:** Comma splice (run-on sentence)
**Explanation:** Two independent clauses are joined by only a comma. Fix: add "and" after the comma, use a semicolon, or make two sentences.

**9.** Identify the verb phrase: "The documents should have been reviewed before the deadline."
**Answer:** should have been reviewed
**Explanation:** Helping verbs: should, have, been. Main verb: reviewed. Together they form the verb phrase.

**10.** What is the object complement? "The committee found the proposal unacceptable."
**Answer:** unacceptable
**Explanation:** "Unacceptable" describes the direct object "the proposal." It's an adjective that completes the meaning of the object.

**11.** Identify the dependent clause: "The employee who arrived late was given a warning."
**Answer:** who arrived late
**Explanation:** It begins with the relative pronoun "who," has a subject (who) and verb (arrived), but cannot stand alone.

**12.** What is the simple predicate? "The team has been working on the project since January."
**Answer:** has been working
**Explanation:** The verb phrase (helping verbs + main verb) is the simple predicate. "On the project since January" are modifiers.

**13.** Is this a sentence or fragment? "Running late for the scheduled meeting with the board."
**Answer:** Fragment
**Explanation:** "Running" is a participle (not a finite verb), and there is no subject. This is a participial phrase, not a sentence.

**14.** Identify the subject: "What the manager said during the meeting surprised everyone."
**Answer:** What the manager said during the meeting
**Explanation:** The entire noun clause serves as the subject. Ask: "What surprised everyone?" → What the manager said during the meeting.

**15.** What sentence pattern is this? "The soup tastes delicious."
**Answer:** S-LV-SC (Subject + Linking Verb + Subject Complement)
**Explanation:** "Tastes" is a linking verb here (soup = delicious). "Delicious" is a predicate adjective.

**16.** Identify the prepositional phrase: "The files on the desk belong to the supervisor."
**Answer:** "on the desk" and "to the supervisor"
**Explanation:** Both begin with prepositions (on, to) and end with their objects (desk, supervisor).

**17.** What is the direct object? "The board announced that all offices would close early on Friday."
**Answer:** that all offices would close early on Friday
**Explanation:** The entire noun clause is the direct object. Announced what? → that all offices would close early on Friday.

**18.** Fix this run-on: "The deadline is tomorrow we need to finish the report."
**Answer:** The deadline is tomorrow; we need to finish the report. (or use a period, or add ", so")
**Explanation:** Two independent clauses are fused without punctuation.

**19.** Identify the appositive: "Mr. Reyes, the department head, approved the request."
**Answer:** the department head
**Explanation:** It renames "Mr. Reyes" and is set off by commas. It's a noun phrase in apposition.

**20.** What is the implied subject? "Please submit your reports before 5 PM."
**Answer:** You (implied/understood)
**Explanation:** This is an imperative sentence. The subject "you" is understood but not stated.

## Quick Recap

| Component | Question to Ask | Example |
|-----------|----------------|---------|
| Subject | Who/what before the verb? | *The clerk* filed the report. |
| Predicate | What does/is the subject? | The clerk *filed the report*. |
| Direct Object | Verb + what/whom? | She wrote *a memo*. |
| Indirect Object | Verb + DO + to/for whom? (before DO) | He gave *her* the file. |
| Subject Complement | What = subject? (after linking verb) | She is *a manager*. |
| Object Complement | What describes/renames the DO? | They called him *competent*. |
| Phrase | Group of words, no subject-verb pair | *in the office* |
| Independent Clause | Subject + verb + complete thought | *She finished the task.* |
| Dependent Clause | Subject + verb + incomplete thought | *because she finished early* |
| Fragment | Missing subject, verb, or complete thought | *Although approved.* |
| Run-on | Two independent clauses improperly joined | *She left he stayed.* |

## Memory Aids

### SPOC — The Four Core Components
- **S**ubject — who/what
- **P**redicate — does/is what
- **O**bject — receives the action
- **C**omplement — completes the meaning

### "LV = Equals Sign" Rule
If you can replace the verb with **=** and the sentence still makes sense, it's a **linking verb** and what follows is a **complement**, not an object.

### "Cross Out the Prep" Trick
To find the true subject, **cross out all prepositional phrases**. The remaining noun before the verb is the subject.
- ~~of the documents~~ → not the subject
- ~~in the department~~ → not the subject
- ~~for the employees~~ → not the subject

### "Finite Verb" Check for Fragments
A sentence needs a **finite verb** (one that shows tense). These are NOT finite verbs:
- running (participle)
- to run (infinitive)
- having run (perfect participle)

If the only "verb" in the word group is one of these, it's a fragment.

### IO Before DO — "Give WHO WHAT"
Indirect objects always come BEFORE direct objects (without a preposition):
- Give [IO] [DO] → Give *him* *the report*
