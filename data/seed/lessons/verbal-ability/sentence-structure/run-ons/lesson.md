# Run-ons

## Explanations

### Introduction

A run-on sentence happens when two or more independent clauses are joined incorrectly. In CSE items, the problem is usually not that the sentence is too long. The real problem is that two complete thoughts were not separated or connected the right way.

### Why Run-ons Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination checks whether you can read sentence boundaries quickly and accurately. Run-ons are tested because they show whether you can:

- spot two complete clauses that are too closely joined
- tell a run-on from a fragment
- recognize comma splices and fused sentences
- choose the cleanest repair under time pressure
- use punctuation and conjunctions the way formal English expects

### Common Mistakes

Candidates often make these mistakes:

- thinking every long sentence is a run-on
- assuming a comma alone can join two independent clauses
- confusing a comma splice with a fused sentence
- using a semicolon when one clause is not independent
- adding a conjunction but forgetting the comma before it

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify a run-on sentence quickly
- distinguish comma splices from fused sentences
- choose the correct fix for a run-on
- recognize when two independent clauses need a period, semicolon, comma and conjunction, or subordinating conjunction
- read sentence-structure items with more confidence and less second-guessing

## What Makes a Run-on Sentence

### Overview

A run-on sentence contains two or more independent clauses that are joined incorrectly. The clauses may be jammed together with no punctuation at all, or they may be separated by only a comma. Either way, the boundary between the clauses is wrong.

### Core Principle

Find the independent clauses first.

If both parts could stand alone as complete sentences, check how they are joined. If the join is weak or missing, the sentence is a run-on.

### Visualization

| Type | Example | Why it works or fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| complete sentence | `The clerk filed the memo before lunch.` | one independent clause |
| run-on | `The clerk filed the memo the supervisor signed it.` | two independent clauses with no break |
| comma splice | `The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.` | two independent clauses joined by only a comma |
| fused sentence | `The clerk filed the memo the supervisor signed it.` | two independent clauses pushed together with no punctuation |

### Common Mistakes

Do not assume that a sentence is correct just because it has a subject and a verb. Also, do not treat length as the only clue. A short sentence can still be a run-on if it has two complete clauses joined badly.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Why is `The office opened early the lights stayed dim.` a run-on?

Reasoning:

- `The office opened early` is one independent clause.
- `the lights stayed dim` is another independent clause.
- There is no punctuation or conjunction between them.

Answer: **It is a run-on sentence.**

### Key Insight

A run-on is a sentence boundary problem. The first job is to find the clauses; the second job is to fix the boundary.

### Quick Check

Question: Which sentence is complete as written?
Choices:
- The office opened early.
- The office opened early the lights stayed dim.
- The office opened early, the lights stayed dim.
Answer: The office opened early.
Rationale: It has one independent clause and no boundary error.

Question: Which sentence is the run-on?
Choices:
- The printer jammed the staff waited.
- The printer jammed, the staff waited.
- The printer jammed, so the staff waited.
Answer: The printer jammed the staff waited.
Rationale: Two complete clauses are joined with no punctuation.

Question: What makes a run-on sentence incorrect?
Choices:
- It joins complete clauses the wrong way.
- It always has too many adjectives.
- It must be shorter than ten words.
Answer: It joins complete clauses the wrong way.
Rationale: The problem is the clause boundary, not the length.

## Comma Splices and Fused Sentences

### Overview

Comma splices and fused sentences are both run-on errors. A comma splice uses only a comma between two independent clauses. A fused sentence uses no punctuation at all. In CSE work, you must notice which kind of join went wrong so you can fix it correctly.

### Core Principle

Ask one simple question: was there enough punctuation or conjunction between the two complete clauses?

If the answer is no, the sentence is still a run-on.

### Visualization

| Error type | Example | What is wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| comma splice | `The meeting ended, the assistant sent the notice.` | comma only between two complete clauses |
| fused sentence | `The meeting ended the assistant sent the notice.` | no punctuation between two complete clauses |
| correct compound sentence | `The meeting ended, and the assistant sent the notice.` | comma plus coordinating conjunction |

### Common Mistakes

Do not think a comma splice is acceptable just because the clauses are short. Do not think a fused sentence is only a very long sentence. The real issue is that the two independent clauses were not joined with the right boundary.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Why is `The office opened early, the lights stayed dim.` a comma splice?

Reasoning:

- `The office opened early` can stand alone.
- `the lights stayed dim` can also stand alone.
- They are joined only by a comma.

Answer: **It is a comma splice.**

### Key Insight

Comma splice and fused sentence are two different ways a run-on can fail. One has too little punctuation; the other has none.

### Quick Check

Question: Which sentence is a comma splice?
Choices:
- The printer jammed, the staff waited.
- The printer jammed; the staff waited.
- The printer jammed, so the staff waited.
Answer: The printer jammed, the staff waited.
Rationale: The comma is the only thing between two independent clauses.

Question: Which sentence is a fused sentence?
Choices:
- The meeting ended, the assistant sent the notice.
- The meeting ended the assistant sent the notice.
- The meeting ended; the assistant sent the notice.
Answer: The meeting ended the assistant sent the notice.
Rationale: Two independent clauses are pushed together with no punctuation.

Question: Which statement is true?
Choices:
- A comma splice uses only a comma between independent clauses.
- A fused sentence uses a comma and a conjunction.
- A fragment always has two independent clauses.
Answer: A comma splice uses only a comma between independent clauses.
Rationale: That is the definition of a comma splice.

## Fixing Run-ons Quickly

### Overview

Run-ons can be fixed in four standard ways: use a period, use a semicolon, use a comma plus a coordinating conjunction, or make one clause dependent with a subordinating conjunction. The best choice depends on how the two ideas relate to each other.

### Core Principle

Choose the repair that matches the relationship between the clauses.

- Use a period when you want two separate sentences.
- Use a semicolon when the clauses are closely related and both are independent.
- Use a comma and a coordinating conjunction when the clauses belong in one compound sentence.
- Use a subordinating conjunction when one idea should depend on the other.

### Visualization

| Repair | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| period | independent clause. independent clause. | `The clerk filed the memo. The supervisor signed it.` |
| semicolon | independent clause; independent clause. | `The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.` |
| comma + conjunction | independent clause, FANBOYS independent clause. | `The clerk filed the memo, and the supervisor signed it.` |
| subordinating conjunction | subordinate clause, independent clause. | `After the clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.` |

### Common Mistakes

Do not add only a comma. Do not use a semicolon if one side is not independent. Do not force a conjunction into the sentence without checking whether the clauses still make sense together.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: How can `The printer jammed the staff waited.` be fixed if you want to show cause and effect?

Reasoning:

- The sentence has two independent clauses.
- A cause-and-effect relationship is clearer if one clause becomes dependent.
- `Because the printer jammed, the staff waited.` shows that relationship well.

Answer: **Because the printer jammed, the staff waited.**

### Key Insight

The correction must do more than stop the error. It should also keep the relationship between the ideas clear.

### Quick Check

Question: Which revision correctly fixes the run-on by splitting it into two sentences?
Choices:
- The clerk filed the memo. The supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo, and the supervisor signed it.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo. The supervisor signed it.
Rationale: A period cleanly separates the two independent clauses.

Question: Which revision correctly uses a semicolon?
Choices:
- The meeting ended; the assistant sent the notice.
- The meeting ended. The assistant sent the notice.
- The meeting ended, and the assistant sent the notice.
Answer: The meeting ended; the assistant sent the notice.
Rationale: Both clauses are independent and closely related.

Question: Which revision correctly uses a subordinating conjunction?
Choices:
- After the schedule changed, everyone stayed calm.
- The schedule changed; everyone stayed calm.
- The schedule changed, and everyone stayed calm.
Answer: After the schedule changed, everyone stayed calm.
Rationale: The first clause becomes dependent and the run-on disappears.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

Question: Which sentence is the run-on in this set?

`The officer reviewed the file the supervisor approved it.`

Reasoning:

- Both parts could stand alone as complete sentences.
- There is no punctuation or conjunction between them.
- The sentence is a fused run-on.

Answer: **The officer reviewed the file the supervisor approved it.**

### Example 2

Question: Which revision correctly fixes the comma splice?

`The lights flickered, the office lost power.`

Reasoning:

- Two independent clauses are joined by only a comma.
- A semicolon can repair the sentence.
- The best fix is: `The lights flickered; the office lost power.`

Answer: **The lights flickered; the office lost power.**

### Example 3

Question: Which revision best repairs the run-on while keeping the cause-and-effect link clear?

`The server failed the staff restarted it.`

Reasoning:

- The second idea follows because of the first.
- A subordinating conjunction makes the relationship clear.
- `Because the server failed, the staff restarted it.` keeps the meaning intact.

Answer: **Because the server failed, the staff restarted it.**

## Key Takeaways

- A run-on joins independent clauses incorrectly.
- A comma splice uses only a comma between two complete clauses.
- A fused sentence has no punctuation between two complete clauses.
- Correct fixes include a period, a semicolon, a comma plus a coordinating conjunction, or a subordinating conjunction.
- The best repair depends on the relationship between the ideas.

## Summary

Run-ons are sentence boundary errors, not just long sentences. To solve them, count the independent clauses first, then check how they are joined. If two complete thoughts are stuck together, fix the boundary with the punctuation or conjunction that matches the meaning.

When you see a sentence-structure item in the CSE, slow down just enough to ask two questions: are there two complete clauses, and are they joined correctly? That simple check will eliminate many traps quickly.

## Final Challenge

### Mixed Practice Set

Question 1: Which revision correctly repairs `The report was ready the manager approved it.`?
Choices:
- The report was ready. The manager approved it.
- The report was ready, the manager approved it.
- The report was ready, and the manager approved it.
Answer: The report was ready. The manager approved it.
Rationale: The period separates the two independent clauses cleanly.

Question 2: Which sentence needs no correction?
Choices:
- The office was busy, the clerk stayed calm.
- The office was busy the clerk stayed calm.
- The office was busy, but the clerk stayed calm.
Answer: The office was busy, but the clerk stayed calm.
Rationale: The comma plus coordinating conjunction correctly joins the clauses.

Question 3: Which revision best keeps the connection between the ideas in `The schedule changed the team adjusted their plans.`?
Choices:
- Because the schedule changed, the team adjusted their plans.
- The schedule changed. The team adjusted their plans.
- The schedule changed; the team adjusted their plans.
Answer: Because the schedule changed, the team adjusted their plans.
Rationale: The subordinating conjunction keeps the cause-and-effect link clear.
