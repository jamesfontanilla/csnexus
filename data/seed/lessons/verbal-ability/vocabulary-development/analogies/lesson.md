# Analogies

## Explanations

### Introduction

**Analogies** are verbal reasoning questions that test your ability to identify logical relationships between pairs of words. In an analogy, you are given one pair of words that share a specific relationship, and you must find another pair (or complete a pair) that shares the same type of relationship. Analogy questions do not simply test vocabulary — they test your ability to think relationally and recognize patterns.

Analogy mastery is critical for the Philippine Civil Service Examination because:
- The CSE Verbal Ability section consistently includes analogy-based items that measure higher-order thinking — not just word knowledge, but the ability to see how words connect logically
- Government work requires relational reasoning: understanding hierarchies, cause-effect chains, part-whole structures, and functional relationships in policies, organizational charts, and legal frameworks
- Analogies are among the highest-difficulty items on the exam, meaning they carry significant weight in separating passers from non-passers
- Strong analogy skills transfer directly to reading comprehension, logical reasoning, and paragraph organization — other CSE sections
- Civil servants must regularly draw parallels between precedents and current situations, making analogical thinking a daily professional skill

### Why Analogies Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Exam tests analogy knowledge because:
- Analogies measure **relational reasoning** — the ability to see how concepts connect, not just what they mean individually
- They efficiently test multiple cognitive skills in a single item: vocabulary breadth, logical classification, and pattern recognition
- Government professionals must identify structural similarities between situations (e.g., applying a policy precedent to a new case requires analogical thinking)
- Analogies reveal whether an examinee can distinguish between superficial similarity and deep structural relationships — a skill essential for legal interpretation and policy analysis
- They test vocabulary in context rather than in isolation, which better predicts real-world language competence

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Focusing on word meaning alone** — choosing an answer because the words are related to the same topic, rather than because the relationship type matches
2. **Ignoring the direction/order of the relationship** — "Puppy is to Dog" is not the same relationship as "Dog is to Puppy" (young-to-adult vs. adult-to-young)
3. **Selecting partial matches** — choosing a pair where only one word fits the pattern while the other does not
4. **Confusing relationship types** — mistaking a cause-effect relationship for a function relationship, or a synonym relationship for a degree relationship
5. **Relying on surface-level associations** — "Doctor" and "Hospital" are associated, but their relationship (worker-to-workplace) is different from "Teacher" and "Student" (role-to-role)
6. **Not testing the relationship as a sentence** — failing to articulate the precise relationship as a bridge sentence before evaluating choices
7. **Rushing through without elimination** — not systematically ruling out choices that fail the relationship test

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Define what analogies are and explain how they differ from simple vocabulary questions
- Identify the specific relationship type in a given word pair
- Distinguish between at least eight major analogy relationship types
- Construct bridge sentences to articulate precise relationships
- Apply systematic elimination strategies to narrow answer choices
- Recognize and avoid common analogy traps (reversed order, partial matches, surface associations)
- Solve CSE-style analogy questions accurately under time pressure
- Analyze complex analogies that combine multiple relationship layers

### 4.1 What Are Analogies?
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The principle behind this operation follows from the fundamental properties of arithmetic. Understanding the "why" — not just the "how" — lets you recognize when to apply this method in unfamiliar problem contexts on the CSE.


An **analogy** is a comparison between two pairs of words based on the logical relationship they share. The word comes from the Greek *analogia* — meaning "proportion" or "correspondence."

#### The Core Principle

An analogy states: **"A is to B as C is to D."**

This means the relationship between A and B is the same type of relationship as between C and D.

Example: **Book is to Reading as Fork is to Eating**
- A book is *used for* reading → A fork is *used for* eating
- The relationship type: **object to function**

#### Key Principles

1. **Analogies test relationships, not definitions.** Knowing what "book" means is not enough — you must identify *how* "book" relates to "reading."
2. **The relationship must be parallel in both pairs.** If the first pair shows "tool → purpose," the second pair must also show "tool → purpose" — not "tool → user" or "tool → material."
3. **Order matters.** "Part : Whole" is different from "Whole : Part." Always preserve the directional logic.
4. **Specificity wins.** If multiple choices seem to work, the one with the most precise parallel relationship is correct.

#### The Bridge Sentence Method

The most reliable strategy for solving analogies is to create a **bridge sentence** — a short sentence that captures the exact relationship between the first pair, then test whether the same sentence works for each answer choice.

Example: **Hammer : Nail**
- Bridge sentence: "A hammer is used to drive a ___."
- Test: "A screwdriver is used to drive a screw." ✓
- Test: "A saw is used to drive a wood." ✗

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- Pen : Write :: Knife : Cut (tool → action)
- Dog : Puppy :: Cat : Kitten (adult → young)
- Hot : Cold :: Light : Dark (antonym pairs)

**Medium:**
- Surgeon : Scalpel :: Painter : Brush (professional → tool)
- Drought : Famine :: Virus : Epidemic (cause → effect)
- Whisper : Shout :: Drizzle : Downpour (mild → intense)

**Hard:**
- Bibliophile : Books :: Philatelist : Stamps (enthusiast → object of interest)
- Preamble : Constitution :: Overture : Opera (introduction → whole work)
- Parsimonious : Generous :: Loquacious : Taciturn (antonym pairs using advanced vocabulary)


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "The formula always works the same way regardless of the problem context."

> **Why it fails:** CSE problems often present variations where the standard formula must be adapted. Blindly applying a memorized formula without checking the context leads to systematic errors.

> **Correct model:** Always read the problem to identify what type of relationship exists (direct, inverse, part-whole, etc.), then apply the appropriate formula. Verify your answer makes sense in the problem's context before selecting it.

### 4.2 Types of Analogies

Analogy questions on the CSE draw from a finite set of relationship types. Learning to recognize these types quickly is the single most effective strategy for improving your score.

| Relationship Type | Pattern | Example |
|-------------------|---------|---------|
| Synonym | A means the same as B | Happy : Joyful |
| Antonym | A means the opposite of B | Hot : Cold |
| Part to Whole | A is a component of B | Wheel : Car |
| Whole to Part | A contains B | Orchestra : Violin |
| Function/Purpose | A is used to B | Knife : Cut |
| Cause and Effect | A leads to B | Study : Knowledge |
| Degree/Intensity | A is a weaker/stronger form of B | Warm : Scorching |
| Category/Classification | A is a type of B | Rose : Flower |
| Tool and User | A is used by B | Stethoscope : Doctor |
| Worker and Workplace | A works in B | Teacher : School |
| Object and Material | A is made of B | Paper : Wood |
| Symbol and Concept | A represents B | Dove : Peace |


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "If my computed answer is close to one of the choices, it must be right."

> **Why it fails:** The CSE deliberately includes distractors that result from common errors — using the wrong operation, misidentifying the proportion type, or reversing the ratio. A "close" answer could be the result of a systematic mistake that the test writers anticipated.

> **Correct model:** Verify your setup before computing. Check that you've identified the correct proportion type, set up the equation properly, and solved accurately. A wrong setup with correct arithmetic still produces a wrong answer — and the CSE will include that wrong answer among the choices.

### 4.3 Synonym Analogies
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** When you follow this procedure, you're exploiting a mathematical invariant — something that stays constant regardless of how you manipulate the numbers. Identifying that invariant is the key to solving problems efficiently rather than memorizing steps.


**Pattern:** Word A has the same or nearly the same meaning as Word B.

The relationship is one of **equivalence in meaning**. Both words in each pair must be interchangeable in most contexts.

#### How to Identify
- Ask: "Does A mean the same thing as B?"
- The bridge sentence is: "A means the same as B, just as C means the same as D."

#### Examples

| Pair 1 | Pair 2 | Explanation |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Happy : Joyful | Angry : Furious | Both pairs are synonyms |
| Begin : Commence | End : Conclude | Both pairs are formal/informal synonym matches |
| Brave : Courageous | Scared : Frightened | Both pairs share equivalent meaning |

#### CSE-Style Example

**Question:** Happy : Joyful :: Sad : _____
- A) Angry
- B) Melancholy
- C) Tired
- D) Lonely

**Answer:** B) Melancholy
**Explanation:** Happy and Joyful are synonyms. Sad and Melancholy are synonyms. The relationship is synonym-to-synonym.

#### Common Trap
Choosing a word that is *related* to the topic but not a synonym. "Sad : Lonely" — loneliness can cause sadness, but lonely does not *mean* sad.

---

### 4.4 Antonym Analogies

**Pattern:** Word A means the opposite of Word B.

#### How to Identify
- Ask: "Is A the opposite of B?"
- Bridge sentence: "A is the opposite of B, just as C is the opposite of D."

#### Examples

| Pair 1 | Pair 2 | Explanation |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Hot : Cold | Light : Dark | Direct opposites |
| Generous : Stingy | Brave : Cowardly | Character trait opposites |
| Expand : Contract | Ascend : Descend | Action opposites |

#### CSE-Style Example

**Question:** Expand : Contract :: Ascend : _____
- A) Climb
- B) Descend
- C) Elevate
- D) Rise

**Answer:** B) Descend
**Explanation:** Expand is the opposite of Contract. Ascend (go up) is the opposite of Descend (go down).

#### Common Trap
Choosing a synonym instead of an antonym. "Ascend : Climb" — these are synonyms, not opposites. Read the relationship in the first pair carefully.

### 4.5 Part-to-Whole Relationships
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** This shortcut works because it's a special case of the more general rule. By understanding the underlying principle, you can verify your answer logically even if you forget the exact formula under exam pressure.


**Pattern:** Word A is a component, piece, or member of Word B.

#### How to Identify
- Ask: "Is A a part of B?" or "Does B contain A?"
- Bridge sentence: "A is a part of B, just as C is a part of D."

#### Subtypes
- **Component → Object:** Wheel : Car, Key : Keyboard
- **Member → Group:** Player : Team, Soldier : Army
- **Section → Document:** Chapter : Book, Verse : Poem

#### Examples

| Pair 1 | Pair 2 | Explanation |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Wheel : Car | Page : Book | Physical component of a larger object |
| Player : Team | Soldier : Army | Individual member of a group |
| Petal : Flower | Brick : Wall | Small unit composing a larger structure |

#### CSE-Style Example

**Question:** Chapter : Book :: Room : _____
- A) Door
- B) Building
- C) Furniture
- D) Window

**Answer:** B) Building
**Explanation:** A chapter is a section of a book. A room is a section of a building. The relationship is part-to-whole.

#### Common Trap
Reversing the direction. "Book : Chapter" is whole-to-part, not part-to-whole. Always check which word comes first.

---

### 4.6 Function Analogies

**Pattern:** Word A is used to perform action B, or the purpose of A is B.

#### How to Identify
- Ask: "What is A used for?" → B
- Bridge sentence: "A is used for B, just as C is used for D."

#### Subtypes
- **Tool → Action:** Knife : Cut, Pen : Write
- **Object → Purpose:** Clock : Time, Thermometer : Temperature
- **Instrument → Result:** Camera : Photograph, Oven : Bake

#### Examples

| Pair 1 | Pair 2 | Explanation |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Knife : Cut | Pen : Write | Tool used to perform action |
| Broom : Sweep | Mop : Clean | Cleaning tool → cleaning action |
| Telescope : Magnify | Microscope : Enlarge | Optical instrument → function |

#### CSE-Style Example

**Question:** Thermometer : Temperature :: Barometer : _____
- A) Weather
- B) Pressure
- C) Wind
- D) Humidity

**Answer:** B) Pressure
**Explanation:** A thermometer measures temperature. A barometer measures (atmospheric) pressure. The relationship is instrument-to-what-it-measures.

#### Common Trap
Choosing a vaguely related concept instead of the precise function. A barometer relates to weather generally, but its specific function is measuring pressure.

---

### 4.7 Cause-and-Effect Analogies

**Pattern:** Word A causes or leads to Word B.

#### How to Identify
- Ask: "Does A cause B?" or "Does A result in B?"
- Bridge sentence: "A causes B, just as C causes D."

#### Subtypes
- **Action → Result:** Study : Knowledge, Exercise : Fitness
- **Event → Consequence:** Earthquake : Destruction, Rain : Flood
- **Behavior → Outcome:** Negligence : Accident, Practice : Mastery

#### Examples

| Pair 1 | Pair 2 | Explanation |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Study : Knowledge | Exercise : Fitness | Positive action → positive result |
| Negligence : Accident | Recklessness : Disaster | Negative behavior → negative outcome |
| Drought : Famine | Flood : Displacement | Natural event → human consequence |

#### CSE-Style Example

**Question:** Negligence : Accident :: Diligence : _____
- A) Failure
- B) Laziness
- C) Success
- D) Effort

**Answer:** C) Success
**Explanation:** Negligence (carelessness) causes accidents. Diligence (careful effort) causes success. The relationship is behavior-to-outcome.

#### Common Trap
Confusing cause-effect with function. "Study : Knowledge" — studying *causes* knowledge (cause-effect), while "Pen : Write" — a pen is *used for* writing (function). The distinction: cause-effect involves a process over time; function is immediate purpose.

### 4.8 Degree or Intensity Analogies

**Pattern:** Word A and Word B describe the same quality but at different levels of intensity.

#### How to Identify
- Ask: "Is B a stronger/weaker version of A?"
- Bridge sentence: "A is a mild form of B, just as C is a mild form of D."

#### Direction Matters
- **Mild → Intense:** Warm : Hot, Annoyed : Furious
- **Intense → Mild:** Terrified : Nervous, Ecstatic : Happy

#### Examples

| Pair 1 | Pair 2 | Explanation |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Warm : Hot | Cool : Cold | Mild → intense (temperature) |
| Dislike : Hate | Like : Love | Mild → intense (emotion) |
| Jog : Sprint | Walk : Run | Slow → fast (movement) |
| Drizzle : Downpour | Breeze : Gale | Mild → intense (weather) |

#### CSE-Style Example

**Question:** Annoyed : Furious :: Pleased : _____
- A) Content
- B) Ecstatic
- C) Satisfied
- D) Calm

**Answer:** B) Ecstatic
**Explanation:** Annoyed is a mild form of furious (both are anger, different intensity). Pleased is a mild form of ecstatic (both are happiness, different intensity). The relationship is mild-to-extreme.

#### Common Trap
Choosing a synonym instead of an intensified form. "Pleased : Content" are near-synonyms at the same level — not a degree shift. "Pleased : Ecstatic" shows the jump from moderate to extreme.

---

### 4.9 Category and Classification Analogies

**Pattern:** Word A is a specific example (member) of category B, or A belongs to class B.

#### How to Identify
- Ask: "Is A a type of B?" or "Does A belong to the category B?"
- Bridge sentence: "A is a type of B, just as C is a type of D."

#### Subtypes
- **Specific → General:** Rose : Flower, Eagle : Bird
- **Individual → Species:** Labrador : Dog, Siamese : Cat
- **Example → Category:** Piano : Instrument, Sonnet : Poem

#### Examples

| Pair 1 | Pair 2 | Explanation |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Rose : Flower | Eagle : Bird | Specific example → general category |
| Hammer : Tool | Sedan : Vehicle | Object → classification |
| Algebra : Mathematics | Biology : Science | Subject → field |

#### CSE-Style Example

**Question:** Sonnet : Poem :: Waltz : _____
- A) Music
- B) Dance
- C) Song
- D) Rhythm

**Answer:** B) Dance
**Explanation:** A sonnet is a type of poem. A waltz is a type of dance. The relationship is specific-to-category.

#### Common Trap
Choosing a related but incorrect category. A waltz involves music, but a waltz *is* a dance — not a type of music. Precision in classification matters.

### 4.10 Contextual Analogy Analysis

This is where most examinees lose points. Some analogies do not fit neatly into one obvious category, or they use unfamiliar vocabulary that obscures the relationship.

#### Strategy: Analyze Before You Choose

1. **Identify the relationship in Pair 1 precisely.** Don't settle for "they're related." Articulate *how* they're related.
2. **Form a specific bridge sentence.** "A ___ B" — fill in the exact verb or preposition.
3. **Test every choice against your bridge sentence.** The correct answer will fit perfectly; wrong answers will require you to change the bridge sentence.

#### Example of Hidden Relationships

**Question:** Marathon : Stamina :: Exam : _____
- A) Paper
- B) Preparation
- C) Knowledge
- D) Stress

Analysis:
- What does a marathon *test*? → Stamina
- What does an exam *test*? → Knowledge
- Bridge sentence: "A marathon tests stamina, just as an exam tests knowledge."

**Answer:** C) Knowledge

#### Why "Preparation" Is Wrong
Preparation is what you do *before* an exam — it's a cause, not what the exam tests. The relationship is "event tests quality," not "event requires action."

### 4.11 Elimination Strategies

When you cannot immediately identify the answer, use systematic elimination.

#### Step-by-Step Elimination Process

1. **Identify the relationship type in Pair 1** (synonym, antonym, part-whole, etc.)
2. **Eliminate choices that use a different relationship type.** If Pair 1 is antonyms, eliminate any choice that shows synonyms, cause-effect, or other types.
3. **Eliminate choices with reversed direction.** If Pair 1 is part-to-whole, eliminate any choice that shows whole-to-part.
4. **Among remaining choices, pick the most precise parallel.** If two choices seem to work, the one with the tighter logical match wins.

#### Elimination Example

**Question:** Author : Novel :: Composer : _____
- A) Music
- B) Symphony
- C) Piano
- D) Concert

Elimination:
- Relationship: "An author *creates* a novel"
- A) Music — too broad (a composer creates music, but "music" is a general category, not a specific creation like "novel")
- B) Symphony — a composer creates a symphony ✓ (specific creative work, parallel to novel)
- C) Piano — a composer *plays* a piano, not creates it ✗
- D) Concert — a composer *performs at* a concert, not creates it ✗

**Answer:** B) Symphony — most precise parallel (creator → specific creative work)

### 4.12 Common Analogy Errors

#### Error 1: Focusing Only on Word Meaning
**Wrong thinking:** "Author and Novel are both about books, so Composer and Music are both about sound."
**Right thinking:** "Author *creates* a Novel, so I need something a Composer *creates*."

#### Error 2: Ignoring Relationship Type
**Wrong thinking:** "Hot : Cold are opposites, and Big : Large are... also a pair!"
**Right thinking:** "Hot : Cold are antonyms. I need another antonym pair, not a synonym pair."

#### Error 3: Choosing Partial Relationships
**Wrong thinking:** "Wheel : Car — wheels are on cars. Engine : Car — engines are also on cars!"
**Right thinking:** "Wheel : Car is part-to-whole. I need another part-to-whole, like Page : Book."

#### Error 4: Misunderstanding Direction
**Wrong thinking:** "Tree : Forest and Ocean : Fish both involve nature."
**Right thinking:** "Tree : Forest is member-to-group. Ocean : Fish is habitat-to-inhabitant — different relationship."

#### Error 5: Relying on Memorization
Memorizing word pairs without understanding the underlying relationship type will fail when the exam uses unfamiliar vocabulary. Learn the *types*, not just examples.

### 4.13 Step-by-Step Solving Strategy

Use this method for every analogy question:

#### The BRIDGE Method

| Step | Action | Example |
|------|--------|---------|
| **B** — Build the bridge | Create a sentence linking Pair 1 | "A knife is used to cut" |
| **R** — Recognize the type | Name the relationship category | Function/Purpose |
| **I** — Insert each choice | Test each option in your bridge sentence | "A pen is used to write" ✓ |
| **D** — Discard mismatches | Eliminate choices that don't fit | "A pen is used to ink" ✗ |
| **G** — Go with precision | Choose the most exact parallel | Select the tightest match |
| **E** — Evaluate direction | Confirm the order is preserved | Part→Whole, not Whole→Part |

### 4.14 Exam Strategies for Time Pressure

On the actual CSE, you have limited time per item. These strategies maximize speed:

1. **Identify the relationship type within 5 seconds.** If you can name it (synonym, antonym, function, etc.), you can eliminate 2-3 choices immediately.
2. **Use the bridge sentence shortcut.** A one-sentence bridge is faster than analyzing each choice independently.
3. **When stuck, eliminate the obvious wrong answers first.** Even removing one choice improves your odds from 25% to 33%.
4. **Don't overthink easy items.** If the relationship is immediately clear, trust your first instinct and move on.
5. **Flag and return.** If an analogy uses vocabulary you don't know, skip it and return after completing easier items.
6. **Watch for "almost right" traps.** The second-best answer is designed to catch people who don't verify precision.

## Mini Practice Set

**Instructions:** Identify the relationship and choose the best answer.

**1.** Pen : Write :: Scissors : _____
- A) Paper  B) Cut  C) Sharp  D) Craft
- **Answer:** B) Cut
- **Explanation:** A pen is used to write. Scissors are used to cut. (Function)

**2.** Fish : School :: Wolf : _____
- A) Pack  B) Forest  C) Hunt  D) Fur
- **Answer:** A) Pack
- **Explanation:** A group of fish is a school. A group of wolves is a pack. (Member → Group name)

**3.** Elated : Happy :: Furious : _____
- A) Sad  B) Angry  C) Calm  D) Excited
- **Answer:** B) Angry
- **Explanation:** Elated is an intense form of happy. Furious is an intense form of angry. (Intense → Mild)

**4.** Petal : Flower :: Brick : _____
- A) Cement  B) Wall  C) Red  D) Heavy
- **Answer:** B) Wall
- **Explanation:** A petal is part of a flower. A brick is part of a wall. (Part → Whole)

**5.** Drought : Famine :: Earthquake : _____
- A) Richter  B) Ground  C) Destruction  D) Plate
- **Answer:** C) Destruction
- **Explanation:** Drought causes famine. An earthquake causes destruction. (Cause → Effect)

**6.** Generous : Stingy :: Brave : _____
- A) Heroic  B) Cowardly  C) Strong  D) Bold
- **Answer:** B) Cowardly
- **Explanation:** Generous is the opposite of stingy. Brave is the opposite of cowardly. (Antonyms)

**7.** Sonnet : Poem :: Waltz : _____
- A) Music  B) Dance  C) Rhythm  D) Stage
- **Answer:** B) Dance
- **Explanation:** A sonnet is a type of poem. A waltz is a type of dance. (Specific → Category)

**8.** Author : Novel :: Sculptor : _____
- A) Marble  B) Statue  C) Museum  D) Chisel
- **Answer:** B) Statue
- **Explanation:** An author creates a novel. A sculptor creates a statue. (Creator → Creation)

**9.** Thermometer : Temperature :: Speedometer : _____
- A) Car  B) Speed  C) Distance  D) Dashboard
- **Answer:** B) Speed
- **Explanation:** A thermometer measures temperature. A speedometer measures speed. (Instrument → Measurement)

**10.** Caterpillar : Butterfly :: Tadpole : _____
- A) Water  B) Frog  C) Pond  D) Swim
- **Answer:** B) Frog
- **Explanation:** A caterpillar becomes a butterfly. A tadpole becomes a frog. (Young form → Adult form)

**11.** Library : Books :: Armory : _____
- A) Soldiers  B) Weapons  C) War  D) Metal
- **Answer:** B) Weapons
- **Explanation:** A library stores books. An armory stores weapons. (Place → What it stores)

**12.** Loquacious : Talkative :: Reticent : _____
- A) Shy  B) Reserved  C) Loud  D) Angry
- **Answer:** B) Reserved
- **Explanation:** Loquacious means talkative (synonyms). Reticent means reserved (synonyms). (Synonym pairs)

**13.** Conductor : Orchestra :: Captain : _____
- A) Ship  B) Team  C) Uniform  D) Ocean
- **Answer:** B) Team
- **Explanation:** A conductor leads an orchestra. A captain leads a team. (Leader → Group led)

**14.** Famished : Hungry :: Exhausted : _____
- A) Tired  B) Sleepy  C) Weak  D) Lazy
- **Answer:** A) Tired
- **Explanation:** Famished is an extreme form of hungry. Exhausted is an extreme form of tired. (Intense → Mild)

**15.** Archipelago : Islands :: Constellation : _____
- A) Sky  B) Stars  C) Night  D) Space
- **Answer:** B) Stars
- **Explanation:** An archipelago is a group of islands. A constellation is a group of stars. (Group → Members)

**16.** Vaccine : Prevention :: Medicine : _____
- A) Doctor  B) Cure  C) Pharmacy  D) Illness
- **Answer:** B) Cure
- **Explanation:** A vaccine is used for prevention. Medicine is used for cure. (Tool → Purpose)

**17.** Reckless : Cautious :: Extravagant : _____
- A) Wealthy  B) Frugal  C) Generous  D) Lavish
- **Answer:** B) Frugal
- **Explanation:** Reckless is the opposite of cautious. Extravagant is the opposite of frugal. (Antonyms)

**18.** Seed : Tree :: Egg : _____
- A) Nest  B) Bird  C) Shell  D) Hatch
- **Answer:** B) Bird
- **Explanation:** A seed grows into a tree. An egg develops into a bird. (Origin → Result)

**19.** Stethoscope : Doctor :: Gavel : _____
- A) Court  B) Judge  C) Law  D) Wood
- **Answer:** B) Judge
- **Explanation:** A stethoscope is used by a doctor. A gavel is used by a judge. (Tool → User)

**20.** Prologue : Epilogue :: Introduction : _____
- A) Body  B) Conclusion  C) Summary  D) Chapter
- **Answer:** B) Conclusion
- **Explanation:** A prologue is the beginning; an epilogue is the end. An introduction is the beginning; a conclusion is the end. (Beginning → End counterpart)

### Quick Recognition Tricks

- If both words mean similar things → **Synonym**
- If both words are opposites → **Antonym**
- If one word is "inside" the other → **Part-Whole**
- If one word "does" something to/with the other → **Function**
- If one word "makes" the other happen → **Cause-Effect**
- If one word is a "stronger version" → **Degree**
- If one word "belongs to" the other's group → **Category**
- If one word is "used by" the other → **Tool-User**
