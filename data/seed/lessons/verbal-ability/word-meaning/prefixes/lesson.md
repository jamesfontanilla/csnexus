# Prefixes

## Explanations

### Introduction

Prefixes are word parts added to the beginning of a base word. They change meaning without replacing the whole word. That makes them one of the fastest tools for unlocking unfamiliar vocabulary in Civil Service Exam verbal ability items.

On the ladder of word meaning, prefixes come early. They help you see whether a word means not, again, before, after, between, under, beyond, or many other common ideas. If you can spot the prefix quickly, you already have a strong clue about the answer.

### Why This Topic Is Tested

The CSC likes prefix questions because they test more than memory. They test whether you can break a word into parts, connect those parts to meaning, and still check the sentence for the final answer.

That matters in government work too. A report, memo, notice, or instruction can change meaning fast if one prefix is missed. A single part at the start of a word can flip the idea completely.

### The Prefix Ladder

Think of the lesson as a ladder:

| Step | What you learn | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A prefix sits at the beginning of a word | un + happy |
| 2 | Common prefixes have common meanings | re- = again |
| 3 | The base word still matters | sub + marine |
| 4 | Context decides the exact answer | "The submarine stayed below the surface." |

### Common Prefix Families

The most useful prefix families for exam work are:

| Family | Common meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| un-, in-, im-, il-, ir-, dis-, non-, anti- | not; opposite of; against | unfair, invisible, disapprove, nonprofit |
| re-, pre-, post-, fore-, mid-, semi- | again; before; after; middle; half | rewrite, preview, postpone, foretell, midpoint |
| sub-, inter-, trans-, over-, under-, extra-, intra-, out- | under; between; across; too much; too little; beyond; within; beyond/greater | submarine, interact, transport, overwork, underpay, extracurricular, intramural, outgrow |
| uni-, bi-, tri-, multi-, micro-, hyper-, auto-, co-, mono-, poly-, tele-, pseudo-, pro- | one; two; three; many; small; excessive; self; together; one; many; far; false; forward/favor | uniform, bicycle, triangle, multilingual, microscope, hyperactive, autobiography, cooperate, monologue, polymer, telephone, pseudoscience, proactive |

### What Prefixes Do

Prefixes usually do one of four jobs:

- they show negation, like `un-` in `unfair`
- they show repetition, like `re-` in `rewrite`
- they show time or order, like `pre-` in `preview`
- they show place, size, or amount, like `sub-`, `micro-`, or `multi-`

That is why prefixes are powerful. They do not just decorate a word. They steer the meaning.

### Common Mistakes

Examinees often make these errors:

- guessing the prefix meaning without checking the whole word
- confusing a prefix with the base word
- treating every word that starts with the same letters as a true prefix word
- forgetting that some prefixes can have more than one meaning
- ignoring context when the word can be read in more than one way

### How To Decode A Word

Use this order:

1. Find the prefix.
2. Say the base word.
3. Match the prefix to a common meaning.
4. Read the sentence to confirm the idea.
5. Eliminate choices that do not fit the meaning or tone.

### Quick Signals To Remember

These prefix signals show up often:

- `un-` often means not
- `re-` often means again
- `pre-` means before
- `post-` means after
- `sub-` means under
- `inter-` means between
- `trans-` means across
- `multi-` means many
- `micro-` means very small
- `auto-` means self

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

Question: What does `re-` mean in `rewrite`?

Answer: Again.

Why: `rewrite` means write again. The prefix tells you the action is being done a second time.

### Example 2

Question: What does `sub-` mean in `submarine`?

Answer: Under or below.

Why: A submarine moves under the surface of the water. The prefix points to a lower position.

### Example 3

Question: What does `bi-` mean in `biweekly`?

Answer: Two; every two.

Why: Biweekly means happening every two weeks. The prefix gives the number clue.

### Example 4

Question: What does `auto-` mean in `autobiography`?

Answer: Self.

Why: An autobiography is a life story written by oneself. The prefix points to the self.

## Exam Strategies

### Universal Solving Framework

Use this sequence:

1. Spot the prefix quickly.
2. Recall its common meaning.
3. Break the word into prefix and base.
4. Check the sentence for the best fit.
5. Choose the option that keeps both meaning and grammar intact.

### Time Management

Do not spend too long trying to memorize every word. The exam usually rewards a small set of common prefix meanings. If you know the families well, many items become fast elimination questions.

### Shortcut & Elimination Techniques

- eliminate answers with the wrong prefix meaning
- eliminate choices that change the base word's idea
- prefer the most common prefix meaning first
- check whether the answer matches the sentence's tone
- watch for a prefix that can mean more than one thing

### Exam-Day Tips

- learn the most common prefix families first
- keep a short list of meaning clues in your head
- read the whole word, not just the first two letters
- use context when two choices look close
- trust the simplest meaning when it fits the sentence

## Memory Aids

### Mnemonics

Use this ladder mnemonic:

- **N**egation
- **R**epeat
- **T**ime
- **P**osition
- **N**umber

If you can place a prefix into one of those groups, the answer usually comes faster.

### Mental Models

Think of a prefix as a signpost at the front of a word. The signpost tells you which direction to read the meaning:

- not
- again
- before
- after
- under
- between
- many
- self

### Shortcuts & Tricks

- if a word sounds like it means the opposite, check for a negative prefix
- if the word sounds repeated, look for `re-`
- if the word sounds like time comes first, look for `pre-` or `post-`
- if the word sounds like quantity, check `uni-`, `bi-`, `tri-`, or `multi-`
- if the word sounds technical, look for size or distance prefixes like `micro-` and `tele-`

## Practice & Review

### Before You Practice

Make sure you can do these three things:

- name the prefix in a word
- tell what the prefix usually means
- use the sentence to confirm the answer

### Which Method?

Question: The word starts with `re-` and the sentence suggests an action done again. Which method should you use?
Answer: Match the prefix to its common meaning and confirm with context.

Question: The word starts with `sub-` and the sentence suggests a lower position. Which method should you use?
Answer: Break the word into parts and test the meaning under or below.

Question: The word starts with `multi-`. Which method should you use?
Answer: Check whether the word points to many or multiple parts.

Question: The word looks familiar, but you are not sure the beginning is a true prefix. Which method should you use?
Answer: Read the full sentence and confirm the meaning before choosing.

### Challenge Questions

Question: Why should you not rely on the first two letters alone?
Answer: Because the same letters can appear in words that do not use the prefix in the way you expect.

Question: Why is context still important?
Answer: Because the sentence tells you whether the prefix meaning really fits.

Question: Why is a small prefix list useful on the CSE?
Answer: Because a few common prefix meanings can unlock many vocabulary items.

Question: What should you do if two choices have similar meaning clues?
Answer: Compare the full word, the sentence, and the prefix family before deciding.

## Key Takeaways

- A prefix is added to the beginning of a word.
- Prefixes can change meaning, time, place, size, or amount.
- The most useful exam prefixes are the common ones: `un-`, `re-`, `pre-`, `sub-`, `inter-`, `multi-`, and similar families.
- A prefix is a clue, not the whole answer.
- The sentence still decides the best meaning.
- A good test taker checks both the word parts and the context.
- Learning a small prefix ladder can help with many vocabulary items at once.

## Summary

Prefixes are one of the fastest ways to break open new words. They give you clues about opposite meaning, repetition, time, place, number, size, and direction. In the CSE, that kind of clue can turn an unfamiliar word into a manageable one.

If you remember only one idea from this lesson, remember this: do not read the beginning of the word in isolation. Match the prefix to its usual meaning, read the base word, and confirm everything in the sentence. That habit will help you in word meaning, context clues, and other verbal ability items.
