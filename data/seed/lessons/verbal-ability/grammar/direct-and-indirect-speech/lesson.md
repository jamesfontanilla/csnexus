# Direct and Indirect Speech

## Explanations

### Introduction

**Direct and indirect speech** (also called quoted and reported speech) is a fundamental grammar concept tested in the Philippine Civil Service Examination. Direct speech reproduces the exact words a person said, enclosed in quotation marks. Indirect speech (reported speech) conveys the meaning of what was said without quoting the exact words — it paraphrases the original statement and integrates it grammatically into the reporting sentence.

Mastering speech transformation is essential because:
- Government memoranda frequently require accurate reporting of statements, directives, and instructions
- The CSE Grammar and Correct Usage section tests your ability to convert between direct and indirect forms
- Errors in reported speech signal weak command of tense sequencing, pronoun reference, and sentence structure

### Why Direct and Indirect Speech Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Exam tests reported speech because:
- Official correspondence requires paraphrasing verbal instructions into written directives
- Legal and administrative documents demand precise reporting of what was said, ordered, or requested
- Conversion questions test multiple grammar skills simultaneously: tense, pronouns, word order, and conjunctions
- It is one of the most error-prone areas for Filipino examinees due to differences between English and Filipino speech-reporting patterns

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. Forgetting to backshift the tense when the reporting verb is in the past tense
2. Retaining the original pronouns instead of adjusting them to the reporter's perspective
3. Keeping question word order (auxiliary before subject) in indirect questions
4. Using "that" with indirect questions instead of "if/whether" for yes/no questions
5. Forgetting to change time and place expressions (today → that day, here → there)
6. Using "said me" instead of "told me" (told requires an object; said does not)
7. Retaining quotation marks or question marks in indirect speech
8. Failing to use the infinitive structure for indirect commands and requests

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:
- Distinguish between direct speech and indirect speech
- Convert direct speech into indirect speech accurately
- Apply tense backshift rules when the reporting verb is in the past tense
- Adjust pronouns correctly based on speaker and listener perspective
- Change time and place expressions appropriately
- Use the correct reporting verbs (said, told, asked, ordered, advised, etc.)
- Convert statements, questions, commands, requests, and exclamations into indirect speech
- Identify cases where tense backshift is NOT required
- Recognize and correct common speech transformation errors
- Solve CSE-style speech conversion questions efficiently under time pressure

### 4.1 What Is Direct Speech?
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Tense backshift in reported speech follows a systematic one-step-back rule: present → past, past → past perfect, will → would. This shift occurs because the reporting verb ("said") establishes a past time frame, and the reported content must be positioned relative to that past moment. What was "now" for the speaker is "then" for the reporter — the entire temporal reference frame shifts backward by one step.


Direct speech reproduces the **exact words** spoken by a person. The spoken words are enclosed in **quotation marks** and are introduced by a **reporting clause** (also called a signal phrase).

#### Structure of Direct Speech

**Reporting clause + comma + opening quotation mark + exact words + closing punctuation inside quotes + closing quotation mark**

Examples:
- Maria said, "I am tired."
- The teacher said, "Study your lessons every day."
- "Where are you going?" asked the mother.
- The director announced, "The meeting is cancelled."

#### Punctuation Rules for Direct Speech

1. The reporting clause is separated from the quoted words by a **comma**
2. The first word inside the quotation marks is **capitalized**
3. The final punctuation mark (period, question mark, exclamation point) goes **inside** the closing quotation mark
4. If the reporting clause comes after the quote, use a comma (not a period) before the closing quote: "I am leaving," she said.

#### Examples by Difficulty

**Easy:**
- He said, "I like coffee."
- She asked, "What is your name?"

**Medium:**
- The supervisor reminded the staff, "All reports must be submitted before Friday."
- "Has everyone received the memorandum?" the director inquired.

**Hard:**
- The commission chairperson declared, "We shall not tolerate any form of corruption in this agency, and those found guilty will face the full force of the law."
- "Why," asked the auditor, "have these discrepancies not been addressed in the previous fiscal year?"


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "The formula always works the same way regardless of the problem context."

> **Why it fails:** CSE problems often present variations where the standard formula must be adapted. Blindly applying a memorized formula without checking the context leads to systematic errors.

> **Correct model:** Always read the problem to identify what type of relationship exists (direct, inverse, sentence-role, etc.), then apply the appropriate formula. Verify your answer makes sense in the problem's context before selecting it.

### 4.2 What Is Indirect Speech?

Indirect speech (reported speech) conveys what someone said **without quoting their exact words**. The speaker's message is paraphrased and integrated into the reporter's sentence. Quotation marks are removed, and grammatical adjustments are made to tense, pronouns, and time/place expressions.

#### Structure of Indirect Speech

**Subject + reporting verb + (that) + reported clause (with adjusted tense/pronouns)**

Examples:
- Maria said **that she was tired**.
- The teacher said **that the students should study their lessons every day**.
- The mother asked **where he was going**.
- The director announced **that the meeting was cancelled**.

#### Key Differences from Direct Speech

| Feature | Direct Speech | Indirect Speech |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| Quotation marks | Present | Removed |
| Exact words | Preserved | Paraphrased |
| Tense | Original tense | Usually backshifted |
| Pronouns | Speaker's perspective | Reporter's perspective |
| Time/place words | Original | Adjusted |
| Word order (questions) | Inverted (aux before subject) | Normal (subject before verb) |

#### Examples

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|----------|
| He said, "I am busy." | He said that he was busy. |
| She said, "I will come tomorrow." | She said that she would come the next day. |
| They said, "We have finished." | They said that they had finished. |
| He asked, "Are you ready?" | He asked if I was ready. |
| She said, "Don't go there." | She told me not to go there. |


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "If my chosen answer is close to one of the choices, it must be right."

> **Why it fails:** The CSE deliberately includes distractors that result from common errors — using the wrong operation, misidentifying the proportion type, or reversing the ratio. A "close" answer could be the result of a systematic mistake that the test writers anticipated.

> **Correct model:** Verify your setup before computing. Check that you've identified the correct proportion type, identify the controlling sentence structure properly, and solved accurately. A wrong setup with correct arithmetic still produces a wrong answer — and the CSE will include that wrong answer among the choices.

### 4.3 Rules for Converting Direct to Indirect Speech
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Pronoun changes in reported speech are necessary because the speaker's perspective differs from the reporter's. When Maria says "I will submit my report," she uses first person. When you report this, Maria is now third person from your perspective: "Maria said she would submit her report." The pronouns shift to maintain the same real-world reference (Maria) while changing the grammatical perspective (first → third).


Follow these steps in order when converting direct speech to indirect speech:

**Step 1: Remove the quotation marks.**

**Step 2: Choose the correct reporting verb** (said, told, asked, ordered, etc.) and add a conjunction if needed ("that" for statements, "if/whether" for yes/no questions, the wh-word for wh-questions).

**Step 3: Change the pronouns** to match the reporter's perspective.

**Step 4: Backshift the verb tense** (if the reporting verb is in the past tense).

**Step 5: Change time and place expressions** (today → that day, here → there, etc.).

**Step 6: Adjust word order** for questions (remove inversion — subject comes before the verb).

#### Worked Conversion Example

**Direct:** The manager said, "I will review your application tomorrow."

Step 1: Remove quotes → The manager said, I will review your application tomorrow.
Step 2: Add "that" → The manager said that I will review your application tomorrow.
Step 3: Change pronouns (I → he/she, your → my) → The manager said that he would review my application tomorrow.
Step 4: Backshift tense (will → would) → The manager said that he would review my application the next day.
Step 5: Change time (tomorrow → the next day) → **The manager said that he would review my application the next day.**

### 4.4 Tense Changes in Indirect Speech

When the reporting verb is in the **past tense** (said, told, asked), the tense of the reported clause shifts one step back into the past. This is called **tense backshift**.

#### Tense Backshift Table

| Direct Speech (Original Tense) | Indirect Speech (Backshifted Tense) | Example (Direct) | Example (Indirect) |
|-------------------------------|-------------------------------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Simple present (am/is/are, verb) | Simple past (was/were, verb+ed) | "I **am** happy." | He said he **was** happy. |
| Present continuous (am/is/are + -ing) | Past continuous (was/were + -ing) | "I **am working**." | She said she **was working**. |
| Present perfect (have/has + past participle) | Past perfect (had + past participle) | "I **have finished**." | He said he **had finished**. |
| Present perfect continuous (have/has been + -ing) | Past perfect continuous (had been + -ing) | "I **have been waiting**." | She said she **had been waiting**. |
| Simple past (verb+ed / irregular past) | Past perfect (had + past participle) | "I **went** home." | He said he **had gone** home. |
| Past continuous (was/were + -ing) | Past perfect continuous (had been + -ing) | "I **was reading**." | She said she **had been reading**. |
| will | would | "I **will** help." | He said he **would** help. |
| can | could | "I **can** swim." | She said she **could** swim. |
| may | might | "I **may** leave." | He said he **might** leave. |
| shall | should / would | "I **shall** return." | He said he **should/would** return. |
| must (obligation) | had to | "I **must** go." | He said he **had to** go. |

#### Important Notes on Tense Backshift

1. **"Could," "would," "should," "might," and "ought to"** do NOT change — they are already past forms:
   - "I could help." → He said he could help. (no change)
   - "You should study." → She said I should study. (no change)

2. **"Must"** can remain "must" or change to "had to" depending on meaning:
   - Obligation: "I must leave." → He said he had to leave.
   - Logical deduction: "She must be tired." → He said she must be tired. (no change)

3. **Past perfect** does NOT backshift further — it stays as past perfect:
   - "I had already eaten." → He said he had already eaten. (no change)

#### CSE-Style Examples

**Easy:** She said, "I am studying for the exam."
→ She said that she **was studying** for the exam.

**Medium:** The officer reported, "The suspect has escaped from custody."
→ The officer reported that the suspect **had escaped** from custody.

**Hard:** The witness testified, "I saw the accused enter the building at 9 PM, and he was carrying a large bag."
→ The witness testified that he **had seen** the accused enter the building at 9 PM and that he **had been carrying** a large bag.

### 4.5 Pronoun Changes in Indirect Speech
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Questions in reported speech lose their inverted word order because they are no longer functioning as questions — they become noun clauses. "Where is the office?" (question: inverted order) becomes "She asked where the office was" (noun clause: normal order). The reporting verb "asked" already signals that a question was posed, so the inverted structure is redundant and grammatically incorrect in the embedded clause.


Pronouns change based on **who is reporting** and **who is being reported about**. The key principle: shift from the original speaker's perspective to the reporter's perspective.

#### Pronoun Conversion Table

| Direct Speech | Indirect Speech (if reporter is third person) |
|--------------|----------------------------------------------|
| I | he / she |
| me | him / her |
| my | his / her |
| mine | his / hers |
| myself | himself / herself |
| we | they |
| us | them |
| our | their |
| ours | theirs |
| ourselves | themselves |
| you (listener) | I / me / he / she / they (depends on context) |
| your | my / his / her / their (depends on context) |

#### How Pronoun Changes Work

The changes depend on the relationship between the original speaker, the original listener, and the person reporting:

**Rule 1:** First person pronouns (I, me, my, we, us, our) change to match the **subject of the reporting verb**.
- Juan said, "**I** am tired." → Juan said that **he** was tired.
- Maria said, "**We** are leaving." → Maria said that **they** were leaving.

**Rule 2:** Second person pronouns (you, your) change to match the **object of the reporting verb** (the person being spoken to).
- She said to me, "**You** are late." → She told me that **I** was late.
- He said to them, "**Your** work is excellent." → He told them that **their** work was excellent.

**Rule 3:** Third person pronouns (he, she, it, they) usually remain **unchanged**.
- She said, "**He** is my brother." → She said that **he** was her brother.

#### CSE-Style Examples

**Easy:** Ana said, "I love my job."
→ Ana said that **she** loved **her** job.

**Medium:** The teacher said to the students, "You must submit your projects by Friday."
→ The teacher told the students that **they** must submit **their** projects by Friday.

**Hard:** Pedro said to Maria, "I will lend you my notes if you return them to me tomorrow."
→ Pedro told Maria that **he** would lend **her his** notes if **she** returned **them** to **him** the next day.

### 4.6 Time and Place Expression Changes

When the reporting verb is in the past tense, time and place expressions shift to reflect the distance from the original moment of speaking.

#### Time Expression Conversion Table

| Direct Speech | Indirect Speech |
|--------------|-----------------|
| today | that day |
| tonight | that night |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| yesterday | the previous day / the day before |
| next week | the following week |
| last week | the previous week / the week before |
| next month | the following month |
| last month | the previous month |
| next year | the following year |
| last year | the previous year / the year before |
| now | then / at that time |
| ago | before / earlier |
| this morning | that morning |
| these days | those days |

#### Place Expression Changes

| Direct Speech | Indirect Speech |
|--------------|-----------------|
| here | there |
| this | that |
| these | those |

#### Examples

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|----------|
| "I will finish it **today**." | He said he would finish it **that day**. |
| "She left **yesterday**." | He said she had left **the previous day**. |
| "Come **here**." | He told me to go **there**. |
| "I bought **this** book **last week**." | She said she had bought **that** book **the previous week**. |
| "We are leaving **now**." | They said they were leaving **then**. |
| "I saw him **two days ago**." | She said she had seen him **two days before**. |

#### CSE-Style Examples

**Easy:** He said, "I will call you tomorrow."
→ He said that he would call me **the next day**.

**Medium:** She said, "I submitted the report yesterday and I will attend the meeting here tomorrow."
→ She said that she had submitted the report **the previous day** and that she would attend the meeting **there the following day**.

**Hard:** The director said, "We discussed this matter last month, and we expect the resolution to be implemented by next week."
→ The director said that they had discussed **that** matter **the previous month** and that they expected the resolution to be implemented by **the following week**.

### 4.7 Reporting Verbs

The choice of reporting verb affects the structure of the indirect speech sentence. Different reporting verbs carry different meanings and require different grammatical patterns.

#### Common Reporting Verbs and Their Patterns

| Reporting Verb | Usage | Pattern | Example |
|---------------|-------|---------|---------|
| said | neutral reporting | said (that) + clause | He said that he was busy. |
| told | reporting to someone | told + person + (that) + clause | He told me that he was busy. |
| asked | questions | asked + if/whether/wh-word + clause | She asked if I was ready. |
| ordered | commands (authority) | ordered + person + to-infinitive | He ordered them to leave. |
| told (command) | commands (general) | told + person + to-infinitive | She told him to sit down. |
| requested | polite requests | requested + person + to-infinitive | He requested me to wait. |
| advised | giving advice | advised + person + to-infinitive | She advised him to study harder. |
| suggested | making suggestions | suggested + that + clause / suggested + -ing | He suggested that we leave early. |
| warned | giving warnings | warned + person + (not) + to-infinitive | She warned them not to be late. |
| promised | making promises | promised + (person) + to-infinitive / that + clause | He promised to return. |
| reminded | reminding | reminded + person + to-infinitive | She reminded me to bring my ID. |
| encouraged | encouraging | encouraged + person + to-infinitive | He encouraged her to apply. |
| insisted | insisting | insisted + that + clause | She insisted that he was innocent. |
| denied | denying | denied + -ing / denied + that + clause | He denied stealing the money. |
| admitted | admitting | admitted + -ing / admitted + that + clause | She admitted that she was wrong. |
| explained | explaining | explained + that + clause | He explained that the delay was unavoidable. |
| complained | complaining | complained + that + clause | She complained that the room was cold. |

#### Critical Distinction: "Said" vs. "Told"

- **Said** does NOT take a personal object directly: He said that... ✅ / He said me that... ❌
- **Told** REQUIRES a personal object: He told me that... ✅ / He told that... ❌
- **Said to** can introduce a person: He said to me that... ✅ (but "told me" is preferred)

#### CSE-Style Examples

**Easy:** The teacher said, "Open your books."
→ The teacher **told** the students **to open** their books.

**Medium:** "Please don't make noise in the library," the librarian said to the visitors.
→ The librarian **requested** the visitors **not to make** noise in the library.

**Hard:** "If I were you, I would not sign that contract without consulting a lawyer first," the senior officer said to the new employee.
→ The senior officer **advised** the new employee **not to sign** that contract without consulting a lawyer first.

### 4.8 Indirect Speech for Statements (Declarative Sentences)

Statements are the most common type of speech conversion tested in the CSE. The conjunction "that" introduces the reported clause (it can be omitted in informal speech but is preferred in formal/exam contexts).

#### Pattern

**Direct:** Subject + said/told, "Statement."
**Indirect:** Subject + said/told (person) + that + adjusted statement.

#### Step-by-Step Examples

**Example 1 (Easy):**
- Direct: He said, "I am a government employee."
- Indirect: He said **that he was** a government employee.
- Changes: I → he, am → was

**Example 2 (Medium):**
- Direct: She said, "I have already submitted my application."
- Indirect: She said **that she had already submitted her** application.
- Changes: I → she, have submitted → had submitted, my → her

**Example 3 (Hard):**
- Direct: The secretary said to the applicants, "Your documents will be processed within five working days, and you can claim your certificates next week."
- Indirect: The secretary told the applicants **that their documents would be processed within five working days and that they could claim their certificates the following week**.
- Changes: your → their, will → would, you → they, can → could, next week → the following week

### 4.9 Indirect Speech for Questions

Questions require special attention because the **word order changes** from inverted (question) order to normal (statement) order. There are two types:

#### Yes/No Questions → Use "if" or "whether"

**Pattern:** Subject + asked + if/whether + subject + verb (normal order)

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|----------|
| "Are you coming?" he asked. | He asked **if/whether I was** coming. |
| "Did she pass the exam?" | He asked **if/whether she had passed** the exam. |
| "Can you help me?" she asked. | She asked **if/whether I could** help her. |
| "Will you attend the meeting?" | He asked **if/whether I would** attend the meeting. |
| "Have they arrived?" | She asked **if/whether they had** arrived. |

**Key Rules:**
- Remove the question mark
- Change inverted order (verb before subject) to normal order (subject before verb)
- Remove auxiliary "do/does/did" — they disappear in indirect speech
- Apply tense backshift as normal

**Example with "do/does/did" removal:**
- Direct: "Do you like coffee?" she asked.
- Indirect: She asked if I **liked** coffee. (NOT: "if I did like")
- Direct: "Does he work here?" she asked.
- Indirect: She asked if he **worked** there. (NOT: "if he does work")

#### Wh-Questions → Keep the question word

**Pattern:** Subject + asked + wh-word + subject + verb (normal order)

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|----------|
| "Where do you live?" he asked. | He asked **where I lived**. |
| "What is your name?" she asked. | She asked **what my name was**. |
| "When will the results come out?" | He asked **when the results would come out**. |
| "Why did you leave early?" | She asked **why I had left** early. |
| "How many applicants passed?" | He asked **how many applicants had passed**. |
| "Who is responsible?" | She asked **who was responsible**. |

**Key Rules:**
- The wh-word (what, where, when, why, how, who, which, whose) stays in place
- Word order becomes normal (subject + verb), NOT inverted
- Remove auxiliary "do/does/did"
- No "that" or "if" — the wh-word serves as the connector

**Special case — "Who" as subject:** When "who" is already the subject, word order does not change:
- Direct: "Who broke the window?" → Indirect: She asked who had broken the window. (same order)

#### CSE-Style Examples

**Easy:** "Is the office open today?" she asked.
→ She asked **if the office was open that day**.

**Medium:** "Where did you put the documents that I gave you last week?" the supervisor asked.
→ The supervisor asked **where I had put the documents that he/she had given me the previous week**.

**Hard:** "How many of the applicants who submitted their requirements before the deadline have already been scheduled for an interview?" the HR director inquired.
→ The HR director inquired **how many of the applicants who had submitted their requirements before the deadline had already been scheduled for an interview**.

### 4.10 Indirect Speech for Commands and Requests

Commands (imperatives) and requests use the **infinitive structure** in indirect speech. The reporting verb changes to reflect the force of the original utterance.

#### Pattern

**Commands:** Subject + told/ordered/commanded + person + **to + base verb**
**Negative commands:** Subject + told/ordered + person + **not to + base verb**
**Requests:** Subject + asked/requested + person + **to + base verb**
**Negative requests:** Subject + asked/requested + person + **not to + base verb**

#### Examples — Commands

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|----------|
| "Sit down," the teacher said. | The teacher told them **to sit down**. |
| "Submit your reports," the boss said. | The boss ordered them **to submit** their reports. |
| "Don't be late," she said. | She told him **not to be** late. |
| "Don't touch the evidence," the officer said. | The officer ordered them **not to touch** the evidence. |
| "Stand up and recite," the professor said. | The professor told the student **to stand up and recite**. |

#### Examples — Requests

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|----------|
| "Please help me," she said. | She asked/requested me **to help** her. |
| "Could you open the window?" he said. | He asked me **to open** the window. |
| "Would you mind waiting?" she said. | She requested me **to wait**. |
| "Please don't disturb me," he said. | He requested me **not to disturb** him. |

#### Choosing the Right Reporting Verb

| Original Tone | Reporting Verb |
|--------------|---------------|
| Neutral instruction | told |
| Authority/force | ordered, commanded |
| Polite request | asked, requested |
| Advice | advised |
| Warning | warned |
| Encouragement | encouraged, urged |
| Reminder | reminded |

#### CSE-Style Examples

**Easy:** "Close the door," the teacher said to the student.
→ The teacher told the student **to close** the door.

**Medium:** "Please do not leave your belongings unattended in the examination room," the proctor said to the examinees.
→ The proctor requested the examinees **not to leave** their belongings unattended in the examination room.

**Hard:** "Review all the documents carefully before signing them, and do not hesitate to ask questions if anything is unclear," the legal officer said to the new employees.
→ The legal officer advised the new employees **to review** all the documents carefully before signing them and **not to hesitate to ask** questions if anything was unclear.

### 4.11 Indirect Speech for Exclamations

Exclamations express strong emotions (surprise, joy, anger, admiration). In indirect speech, the emotion is reported using appropriate verbs and adjectives rather than the exclamatory structure.

#### Pattern

**Direct:** "How/What + exclamatory structure!"
**Indirect:** Subject + exclaimed/cried out/remarked + that + descriptive clause

#### Reporting Verbs for Exclamations

| Emotion | Reporting Verb |
|---------|---------------|
| Surprise | exclaimed, cried out |
| Joy | exclaimed with joy/delight |
| Anger | exclaimed angrily |
| Admiration | remarked with admiration, exclaimed |
| Sorrow | cried out in sorrow, lamented |

#### Examples

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|----------|
| "What a beautiful day!" she said. | She exclaimed that it was a very beautiful day. |
| "How wonderful!" he said. | He exclaimed that it was wonderful. |
| "What a terrible mistake!" the boss said. | The boss exclaimed that it was a terrible mistake. |
| "Hurrah! We won!" they shouted. | They shouted with joy that they had won. |
| "Alas! I have failed," he said. | He lamented that he had failed. |
| "Bravo! Well done!" the coach said. | The coach exclaimed with admiration that it was well done. |

#### Conversion Strategy

1. Remove the exclamation mark and interjections (Hurrah, Alas, Bravo, Oh)
2. Replace "What a/an" with "a very" or "such a"
3. Replace "How + adjective" with "very + adjective"
4. Use an appropriate reporting verb that captures the emotion
5. Apply standard tense backshift and pronoun changes

#### CSE-Style Examples

**Easy:** "What a lovely garden!" she said.
→ She exclaimed that it was a very lovely garden.

**Medium:** "How efficiently the new system processes applications!" the director remarked.
→ The director remarked with admiration that the new system processed applications very efficiently.

**Hard:** "What an extraordinary achievement it is that our department has maintained a zero-deficiency rating for three consecutive years!" the secretary announced.
→ The secretary announced with pride that it was an extraordinary achievement that their department had maintained a zero-deficiency rating for three consecutive years.

### 4.12 Cases Where Tense Does NOT Change

Tense backshift is NOT required in the following situations:

#### 1. Universal Truths and Scientific Facts

If the reported statement is a general truth, natural law, or habitual fact that is still true at the time of reporting, the tense may remain unchanged.

- The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
  → The teacher said that the Earth **revolves** around the Sun. ✅
  → The teacher said that the Earth **revolved** around the Sun. ✅ (also acceptable)

- He said, "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius."
  → He said that water **boils** at 100 degrees Celsius.

#### 2. Reporting Verb in the Present Tense

When the reporting verb is in the present tense (says, tells, asks), no backshift is needed because the report is current.

- She says, "I am happy." → She says that she **is** happy. (no change)
- He tells me, "I will come." → He tells me that he **will** come. (no change)

#### 3. Statements Still True at the Time of Reporting

If the situation described is still ongoing or relevant, backshift is optional.

- She said, "I work at the Department of Education."
  → She said that she **works** at the Department of Education. (still works there)
  → She said that she **worked** at the Department of Education. (also correct)

#### 4. Conditional Sentences (Type 2 and Type 3)

Unreal conditionals do not backshift because they already use past or past perfect forms.

- He said, "If I were rich, I would travel the world."
  → He said that if he **were** rich, he **would** travel the world. (no change)

- She said, "If I had known, I would have helped."
  → She said that if she **had known**, she **would have** helped. (no change)

#### 5. "Would," "Could," "Should," "Might," "Ought to"

These modal verbs are already in their past form and do not change further.

- "I could help you," he said. → He said that he **could** help me. (no change)
- "You should apply early," she said. → She said that I **should** apply early. (no change)

#### CSE Exam Tip

In the CSE, if a question involves a universal truth or scientific fact, both the backshifted and non-backshifted forms may appear in the choices. Choose the non-backshifted form (present tense) when the statement is clearly a permanent truth. When in doubt and both options are available, the backshifted form is the safer choice for exam purposes.

### 4.13 Common Speech Transformation Errors

#### Error 1: Forgetting Tense Backshift

❌ He said that he **is** busy. (when reporting verb is past)
✅ He said that he **was** busy.

#### Error 2: Wrong Pronoun Changes

❌ She said that **I** was tired. (when reporting about herself)
✅ She said that **she** was tired.

#### Error 3: Retaining Question Word Order

❌ He asked where **was I** going.
✅ He asked where **I was** going.

#### Error 4: Using "That" for Yes/No Questions

❌ She asked **that** I was ready.
✅ She asked **if/whether** I was ready.

#### Error 5: Keeping "Do/Does/Did" in Indirect Questions

❌ He asked where **did** I live.
✅ He asked where I **lived**.

#### Error 6: Using "Said" with a Personal Object

❌ He **said me** that he was leaving.
✅ He **told me** that he was leaving.
✅ He **said to me** that he was leaving.

#### Error 7: Forgetting the Infinitive for Commands

❌ She told him **that he sits** down.
✅ She told him **to sit** down.

#### Error 8: Retaining Quotation Marks or Question Marks

❌ He asked if I was ready**?**
✅ He asked if I was ready**.**

#### Error 9: Incorrect Time Expression Changes

❌ He said he would come **tomorrow**. (when reporting verb is past)
✅ He said he would come **the next day**.

#### Error 10: Double Backshift

❌ He said he **had had been** working. (over-backshifting)
✅ He said he **had been** working.

### Exam Strategies for Speech Transformation Questions

#### Strategy 1: Identify the Reporting Verb Tense First

Before looking at the choices, check whether the reporting verb is past or present. If past → expect backshift. If present → no backshift needed.

#### Strategy 2: Check Pronoun Alignment

Quickly verify that pronouns in the indirect version match the reporter's perspective, not the original speaker's.

#### Strategy 3: Watch for Question Word Order

If the original is a question, the indirect form MUST have normal word order (subject before verb). Eliminate any choice that retains inverted order.

#### Strategy 4: Eliminate "Do/Does/Did" in Indirect Questions

Any indirect question that retains "do/does/did" as an auxiliary is automatically wrong.

#### Strategy 5: Match Reporting Verb to Sentence Type

- Statement → said/told + that
- Question → asked + if/whether/wh-word
- Command → told/ordered + to-infinitive
- Request → asked/requested + to-infinitive

If the reporting verb doesn't match the sentence type, eliminate that choice.

#### Strategy 6: Check Time/Place Expressions

If the reporting verb is past tense, "today" should become "that day," "tomorrow" should become "the next day," etc. Choices that retain the original time expressions are likely wrong.

### Memory Aids

#### Tense Backshift Mnemonic: "PUSH IT BACK"

- **P**resent → **P**ast
- **P**resent perfect → **P**ast perfect
- **P**ast → **P**ast perfect
- **W**ill → **W**ould
- **C**an → **C**ould
- **M**ay → **M**ight

#### Reporting Verb Memory: "STAR"

- **S**tatements → said/told + that
- **T**elling (commands) → told + to
- **A**sking (questions) → asked + if/wh-word
- **R**equests → requested/asked + to

#### Time Expression Memory: "TODAY becomes THAT DAY"

Remember the pattern: immediate time words gain distance:
- to**day** → **that** day
- to**morrow** → the **next** day
- **yester**day → the **previous** day
- **now** → **then**
- **here** → **there**

> 🤔 **Why does this work?** Converting direct to indirect speech requires systematic shifts because the reporting context changes the reference frame. Pronouns shift because "I" in the speaker's mouth becomes "he/she" from the reporter's perspective. Tenses backshift because what was present-tense for the speaker is past-tense by the time it's reported. Time expressions shift because "today" for the speaker is "that day" for the reporter. Each shift maintains the same meaning in the new reference frame.

## Worked Examples

### Converting a Statement

**Problem:** Convert to indirect speech: The manager said, "I will approve the budget next week."

**Solution:**
1. Reporting verb "said" is past → apply backshift
2. Remove quotes, add "that"
3. I → he (the manager is male) / she (if female)
4. will → would
5. next week → the following week
6. **Result:** The manager said that he would approve the budget the following week.

### Converting a Yes/No Question

**Problem:** Convert to indirect speech: "Have you completed the training?" the HR officer asked the employee.

**Solution:**
1. It's a yes/no question → use "if" or "whether"
2. Reporting verb "asked" is past → apply backshift
3. Remove question mark, change to normal word order
4. you → he/she (the employee)
5. have completed → had completed
6. **Result:** The HR officer asked the employee if he/she had completed the training.

### Converting a Wh-Question

**Problem:** Convert to indirect speech: "When will the results be released?" the applicant asked.

**Solution:**
1. It's a wh-question → keep "when" as connector
2. Reporting verb "asked" is past → apply backshift
3. Change to normal word order (subject before verb)
4. will → would
5. **Result:** The applicant asked when the results would be released.

### Converting a Command

**Problem:** Convert to indirect speech: "Do not use your cellphones during the examination," the proctor said to the examinees.

**Solution:**
1. It's a negative command → use "not to + base verb"
2. Choose reporting verb: told/ordered/instructed
3. your → their
4. **Result:** The proctor told/instructed the examinees not to use their cellphones during the examination.

### Converting an Exclamation

**Problem:** Convert to indirect speech: "What an impressive presentation!" the panel said.

**Solution:**
1. It's an exclamation → use exclaimed + that
2. "What an impressive" → "a very impressive"
3. Apply backshift if needed
4. **Result:** The panel exclaimed that it was a very impressive presentation.

## Key Takeaways

- Direct speech uses quotation marks to reproduce exact words; indirect speech paraphrases without quotation marks
- When the reporting verb is past tense, apply tense backshift: present → past, past → past perfect, will → would, can → could, may → might
- Pronouns shift from the original speaker's perspective to the reporter's perspective
- Time and place expressions shift to reflect distance: today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, here → there
- Yes/No questions use "if" or "whether" in indirect speech with normal word order
- Wh-questions keep the question word but change to normal word order (no do/does/did)
- Commands and requests use the infinitive structure: told/asked + person + to + base verb
- Negative commands use: told + person + not to + base verb
- Universal truths, scientific facts, and still-true statements may keep the original tense
- "Said" does not take a direct personal object; "told" requires one
- Exclamations are reported using verbs like "exclaimed" with descriptive clauses
- Always remove question marks and exclamation marks in indirect speech — use periods instead

## Summary

Direct and indirect speech is a grammar topic that tests your ability to transform quoted statements into reported form while maintaining grammatical accuracy. The conversion process involves five simultaneous adjustments: removing quotation marks, backshifting tenses (when the reporting verb is past), changing pronouns to the reporter's perspective, adjusting time and place expressions, and selecting the appropriate reporting verb and sentence structure. The CSE frequently tests this topic because it requires mastery of multiple grammar skills at once — tense sequencing, pronoun reference, word order, and conjunction usage. The most common errors involve forgetting tense backshift, retaining question word order in indirect questions, using "that" instead of "if/whether" for yes/no questions, and failing to use the infinitive structure for commands. Master the conversion rules systematically, practice with varied sentence types, and always check that your indirect version reads as a grammatically complete statement — not a question or command in disguise.
