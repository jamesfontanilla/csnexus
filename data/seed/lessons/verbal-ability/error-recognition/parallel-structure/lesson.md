# Parallel Structure

## Explanations

### Introduction

Parallel structure means using the same grammatical pattern for ideas that have the same importance. If one item in a list is a gerund, the others should also be gerunds. If one side of a comparison is a clause, the other side should also be a clause. If a correlative conjunction introduces two equal ideas, both halves should match in form.

In CSE error-recognition items, faulty parallelism is often hidden inside a sentence that looks smooth at first glance. The sentence may switch from a noun to a verb, from a phrase to a clause, or from active voice to passive voice without warning. Your task is to notice the pattern and check whether every part of the sentence is built the same way.

### Why Parallel Structure Is Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination checks whether you can read formal English with precision. Parallel structure is tested because it shows whether you can:

- keep lists and series balanced
- match words, phrases, and clauses correctly
- use correlative conjunctions in a paired way
- compare like with like using `than` or `as`
- spot awkward changes in form inside long formal sentences

This matters in government writing, where a sentence must be clear, balanced, and easy to follow.

### Common Mistakes

Examinees often make these mistakes:

- mixing gerunds and infinitives in one list
- mixing nouns, phrases, and clauses in the same series
- using different structures after `either/or`, `neither/nor`, `both/and`, `not only/but also`, `whether/or`, or `rather than`
- comparing an action with a noun instead of comparing like with like
- shifting from active voice to passive voice inside one parallel set
- leaving items after a colon in different forms

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify parallel and nonparallel sentence parts
- keep lists and series in the same grammatical form
- repair correlative conjunction errors
- compare like with like using `than` or `as`
- choose the safest CSE-style correction when a sentence looks balanced but is not

## What Parallel Structure Does

### Overview

Parallel structure creates balance. It makes sentences easier to read because equal ideas are expressed with equal grammar. Purdue OWL explains that parallel structure can appear at the word, phrase, or clause level, while other writing guides emphasize that paired ideas should match in form.

### Core Principle

If two or more ideas are equal in importance, give them the same grammatical shape.

- nouns with nouns
- verbs with verbs
- phrases with phrases
- clauses with clauses

When the sentence uses a linking verb or a verb of being, the complements should also stay balanced.

### Visualization

| Level | Parallel example | Faulty pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| words | `clear, accurate, and complete` | `clear, accuracy, and complete` |
| phrases | `reading reports, checking records, and filing notices` | `reading reports, to check records, and filing notices` |
| clauses | `that the file was checked, that the form was signed, and that the memo was filed` | `that the file was checked, that the form was signed, and to file the memo` |
| comparison | `swimming in the pool is easier than swimming in the lake` | `swimming in the pool is easier than a lake` |

### Common Mistakes

The sentence may sound almost right even when the structure is broken. That is why you should read the parts one by one and ask whether each part has the same grammatical form.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which revision is parallel?

`The office values accuracy, patience, and being careful.`

Options:

1. The office values accuracy, patience, and care.
2. The office values accuracy, patience, and being careful.
3. The office values accurate, patience, and care.

Reasoning:

- The first two items are nouns.
- The third item should also be a noun.
- `care` matches the other items.

Answer: **The office values accuracy, patience, and care.**

### Quick Check

Question: Which item best completes the list to keep the forms parallel?
Choices:
- filing
- to file
- filed
Answer: `filing`
Rationale: The list uses gerunds, so the missing item should also be a gerund.

Question: Which sentence is parallel?
Choices:
- The plan is practical, affordable, and easy to use.
- The plan is practical, affordability, and easy to use.
- The plan is practical, affordable, and it is easy to use.
Answer: `The plan is practical, affordable, and easy to use.`
Rationale: The items after the linking verb should all be adjective complements.

Question: Which phrase best keeps the comparison parallel?
Choices:
- swimming
- to swim
- swam
Answer: `swimming`
Rationale: The comparison should match the gerund form before `than`.

### Key Insight

Parallel structure is not about sounding fancy. It is about giving equal ideas equal grammar.

## Matching Forms in Lists and Series

### Overview

Parallel structure often appears in lists joined by `and`, `or`, or `but`, or in lists introduced by a colon. Purdue OWL notes that you can use either a repeated `to` or a single `to` before a series of infinitives, but you must still keep the pattern consistent. A strong sentence keeps the list in one grammatical form from beginning to end.

### Core Principle

Match the form to the form:

- gerund to gerund
- infinitive to infinitive
- adjective to adjective
- noun to noun
- clause to clause

### Visualization

| Series type | Parallel example |
| --- | --- |
| gerunds | `reading, writing, and editing` |
| infinitives | `to read, to write, and to edit` or `to read, write, and edit` |
| adjectives | `clear, accurate, and brief` |
| nouns | `patience, discipline, and focus` |
| clauses | `that the file was checked, that the form was signed, and that the memo was filed` |

### Common Mistakes

A list becomes faulty when one item suddenly changes form. The sentence may begin with verbs and end with a noun, or begin with phrases and end with a clause. That is exactly the kind of slip the CSE likes to hide.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which revision is parallel?

`The committee must review the data, check the figures, and to sign the report.`

Options:

1. The committee must review the data, check the figures, and sign the report.
2. The committee must review the data, checking the figures, and sign the report.
3. The committee must review the data, check the figures, and to sign the report.

Reasoning:

- The first two verbs are bare verbs.
- The third item should match that pattern.
- `sign` keeps the series parallel.

Answer: **The committee must review the data, check the figures, and sign the report.**

### Quick Check

Question: Which phrase best completes the sentence: `The office plans to file the forms, archive the records, and ____ the notices.`
Choices:
- send
- to send
- sent
Answer: `send`
Rationale: The infinitive pattern is repeated once and then continued with bare verbs.

Question: Which word best completes the sentence: `The instructions were clear, brief, and ____.`
Choices:
- precise
- precisely
- precision
Answer: `precise`
Rationale: The list uses adjectives, so the third item should also be an adjective.

Question: Which word best completes the sentence: `The clerk worked quickly, quietly, and ____.`
Choices:
- careful
- carefully
- care
Answer: `carefully`
Rationale: The list uses adverbs, so the missing item should also be an adverb.

### Key Insight

When a list starts with one grammatical form, keep that form all the way through.

## Correlative Conjunctions

### Overview

Correlative conjunctions are paired words such as `either/or`, `neither/nor`, `both/and`, `not only/but also`, `whether/or`, and `rather/than`. Towson University notes that correlative conjunctions work in pairs and that whatever follows one part must be parallel to what follows the other part.

### Core Principle

Whatever grammatical structure follows the first part of the pair should match the grammatical structure after the second part.

### Visualization

| Pair | Parallel pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `either/or` | same form on both sides | `either call the witness or meet the supervisor` |
| `neither/nor` | same form on both sides | `neither tired nor distracted` |
| `both/and` | same form on both sides | `both reviewed and signed` |
| `not only/but also` | same form on both sides | `not only checked the figures but also filed the report` |
| `whether/or` | same form on both sides | `whether to expand services or reduce costs` |
| `rather/than` | same form on both sides | `rather scan the records than store the paper copies` |

### Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is putting one correlative part before a verb and the other part before a noun or phrase that does not match. Another common trap is repeating `to` on one side but not the other in a way that breaks the pattern.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which revision is parallel?

`The manager both reviewed and to sign the memo.`

Options:

1. The manager both reviewed and signed the memo.
2. The manager both reviewed and to sign the memo.
3. The manager both reviewing and signed the memo.

Reasoning:

- `both/and` should connect matching verb forms.
- `reviewed` and `signed` are parallel.

Answer: **The manager both reviewed and signed the memo.**

### Quick Check

Question: Which word best completes the sentence: `The officer must either ____ the witness or speak with the supervisor.`
Choices:
- call
- calling
- called
Answer: `call`
Rationale: `Either/or` should connect parallel verb forms.

Question: Which word best completes the sentence: `The report is neither accurate nor ____.`
Choices:
- complete
- completed
- completing
Answer: `complete`
Rationale: The correlative pair joins adjectives.

Question: Which word best completes the sentence: `The clerk not only filed the memo but also ____ the log.`
Choices:
- updated
- updating
- to update
Answer: `updated`
Rationale: The two halves of `not only/but also` should match in verb form.

### Key Insight

If one side of a correlative pair is a verb, the other side should be a verb too. If one side is a phrase, the other side should be a phrase too.

## Comparisons and Linked Constructions

### Overview

Comparisons using `than` or `as` should compare like with like. A sentence should not compare an action with a noun, or a clause with a phrase, if the meanings are supposed to be balanced. UMN Writing for Success explains that comparison words like `than` and `as` should keep the same grammatical structure on both sides.

### Core Principle

When a comparison appears, make the two sides parallel:

- action with action
- phrase with phrase
- clause with clause

The same idea applies to linked patterns such as `the more..., the more...`.

### Visualization

| Pattern | Parallel example |
| --- | --- |
| `than` | `reading the report is easier than reading it aloud` |
| `as` | `the new policy is as clear as the old policy` |
| `not as ... as` | `the office is not as busy as the branch` |
| `the more..., the more...` | `the more carefully the clerk checks the form, the fewer mistakes appear` |

### Common Mistakes

Comparisons become faulty when the sentence compares different kinds of things. A gerund may be compared to a noun, or a clause may be compared to a phrase. The reader can still understand the basic idea, but the structure is not balanced.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which revision is parallel?

`Reading the report is easier than to read it aloud.`

Options:

1. Reading the report is easier than reading it aloud.
2. Reading the report is easier than to read it aloud.
3. Reading the report is easier than it aloud.

Reasoning:

- The element before `than` is a gerund phrase.
- The element after `than` should also be a gerund phrase.

Answer: **Reading the report is easier than reading it aloud.**

### Quick Check

Question: Which phrase best completes the sentence: `The new policy is as clear as ____.`
Choices:
- the old policy
- the old policy was
- the old policy clearly
Answer: `the old policy`
Rationale: The comparison should stay in the same noun-phrase pattern.

Question: Which phrase best completes the sentence: `The clerk worked as carefully as the auditor ____.`
Choices:
- did
- doing
- does
Answer: `did`
Rationale: The comparison needs the same grammatical pattern on both sides.

Question: Which word best completes the sentence: `The more carefully the clerk checks the form, the ____ mistakes appear.`
Choices:
- fewer
- fewest
- less
Answer: `fewer`
Rationale: The linked comparison keeps the structure balanced and uses the countable comparative.

### Key Insight

Comparisons must compare equals. If the two sides do not match in form, the sentence is not truly parallel.

## Common Traps and Safe Exam Choices

### Overview

Parallel structure errors are often hidden in long, formal sentences. The safest exam choice is usually the one that keeps every item in the same form and keeps each side of a pair or comparison balanced.

### Core Principle

Use these quick checks:

- Are the list items the same kind of grammar?
- Do the two parts of a correlative pair match?
- Are both sides of a comparison built the same way?
- Did the sentence suddenly shift from a word to a phrase or a clause?

### Visualization

| Trap | Safe check |
| --- | --- |
| mixed forms in a list | make every item the same grammatical type |
| broken correlative pair | match the structure on both sides |
| faulty comparison | compare like with like |
| mixed voice or tense in a series | keep the same pattern throughout |

### Common Mistakes

The sentence may sound polished even when it is not parallel. Do not choose the option that sounds most impressive if it breaks the structure. Choose the option that keeps the sentence balanced from start to finish.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which revision is safest for parallelism?

`The candidate was asked to explain the plan, to give examples, and how the timeline would work.`

Options:

1. The candidate was asked to explain the plan, to give examples, and to describe how the timeline would work.
2. The candidate was asked to explain the plan, to give examples, and how the timeline would work.
3. The candidate was asked to explain the plan, giving examples, and how the timeline would work.

Reasoning:

- The first two items are infinitive phrases.
- The third item should also be an infinitive phrase.
- `to describe how the timeline would work` keeps the series parallel.

Answer: **The candidate was asked to explain the plan, to give examples, and to describe how the timeline would work.**

### Quick Check

Question: Which sentence is safest for parallelism?
Choices:
- The office is orderly, quiet, and efficient.
- The office is order, quiet, and efficient.
- The office is orderly, quiet, and efficiently.
Answer: `The office is orderly, quiet, and efficient.`
Rationale: The items after the linking verb are all adjectives.

Question: Which phrase best completes the sentence: `The supervisor told the staff that they should stay alert, that they should report problems, and ____ in the log.`
Choices:
- that they should record events
- record events
- recording events
Answer: `that they should record events`
Rationale: The three clauses should stay parallel.

Question: Which phrase best completes the sentence: `The branch chose to scan the records rather than ____ them.`
Choices:
- store
- storing
- stored
Answer: `store`
Rationale: `Rather than` should match the infinitive pattern in the sentence.

### Key Insight

When in doubt, line up the parts side by side and compare their grammar. The best answer is the one that keeps the pattern steady.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

`The clerk likes reading reports, checking records, and ____ notices.`

Options:

1. filing
2. to file
3. filed

Answer: **filing**

Reasoning: The series uses gerunds, so the missing item should also be a gerund.

### Example 2

`The manager was both strict and ____ with deadlines.`

Options:

1. fair
2. fairly
3. fairness

Answer: **fair**

Reasoning: The items after `both/and` should match in grammatical form.

### Example 3

`The more carefully the auditor checks the figures, the ____ errors appear.`

Options:

1. fewer
2. fewest
3. less

Answer: **fewer**

Reasoning: The linked comparison must stay parallel and the noun `errors` is countable.

## Key Takeaways

- Parallel structure uses the same grammar for equal ideas.
- Lists should keep the same form from item to item.
- Correlative conjunctions work in pairs and must stay balanced.
- Comparisons with `than` or `as` should compare like with like.
- The more...the more pattern also depends on balance.
- If the sentence switches from a word to a phrase or clause, check whether the pattern broke.
- The safest answer is the one that keeps the structure steady.

## Summary

Parallel structure keeps formal English clear by matching equal ideas with equal grammar. In CSE error-recognition items, the error usually appears in a list, a correlative pair, or a comparison. A strong test taker checks whether the sentence keeps the same pattern across all its parts. If the sentence begins with gerunds, stay with gerunds. If it begins with clauses, stay with clauses. If it uses a correlative pair, make both halves match. Parallel structure is really a test of balance, and the balanced answer is usually the correct one.

### Mixed Practice Set

1. `The office values accuracy, patience, and ____ in every report.`
   - Answer: `care`
   - Why: The list should stay in noun form.

2. `The committee must review the data, check the figures, and ____ the report.`
   - Answer: `sign`
   - Why: The series uses bare verbs, so the final item should match.

3. `The report is neither complete nor ____.`
   - Answer: `accurate`
   - Why: The correlative pair joins adjectives.

4. `The office must decide whether to expand services or ____ costs.`
   - Answer: `reduce`
   - Why: `Whether/or` should keep the same verbal pattern.

5. `Swimming in the pool is easier than ____ in the river.`
   - Answer: `swimming`
   - Why: The comparison should match phrase to phrase.
