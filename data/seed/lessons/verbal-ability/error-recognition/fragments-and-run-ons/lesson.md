# Fragments and Run-ons

## Explanations

### Introduction

Fragments and run-ons are sentence-structure errors that often appear in Civil Service Examination grammar and correct-usage items. A fragment is incomplete because it lacks a subject, a verb, or a complete thought. A run-on happens when two independent clauses are joined incorrectly, either with no punctuation at all or with punctuation that does not do the job.

### Why Fragments and Run-ons Are Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination checks whether you can read formal English carefully and keep sentences structurally sound. Fragments and run-ons are tested because they show whether you can:

- tell a complete sentence from an incomplete one
- spot dependent clauses that cannot stand alone
- detect fused sentences and comma splices
- repair two ideas with the right punctuation or conjunction
- work quickly without losing accuracy

These skills matter in notices, memos, reports, and other workplace writing where sentence clarity is essential.

### Common Mistakes

Candidates often make these mistakes:

- treating a long sentence as complete just because it looks busy
- assuming every sentence that starts with `because`, `although`, or `when` is complete
- forgetting that a phrase by itself is not a sentence
- joining two independent clauses with only a comma
- using a semicolon after a fragment
- fixing the wrong part of a sentence and changing the meaning

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify a complete sentence, a fragment, and a run-on
- tell the difference between a fused sentence and a comma splice
- recognize subjectless, verbless, dependent-clause, and phrase fragments
- choose the correct repair strategy for each error type

## What Makes a Complete Sentence

### Overview

A complete sentence needs three things:

- a subject
- a finite verb
- a complete thought

If one of those is missing, the sentence is not complete. If two independent clauses are pushed together incorrectly, the result is a run-on.

### Core Principle

When you test a sentence, ask three questions:

1. Does it have a subject?
2. Does it have a finite verb?
3. Does it express a complete thought on its own?

If the answer to any of those questions is no, the sentence needs repair.

### Visualization

| Type | What it has | What it lacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Complete sentence | subject + verb + complete thought | nothing major |
| Fragment | part of a sentence | subject, verb, or complete thought |
| Run-on | two independent clauses pushed together | proper separation |

### Common Mistakes

Do not rely on length. A short sentence can be complete, and a long sentence can still be a fragment. Also, do not assume punctuation alone makes a sentence complete.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Is this a complete sentence or a fragment?

`After the briefing ended.`

Reasoning:

- There is no main clause.
- The phrase cannot stand alone as a full thought.
- It is a fragment.

Answer: **fragment**

### Quick Check

Question: Which sentence is complete as written?
Choices:
- After the briefing ended.
- The clerk filed the memo before lunch.
- The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo before lunch.
Rationale: It has a subject, a verb, and a complete thought.

Question: Which sentence can stand alone as a complete sentence?
Choices:
- Because the memo arrived late.
- Reviewed the report carefully.
- The supervisor approved the schedule.
Answer: The supervisor approved the schedule.
Rationale: It contains a subject, a verb, and a complete thought.

Question: Which sentence is not a fragment?
Choices:
- At the front desk.
- The officer waited at the front desk.
- Because the officer waited at the front desk.
Answer: The officer waited at the front desk.
Rationale: It is a complete sentence with subject and verb.

### Key Insight

If a sentence can stand alone and still make sense, it is complete. If it needs more to finish the thought, it is not.

## Sentence Fragments

### Overview

A fragment is a group of words that is written like a sentence but does not function like one. Common fragments in CSE items include:

- subjectless fragments
- verbless fragments
- dependent clauses standing alone
- phrase fragments

### Core Principle

A fragment is incomplete because it is missing something essential. The missing part may be a subject, a verb, or the rest of the thought.

### Visualization

| Fragment type | Example | Why it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| subjectless fragment | `Reviewed the memo before lunch.` | no subject |
| verbless fragment | `The clerk before lunch.` | no verb |
| dependent clause | `Because the memo arrived late.` | cannot stand alone |
| phrase fragment | `At the front desk.` | no subject or verb |

### Common Mistakes

Many fragments begin with a subordinating word such as `because`, `although`, `when`, or `if`. Others are just phrases that sound natural but never become complete thoughts. In exam items, the fragment may be hidden inside a longer list of choices, so check each option carefully.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which sentence is a fragment?

`Because the report was late.`

Reasoning:

- The sentence has a subject and a verb.
- But it begins with a dependent marker and does not express a complete thought.
- It is a fragment.

Answer: **Because the report was late.**

### Quick Check

Question: Which sentence is a fragment because it lacks a subject?
Choices:
- Reviewed the memo before lunch.
- The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
- The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch, and the supervisor signed it.
Answer: Reviewed the memo before lunch.
Rationale: The verb is present, but the subject is missing.

Question: Which sentence is a fragment because it is only a phrase?
Choices:
- At the front desk.
- The supervisor waited at the front desk.
- Because the supervisor waited at the front desk.
Answer: At the front desk.
Rationale: A phrase is not a complete sentence.

Question: Which sentence is a fragment because it cannot stand alone?
Choices:
- Because the memo arrived late.
- The memo arrived late.
- The memo arrived late, and the supervisor signed it.
Answer: Because the memo arrived late.
Rationale: It is a dependent clause that needs a main clause.

### Key Insight

If the sentence leaves you waiting for more, it is probably a fragment.

## Run-ons and Comma Splices

### Overview

A run-on happens when two independent clauses are joined incorrectly. The two most common forms are:

- fused sentence: no punctuation at all between the clauses
- comma splice: only a comma is used between the clauses

### Core Principle

Two independent clauses need a proper bridge. That bridge can be:

- a period
- a semicolon
- a comma plus a coordinating conjunction
- a subordinating conjunction, if one clause becomes dependent

### Visualization

| Error or fix | Example |
| --- | --- |
| fused sentence | `The clerk filed the memo the supervisor signed it.` |
| comma splice | `The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.` |
| period fix | `The clerk filed the memo. The supervisor signed it.` |
| semicolon fix | `The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.` |
| comma + conjunction fix | `The clerk filed the memo, and the supervisor signed it.` |

### Common Mistakes

Do not use only a comma between two complete thoughts. Do not use a semicolon after a fragment. And do not assume that every sentence with two verbs is a run-on. The real question is whether there are two independent clauses and whether they are joined correctly.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which sentence is a comma splice?

`The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.`

Reasoning:

- Both parts can stand alone.
- A comma is not enough to join them.
- It is a comma splice.

Answer: **The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.**

### Quick Check

Question: Which sentence is a fused run-on?
Choices:
- The clerk filed the memo the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo. The supervisor signed it.
- Because the clerk filed the memo.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo the supervisor signed it.
Rationale: Two independent clauses are pushed together with no punctuation.

Question: Which sentence is a comma splice?
Choices:
- The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo and the supervisor signed it.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.
Rationale: A comma alone cannot join the two independent clauses.

Question: Which sentence is complete and correctly joined?
Choices:
- The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.
- Because the clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.
Rationale: A semicolon correctly links two closely related independent clauses.

### Key Insight

If both parts could stand alone, give them a real bridge. If the bridge is missing or too weak, you have a run-on.

## Fixing Fragments and Run-ons

### Overview

The right repair depends on the error:

- add a subject if the sentence is subjectless
- add a verb if the sentence is verbless
- attach a dependent clause to a main clause
- add a main clause to a phrase fragment
- separate fused clauses with a period or semicolon
- join related clauses with a comma plus a coordinating conjunction

### Core Principle

Do not patch a sentence blindly. First identify the structure problem, then choose the smallest change that makes the sentence complete and clear.

### Visualization

| Problem | Safe repair |
| --- | --- |
| subjectless fragment | add a subject |
| verbless fragment | add a verb |
| dependent clause fragment | attach a main clause |
| phrase fragment | build a full clause around it |
| fused run-on | separate with a period or semicolon |
| comma splice | use comma + conjunction, semicolon, or period |

### Common Mistakes

The biggest repair errors are fixing the wrong part of the sentence, using punctuation without changing the structure, or choosing a rewrite that changes the meaning too much.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which revision best fixes the sentence?

`Because the memo arrived late`

Reasoning:

- The clause is dependent.
- It needs a main clause to finish the thought.
- The best revision attaches it to a complete sentence.

Answer: **Because the memo arrived late, the supervisor postponed the meeting.**

### Quick Check

Question: Which revision correctly fixes the subjectless fragment?
Choices:
- The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
- Reviewed the memo before lunch, the clerk.
- The memo reviewed the clerk before lunch.
Answer: The clerk reviewed the memo before lunch.
Rationale: The revision adds a clear subject and keeps a complete thought.

Question: Which revision correctly fixes the fused sentence?
Choices:
- The clerk filed the memo. The supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo. The supervisor signed it.
Rationale: A period cleanly separates the two independent clauses.

Question: Which revision correctly fixes the comma splice?
Choices:
- The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo, the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk filed the memo the supervisor signed it.
Answer: The clerk filed the memo; the supervisor signed it.
Rationale: The semicolon correctly joins two related independent clauses.

### Key Insight

The best repair is the one that makes the sentence complete with the fewest unnecessary changes.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

`Because the office was closed.` is a fragment because it is a dependent clause. A correct revision is `Because the office was closed, the staff left early.`

### Example 2

`The officer checked the log the assistant updated the file.` is a fused run-on. A correct revision is `The officer checked the log, and the assistant updated the file.`

### Example 3

`At the front desk.` is only a phrase. A correct revision is `The supervisor waited at the front desk.`

### Example 4

`The memo was delayed, the meeting started late.` is a comma splice. A correct revision is `The memo was delayed; the meeting started late.`

## Key Takeaways

- A complete sentence needs a subject, a verb, and a complete thought.
- Fragments are incomplete because they are missing something essential.
- Run-ons join independent clauses incorrectly.
- A fused sentence has no proper break at all.
- A comma splice uses only a comma between complete clauses.
- The safest fixes are period, semicolon, comma plus coordinating conjunction, or a clear dependent-clause rewrite.

## Summary

Fragments and run-ons are among the easiest CSE grammar errors to miss because they often hide inside long, formal sentences. The best way to catch them is to isolate each clause and ask whether it can stand alone. If it cannot, the sentence is a fragment. If two complete thoughts are jammed together without the right bridge, the sentence is a run-on. Once you know which problem you have, the repair becomes straightforward.

- The clerk finished the report; the supervisor signed it.
- The clerk finished the report the supervisor signed it.
Answer: The clerk finished the report; the supervisor signed it.
Rationale: The semicolon correctly joins two independent clauses.

Choices:
- The officer checked the gate.

- The officer checked the gate, and the guard logged the time.
Answer: At the end of the shift.
Rationale: It is only a phrase and cannot stand alone.
