# Connector Words

## Explanations

### Introduction

Connector words and connector phrases show how one idea relates to another. They can add information, show contrast, explain a reason, show a result, mark order, give an example, restate an idea, or set a condition.

In sentence completion, the blank is often testing more than vocabulary. It is testing whether you can see the relationship between the ideas on both sides of the blank. A connector is correct only if it fits the meaning of the sentence and the grammar around it.

Think of a connector as a bridge. If the bridge points in the wrong direction, the sentence may still look complete, but the meaning will not travel correctly from one idea to the next.

### Why Connector Words Is Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination checks whether you can read formal English accurately and quickly. That matters because government work depends on clear memos, notices, reports, instructions, and responses.

Connector-word questions test whether you can:

- follow the relationship between ideas
- choose a connector that matches the sentence's logic
- tell the difference between similar-looking connectors
- avoid choices that are grammatical but logically wrong
- read clause structure and meaning at the same time

This skill matters in workplace writing because a wrong connector can change the message. A report can sound as if it is giving a reason when it is really giving a result, or it can sound as if it is adding a point when it is actually turning in the opposite direction.

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

Examinees often make these mistakes:

- choosing a connector because it is familiar rather than because it fits the relationship
- confusing addition with contrast
- confusing cause with result
- using an example word when the sentence is really restating the idea
- using a condition word when the sentence needs a sequence word
- ignoring whether the connector needs a full clause, a phrase, or a sentence
- reading only the blank and not the whole sentence

These mistakes happen because connector words are small and familiar. But in the exam, small words carry a lot of meaning. You must ask what the sentence is doing before you choose the connector.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify the relationship between two ideas in a sentence
- distinguish addition, contrast, cause, sequence, example, restatement, condition, and exception
- choose connectors that match both meaning and grammar
- recognize coordinating and subordinating connectors
- solve sentence-completion items by reading the full logical flow
- explain why incorrect connector choices do not fit the sentence

## What Connector Words Do

### Overview

Connector words and phrases are signals. They tell the reader how one idea connects to another idea. Some connectors join equal ideas. Some connect a main idea to a dependent idea. Some point to a reason, a result, a contrast, or a sequence.

### Core Principle

Before choosing a connector, ask this question: what job is the blank doing?

- adding another point
- turning to a different idea
- showing a reason
- showing a result
- showing order
- giving an example
- restating the same idea
- setting a condition
- showing an exception

### Visualization

| Relationship | What it does | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| addition | keeps the same direction and adds support | `and`, `also`, `moreover` |
| contrast | turns to an opposite or different idea | `but`, `however`, `yet` |
| cause/result | shows why something happened or what happened because of it | `because`, `so`, `therefore` |
| sequence | shows the next step or time order | `first`, `next`, `then` |
| example/restatement | gives a case or rewords the idea | `for example`, `in other words` |
| condition/exception | sets a rule or a limit | `if`, `unless`, `otherwise` |

### Common Mistakes

The biggest mistake is treating connector words as a memorized list. They are not just vocabulary items. They are logic signals. If you miss the signal, the sentence can become misleading even if it still sounds fluent.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which connector best completes the sentence below?

"The office checked the totals, ____ it verified the signatures."

Options:

1. however
2. and
3. unless
4. for example

Reasoning:

- the second idea adds one more action
- the sentence keeps the same direction
- `and` joins the two ideas correctly
- `however` would show contrast
- `unless` would show a condition
- `for example` would introduce an example, not a second action

Answer: **and**

### Quick Check

Question: What is a connector's main job?
Answer: To show the relationship between ideas.

Question: True or false: every connector can be used anywhere if the sentence sounds natural.
Answer: False.

Question: Which relationship does `however` usually show?
Answer: Contrast.

Question: Which relationship does `because` usually show?
Answer: Cause or reason.

### Key Insight

Connector words are not decoration. They are instructions about how the sentence should be read.

## Coordinating Connectors

### Overview

Coordinating connectors join equal units. They can join two words, two phrases, or two independent clauses. In English, common coordinators include `and`, `but`, `or`, `nor`, `for`, `yet`, and `so`.

### Core Principle

Use a coordinating connector when the ideas are equal in rank and the sentence needs a direct link between them.

- `and` adds
- `but` contrasts
- `or` offers a choice
- `nor` adds a negative alternative
- `for` gives a reason
- `yet` adds a contrast
- `so` shows a result

### Visualization

| Coordinator | Main function | Simple effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `and` | adds | same direction |
| `but` | contrasts | opposite direction |
| `or` | offers choice | one option or another |
| `nor` | adds a negative alternative | neither this nor that |
| `for` | gives a reason | explains why |
| `yet` | contrasts | strong turn |
| `so` | shows result | effect follows cause |

### Common Mistakes

Examinees often confuse `and` with `but` or `so`. Another mistake is using a coordinator when the sentence really needs a dependent-clause connector such as `because` or `although`.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which connector best completes the sentence below?

"The memo was short, ____ it was clear."

Options:

1. and
2. because
3. unless
4. for example

Reasoning:

- the second idea adds a second quality of the memo
- the sentence needs a direct link between equal ideas
- `and` fits the addition
- `because` would give a reason
- `unless` would set a condition
- `for example` would introduce an illustration

Answer: **and**

### Quick Check

Question: Which coordinator usually shows contrast?
Answer: `but`.

Question: Which coordinator usually shows result?
Answer: `so`.

Question: Which coordinator usually adds a reason in formal grammar descriptions?
Answer: `for`.

Question: True or false: coordinating connectors always show the same relationship.
Answer: False.

### Key Insight

Coordinating connectors work like equal partners. They connect ideas that stand at the same level.

## Subordinating Connectors

### Overview

Subordinating connectors introduce a dependent idea. The dependent clause cannot stand alone as a complete sentence. Common examples include `because`, `although`, `if`, `when`, `while`, `before`, `after`, `since`, `unless`, and `provided that`.

### Core Principle

Use a subordinating connector when one idea depends on another idea to make full sense.

- `because` gives a reason
- `although` introduces a concession
- `if` sets a condition
- `when`, `while`, `before`, and `after` show time relationships
- `unless` sets an exception
- `provided that` sets a condition or requirement

### Visualization

| Subordinator | Relationship | What it signals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `because` | reason | why something happens |
| `although` | concession | one fact does not stop the main idea |
| `if` | condition | a requirement or possibility |
| `when` | time | the moment an action happens |
| `unless` | exception | except if a condition is not met |
| `provided that` | condition | only if a requirement is satisfied |

### Common Mistakes

A common mistake is treating a dependent clause like a complete sentence. Another mistake is choosing a connector that shows the wrong relationship, such as using `although` when the sentence is actually explaining a reason.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which connector best completes the sentence below?

"The meeting was postponed ____ the supervisor was ill."

Options:

1. because
2. however
3. next
4. for example

Reasoning:

- the illness explains why the meeting was postponed
- the blank needs a reason connector
- `because` fits
- `however` would show contrast
- `next` would show sequence
- `for example` would introduce an illustration

Answer: **because**

### Quick Check

Question: Which connector often begins a dependent reason clause?
Answer: `because`.

Question: Which connector often means "except if"?
Answer: `unless`.

Question: Which connector often introduces a condition?
Answer: `if`.

Question: True or false: a subordinating connector usually links a dependent idea to a main idea.
Answer: True.

### Key Insight

Subordinating connectors create dependence. They tell the reader that one idea cannot stand alone.

## Addition and Reinforcement

### Overview

Addition connectors keep the sentence moving in the same direction. They add support, bring in another point, or reinforce the first idea.

### Core Principle

If the second idea supports the first idea, use an additive connector.

Common addition connectors include `and`, `also`, `moreover`, `furthermore`, `in addition`, `besides`, `likewise`, and `similarly`.

### Visualization

| Connector | Typical use | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `and` | joins equal ideas | adds without changing direction |
| `also` | adds an extra point | reinforces the first idea |
| `moreover` | adds stronger support | formal reinforcement |
| `furthermore` | adds another point | formal continuation |
| `in addition` | introduces another point | extra support |

### Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is using a contrast connector where the sentence simply adds more support. Another mistake is using an example connector when the sentence is only giving another related point.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which connector best completes the sentence below?

"The office reviewed the budget, ____ it checked the attachments."

Options:

1. however
2. moreover
3. unless
4. because

Reasoning:

- the second action adds another check
- the sentence stays in the same direction
- `moreover` fits the added support
- `however` would show contrast
- `unless` would show exception
- `because` would show reason

Answer: **moreover**

### Quick Check

Question: Which connector usually adds one more point?
Answer: `also`.

Question: Which connector sounds more formal: `moreover` or `but`?
Answer: `moreover`.

Question: Which connector keeps the same direction of thought?
Answer: An addition connector.

Question: True or false: addition connectors usually turn the sentence to the opposite idea.
Answer: False.

### Key Insight

Addition connectors do not change the direction of the sentence. They extend it.

## Contrast and Concession

### Overview

Contrast connectors show a turn to a different or opposite idea. Concession connectors admit one fact but keep the main point moving in another direction.

### Core Principle

Use contrast when the second idea opposes the first. Use concession when the sentence admits something but still moves to the main idea.

- Contrast connectors: `but`, `however`, `yet`, `on the other hand`
- Concession connectors: `although`, `though`, `nevertheless`, `still`

### Visualization

| Relationship | Connector | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| contrast | `but`, `however`, `yet` | opposite or different idea |
| concession | `although`, `though`, `nevertheless`, `still` | admits one fact, then keeps the main idea |

### Common Mistakes

Examinees often choose a reason connector when the sentence is actually turning in the opposite direction. Another mistake is using `and` when the sentence needs a clear contrast.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which connector best completes the sentence below?

"The report was brief; ____, it still answered every requirement."

Options:

1. however
2. because
3. next
4. for example

Reasoning:

- the report is brief, which might suggest it is incomplete
- the second clause turns the idea around
- `however` shows contrast
- `because` gives a reason
- `next` shows sequence
- `for example` gives an illustration

Answer: **however**

### Quick Check

Question: Which connector usually shows an opposite idea?
Answer: `but` or `however`.

Question: Which connector often admits one fact before the main point continues?
Answer: `nevertheless`.

Question: Which connector is closer to concession than simple contrast?
Answer: `although`.

Question: True or false: contrast means the two ideas point in the same direction.
Answer: False.

### Key Insight

Contrast connectors tell the reader to turn. Concession connectors say "yes, but" without losing the main idea.

## Cause, Result, and Conclusion

### Overview

Cause connectors show why something happens. Result connectors show what happens because of that cause. Conclusion connectors show the final inference or outcome.

### Core Principle

Ask one of two questions:

- Why did this happen?
- What happened because of this?

Common connectors include `because`, `since`, `so`, `therefore`, `thus`, `as a result`, and `consequently`.

### Visualization

| Connector | Main use | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `because` | reason | explains why |
| `since` | reason | explains why |
| `so` | result | shows an effect |
| `therefore` | conclusion/result | formal effect or inference |
| `thus` | conclusion/result | formal result |
| `as a result` | result | consequence |
| `consequently` | result | formal consequence |

### Common Mistakes

The biggest mistake is reversing the direction. A reason connector does not show a result, and a result connector does not show a reason. Another mistake is using `however` where the sentence really needs a cause-and-effect link.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which connector best completes the sentence below?

"The printer jammed, ____ the copies were delayed."

Options:

1. so
2. however
3. next
4. for example

Reasoning:

- the jam is the cause
- the delayed copies are the result
- `so` shows the result clearly
- `however` shows contrast
- `next` shows order
- `for example` shows illustration

Answer: **so**

### Quick Check

Question: Which connector usually introduces a reason?
Answer: `because` or `since`.

Question: Which connector usually introduces a result?
Answer: `therefore` or `as a result`.

Question: What should you ask first in a cause-result item?
Answer: Whether the blank shows a reason or an effect.

Question: True or false: cause and result are the same direction.
Answer: False.

### Key Insight

Cause and result are linked, but they are not identical. One explains why; the other shows what happened next.

## Sequence, Example, Restatement, and Condition

### Overview

Some connector words help the reader move through time or clarify meaning more precisely.

- Sequence connectors show order: `first`, `next`, `then`, `afterward`, `finally`
- Example connectors give a case: `for example`, `for instance`, `namely`
- Restatement connectors rephrase the same idea: `in other words`, `that is`, `specifically`
- Condition and exception connectors set rules: `if`, `unless`, `provided that`, `otherwise`, `even if`

### Core Principle

Ask what the sentence is doing:

- Is it moving to the next step?
- Is it giving a specific case?
- Is it explaining the same idea again?
- Is it setting a rule, limit, or exception?

### Visualization

| Relationship | Connectors | What they do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sequence | `first`, `next`, `then`, `afterward`, `finally` | show order |
| example | `for example`, `for instance`, `namely` | give a case |
| restatement | `in other words`, `that is`, `specifically` | rephrase the idea |
| condition | `if`, `provided that` | set a requirement |
| exception | `unless`, `otherwise`, `even if` | show what happens if the condition is not met |

### Common Mistakes

Examinees often confuse example with restatement. Another mistake is choosing a sequence connector when the sentence is actually setting a condition. The sentence must be read for its function, not just for a familiar word.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which connector best completes the sentence below?

"The clerk will resend the form ____ the signature is missing."

Options:

1. if
2. next
3. however
4. in other words

Reasoning:

- the second idea sets the condition for the action
- `if` fits the condition
- `next` shows sequence
- `however` shows contrast
- `in other words` restates an idea, which is not the job here

Answer: **if**

### Quick Check

Question: Which connector usually introduces an example?
Answer: `for example`.

Question: Which connector usually rephrases the same idea?
Answer: `in other words`.

Question: Which connector usually shows the next step?
Answer: `next` or `then`.

Question: Which connector usually means "except if"?
Answer: `unless`.

### Key Insight

Sequence, example, restatement, and condition all help the reader move through the sentence in a controlled way. The blank tells you what kind of movement it is.

## Exam Strategies

### Universal Solving Framework

Use this sequence:

1. Read both sides of the blank.
2. Identify the relationship.
3. Decide whether the connector should add, contrast, explain, order, illustrate, restate, or condition.
4. Check the grammar that follows the connector.
5. Eliminate choices that point in the wrong direction.

### Time Management

If the relationship is obvious, answer quickly. If two choices seem possible, test each one against the sentence's meaning. If the connector changes the direction of the sentence, it is probably wrong.

### Shortcut & Elimination Techniques

- `and`, `also`, `moreover`, `furthermore`, `in addition` usually add or reinforce
- `but`, `however`, `yet`, `nevertheless` usually contrast
- `because`, `so`, `therefore`, `as a result` usually show cause or result
- `first`, `next`, `then`, `finally` usually show order
- `for example`, `for instance`, `namely` usually show illustration
- `in other words`, `that is`, `specifically` usually restate
- `if`, `unless`, `provided that`, `otherwise` usually show condition or exception

### Exam-Day Tips

- read the whole sentence before choosing
- do not use a connector just because it sounds formal
- watch for semicolons, commas, and dependent clauses
- if the sentence turns direction, look for contrast or concession
- if one event causes another, look for cause or result

## Memory Aids

### Mnemonics

- `ADD` for addition
- `TURN` for contrast
- `WHY` for cause
- `STEP` for sequence
- `SHOW` for example
- `SAY AGAIN` for restatement
- `RULE` for condition
- `EXCEPT` for exception

### Mental Models

- Think of addition connectors as a straight road.
- Think of contrast connectors as a turn in the road.
- Think of cause-result connectors as an arrow.
- Think of sequence connectors as steps on a ladder.
- Think of example connectors as a flashlight shining on one case.
- Think of restatement connectors as a paraphrase.
- Think of condition connectors as a gate that opens only if a rule is met.

### Shortcuts & Tricks

- if the sentence says `another`, `also`, or `more`, check addition first
- if the sentence says `but`, `yet`, or `still`, check contrast first
- if the sentence says `because` or `so`, check cause and result first
- if the sentence says `first` or `next`, check sequence first
- if the sentence says `for example`, check illustration first
- if the sentence says `in other words`, check restatement first
- if the sentence says `unless`, check exception first

In the CSE, the safest habit is to read the whole sentence first, then decide what job the connector must do.

## Key Takeaways

- Connector words are bridges between ideas.
- The best connector matches both logic and grammar.
- Addition connectors keep the sentence moving in the same direction.
- Contrast connectors turn the sentence toward a different idea.
- Cause and result connectors show why something happened or what happened next.
- Sequence connectors show order or progression.
- Example and restatement connectors clarify meaning.
- Condition and exception connectors control what happens under a rule or limit.

## Summary

Connector words help a sentence show how ideas relate. In CSE sentence completion, the right connector does not just sound formal. It fits the sentence's meaning, structure, and direction.

If you can tell whether the sentence is adding, contrasting, explaining, ordering, illustrating, restating, or setting a condition, you can usually eliminate the wrong choices quickly. The blank is asking for a bridge, so choose the bridge that points to the right idea.
