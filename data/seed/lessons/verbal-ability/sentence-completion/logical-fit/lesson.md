# Logical Fit

## Explanations

### Introduction

Logical fit means the blank must make the idea flow correctly. In sentence completion, the best answer is not just a word that "sounds right." It is the word or phrase that shows the correct relationship between ideas.

That relationship may show:

- cause and result
- contrast or concession
- addition or reinforcement
- sequence or time order
- example or restatement
- condition or exception

Think of the blank as a bridge. If the bridge points in the wrong direction, the sentence may still look complete, but the logic will break.

### Why Logical Fit Is Tested in the CSE

The Civil Service Examination checks whether you can follow and build clear meaning quickly. That matters in government work because memos, reports, notices, directives, and responses all depend on precise relationships between ideas.

Logical-fit questions test whether you can:

- recognize how two ideas relate to each other
- follow clues that show cause, contrast, or sequence
- choose a connector that matches the sentence's direction
- avoid distractors that are grammatical but logically wrong
- read carefully under time pressure

This is why logical fit matters. A sentence can be grammatical and still be wrong if the idea relationship does not make sense.

### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

Examinees often make these mistakes:

- choosing the most familiar connector instead of the correct one
- confusing cause and effect
- confusing contrast with addition
- using an example word where a restatement is needed
- missing time-order clues
- ignoring the meaning of the full sentence
- letting grammar override logic, or logic override grammar

These mistakes happen because some connector words are common in everyday speech. In the exam, though, you must ask a more careful question: what relationship does the sentence actually need?

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- identify the relationship between sentence parts
- match connectors to cause, contrast, addition, sequence, example, and condition
- distinguish a result from a reason
- distinguish an example from a restatement
- solve logical-fit items with elimination
- explain why a distractor is wrong

## Cause and Result

### Overview

Cause and result questions show why something happened or what happened because of it. These items are common in CSE sentence completion because workplace writing often explains decisions, delays, actions, and outcomes.

### Core Principle

Ask one simple question: does the blank show a reason, or does it show an effect?

- A reason answers "why"
- An effect answers "what happened next"

Signal words often include `because`, `so`, `therefore`, `thus`, `as a result`, and `consequently`.

### Visualization

| Relationship | Common signals | What the blank does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| reason | because, since, for | explains why something happened |
| result | so, therefore, thus, as a result, consequently | shows the outcome |

### Common Mistakes

The biggest mistake is choosing a connector that points in the wrong direction. For example, `however` does not explain a cause. It changes direction and shows contrast.

Another mistake is using a result word when the sentence really needs a reason.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which word best completes the sentence below?

"The office was short-staffed, ____ the briefing was postponed."

Options:

1. so
2. however
3. for example
4. unless

Reasoning:

- the first clause gives the cause
- the second clause gives the result
- `so` fits the cause-result relationship
- `however` shows contrast, not result
- `for example` introduces an illustration
- `unless` shows condition

Answer: **so**

### Quick Check

Question: Which word usually shows a reason
Choices:
- because
- meaning
- grammar
Answer: because
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: Which word usually shows a result
Choices:
- meaning
- so
- grammar
Answer: so
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: True or false: `however` shows cause and effect
Choices:
- True
- It depends
- False
Answer: False
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

### Key Insight

Cause and result connectors act like arrows. Follow the arrow, and the blank becomes much easier to solve.

## Contrast and Concession

### Overview

Contrast questions show an opposite idea. Concession questions go one step further: they admit a fact, but the main point still goes in a different direction.

### Core Principle

Ask whether the sentence is turning away from the first idea.

- Contrast signals: `but`, `however`, `yet`, `on the other hand`
- Concession signals: `although`, `though`, `nevertheless`, `still`

### Visualization

| Relationship | Common signals | What the blank does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| contrast | but, however, yet, on the other hand | shows an opposite idea |
| concession | although, though, nevertheless, still | admits one fact but keeps the main point |

### Common Mistakes

Examinees often choose a cause word when the sentence actually needs an opposite idea. They also confuse `on the other hand` with simple addition. It does not add a similar point; it shifts the viewpoint.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which word best completes the sentence below?

"The report was short; ____ , it still answered every question."

Options:

1. however
2. therefore
3. also
4. because

Reasoning:

- the second clause surprises us because the report is short but complete
- `however` fits the contrast
- `therefore` shows result, not contrast
- `also` adds another point
- `because` gives a reason

Answer: **however**

### Quick Check

Question: Which word usually introduces an opposite idea
Choices:
- however
- meaning
- grammar
Answer: however
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: Which connector often admits a fact before the main point continues
Choices:
- meaning
- although
- grammar
Answer: although
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: True or false: contrast means the same thing as addition
Choices:
- True
- It depends
- False
Answer: False
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

### Key Insight

Contrast tells the reader to turn. When the sentence changes direction, look for a contrast connector.

## Addition and Reinforcement

### Overview

Addition questions continue the same line of thought. The second idea supports, enlarges, or reinforces the first idea.

### Core Principle

Ask whether the blank adds another point instead of changing direction.

Common signals include `also`, `moreover`, `furthermore`, `in addition`, and `besides`.

### Visualization

| Relationship | Common signals | What the blank does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| addition | also, moreover, furthermore, in addition, besides | adds another related point |

### Common Mistakes

The most common error is using a contrast word where the sentence simply adds support. Another mistake is using an example word when the sentence does not introduce a specific case.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which word best completes the sentence below?

"The office checked the totals; ____ , it verified the signatures."

Options:

1. moreover
2. however
3. unless
4. for example

Reasoning:

- the second clause adds another check
- `moreover` fits the added support
- `however` would create contrast
- `unless` shows condition
- `for example` introduces an illustration, not simple addition

Answer: **moreover**

### Quick Check

Question: Which word adds another point
Choices:
- moreover
- meaning
- grammar
Answer: moreover
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: Which word is closest to `furthermore`
Choices:
- meaning
- moreover
- grammar
Answer: moreover
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: True or false: addition connectors always show a new idea that is unrelated
Choices:
- True
- It depends
- False
Answer: False
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

### Key Insight

Addition does not turn the sentence. It keeps the sentence moving in the same direction.

## Sequence and Time Order

### Overview

Some items ask you to follow steps or time order. These questions are common when a sentence describes a process, a sequence of actions, or the next stage in a plan.

### Core Principle

Ask what happens first, next, later, or finally.

Common signals include `initially`, `next`, `then`, `afterward`, and `finally`.

### Visualization

| Relationship | Common signals | What the blank does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sequence | initially, next, then, afterward, finally | shows order or progression |

### Common Mistakes

Examinees often confuse sequence with cause. A sentence can move from one step to another without showing a reason. Another mistake is overlooking words like `first`, `later`, or `finally`.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which word best completes the sentence below?

"The clerk sorted the files; ____ , she stamped each folder."

Options:

1. next
2. however
3. because
4. for example

Reasoning:

- the sentence describes a process in order
- `next` fits the second step
- `however` would show contrast
- `because` would show reason
- `for example` would introduce an illustration

Answer: **next**

### Quick Check

Question: Which word often signals the last step
Choices:
- finally
- meaning
- grammar
Answer: finally
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: Which word often signals the step after the first one
Choices:
- meaning
- next
- grammar
Answer: next
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: True or false: sequence words always show cause
Choices:
- True
- It depends
- False
Answer: False
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

### Key Insight

Sequence words are like markers on a path. Follow the markers, not your guess.

## Example and Restatement

### Overview

Some blanks introduce an example, while others restate the same idea in simpler or clearer words. These questions reward careful reading because example and restatement are close, but not identical.

### Core Principle

Ask whether the sentence is giving a specific case or rephrasing the idea.

- Example signals: `for example`, `for instance`, `such as`, `namely`
- Restatement signals: `in other words`, `specifically`, `that is`

### Visualization

| Relationship | Common signals | What the blank does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| example | for example, for instance, such as, namely | gives a specific case |
| restatement | in other words, specifically, that is | explains the same idea again |

### Common Mistakes

The biggest mistake is choosing `for example` when the sentence is actually redefining the idea. Another mistake is using `in other words` when the sentence is only showing one case.

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which phrase best completes the sentence below?

"The policy listed several checks; ____ , it required two signatures for every release."

Options:

1. for example
2. however
3. therefore
4. unless

Reasoning:

- the second clause gives one specific check
- `for example` introduces that specific case
- `however` would create contrast
- `therefore` would show result
- `unless` would show condition

Answer: **for example**

### Quick Check

Question: Which phrase often introduces a specific case
Choices:
- for example
- meaning
- grammar
Answer: for example
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: Which phrase often repeats the same idea in clearer words
Choices:
- meaning
- in other words
- grammar
Answer: in other words
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: True or false: example and restatement mean exactly the same thing
Choices:
- True
- It depends
- False
Answer: False
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

### Key Insight

Examples point outward to a case. Restatements point inward to the same idea.

## Condition and Exception

### Overview

Condition questions show what must happen before the main idea can happen. Exception questions show what happens unless something changes.

### Core Principle

Ask what rule, limit, or requirement controls the sentence.

Common signals include `if`, `unless`, `otherwise`, `provided that`, and `in case`.

### Visualization

| Relationship | Common signals | What the blank does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| condition | if, provided that, in case | sets a requirement or preparation |
| exception | unless, otherwise | shows what happens when the condition is not met |

### Common Mistakes

Examinees often use a result word when the sentence actually needs a condition. Another mistake is forgetting that `unless` means "except if."

### Worked Mini Example

Question: Which word best completes the sentence below?

"The clerk will resend the form ____ the signature is missing."

Options:

1. if
2. however
3. therefore
4. for example

Reasoning:

- the blank sets a condition for the action
- `if` fits
- `however` shows contrast
- `therefore` shows result
- `for example` shows illustration

Answer: **if**

### Quick Check

Question: Which word means "except if"
Choices:
- unless
- meaning
- grammar
Answer: unless
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: Which phrase prepares for a possible problem
Choices:
- meaning
- otherwise
- grammar
Answer: otherwise
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

Question: True or false: `provided that` shows a condition
Choices:
- False
- It depends
- True
Answer: True
Rationale: The sentence relationship determines the correct choice.

### Key Insight

Condition and exception questions are about control. The blank tells you what must happen before the sentence can move forward.

## Key Takeaways

- Logical fit is about the relationship between ideas.
- The best answer may show cause, contrast, addition, sequence, example, or condition.
- A connector can be grammatical and still be logically wrong.
- `Because` usually shows a reason; `so`, `therefore`, and `as a result` usually show a result.
- `However`, `yet`, and `nevertheless` usually show contrast or concession.
- `For example` introduces a case; `in other words` restates the idea.
- `If`, `unless`, and `provided that` show conditions or exceptions.

## Summary

Logical fit is the part of sentence completion that asks whether the sentence makes sense as a whole. The blank is not just a missing word. It is a missing relationship. Once you learn to identify the relationship first, the choices become much easier to compare.

In CSE-style items, the safest habit is simple: read the whole sentence, name the relationship, and choose the connector or phrase that matches that relationship exactly. If a choice sounds formal but changes the meaning, it is not the answer.
