# Author's Purpose and Tone

## Focus Areas
- Author's Purpose
- Author's Tone
- Mood

## Explanations

### Introduction

**Author's Purpose and Tone** is the study of WHY an author writes a passage and HOW their attitude shapes the language they use. These two concepts — purpose and tone — are inseparable in practice: the reason for writing determines the tone, and the tone reveals the purpose. Together with mood (the emotional atmosphere the reader experiences), they form a triad that governs how meaning is constructed and interpreted in any text.

Unlike literal comprehension questions that ask "What did the passage say?", purpose-and-tone questions ask:
- "Why did the author write this?"
- "What is the author's attitude toward the subject?"
- "How does the passage make the reader feel?"
- "What effect does the author's word choice create?"

These questions require you to step back from the content and analyze the *intent* and *emotional texture* of the writing itself.

#### Why This Subtopic Is Critical for the CSE

The Civil Service Exam (both Professional and Sub-Professional levels) tests author's purpose and tone because:

1. **Government communication requires tone awareness** — Civil servants must read memos, policies, directives, and public communications. Misreading tone (interpreting a directive as a suggestion, or sarcasm as sincerity) leads to errors in judgment and action.

2. **It tests reading maturity** — Identifying purpose and tone requires reading beyond surface meaning. It measures whether you can detect persuasion, bias, and emotional manipulation — skills essential for evaluating reports, proposals, and public statements.

3. **It differentiates competent readers from superficial ones** — Most examinees can identify what a passage says. Fewer can articulate WHY it was written and what attitude the author holds.

4. **Approximately 8–15 questions per exam** involve purpose, tone, or mood identification, making it a high-yield subtopic for score improvement.

5. **It overlaps with other analytical skills** — Understanding tone helps with inference, bias detection, and evaluating arguments.

#### Common Mistakes Examinees Make

1. **Confusing tone with mood** — Tone is the author's attitude; mood is the reader's emotional response. They often align but are not the same thing.
2. **Confusing purpose with topic** — The topic is WHAT the passage is about; the purpose is WHY the author wrote it.
3. **Defaulting to "to inform"** — Many examinees label every passage as informative because it contains information. Persuasive and descriptive passages also contain information — the difference is intent.
4. **Ignoring word choice** — Tone lives in diction. Examinees who skip over adjectives, adverbs, and figurative language miss the tone entirely.
5. **Projecting personal feelings** — Answering "How does this make YOU feel?" instead of "What is the AUTHOR's attitude?"
6. **Choosing extreme tone labels** — Selecting "furious" when the passage is merely "critical," or "ecstatic" when it is simply "optimistic."
7. **Failing to distinguish neutral reporting from subtle persuasion** — Some persuasive writing mimics informative style while embedding evaluative language.

### Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define author's purpose and identify the five major purposes (inform, persuade, entertain, describe, explain)
- Analyze the author's tone using word choice, sentence structure, and contextual clues
- Distinguish tone from mood accurately
- Identify emotional and contextual clues that reveal attitude
- Recognize persuasive techniques disguised as neutral information
- Detect author bias through language analysis
- Apply elimination strategies to purpose-and-tone questions
- Solve CSE-style reading comprehension questions involving purpose, tone, and mood under time pressure
- Avoid common traps including tone-mood confusion, extreme labels, and personal projection

### 4.1 What Is Author's Purpose?
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** The principle behind this operation follows from the fundamental properties of arithmetic. Understanding the "why" — not just the "how" — lets you recognize when to apply this method in unfamiliar problem contexts on the CSE.


**Author's purpose** is the reason an author writes a particular text. Every piece of writing exists because the author wanted to achieve something — to share information, change someone's mind, make someone laugh, paint a picture with words, or clarify a complex idea.

#### The Core Principle

> Before asking "What does this passage say?", ask "Why does this passage exist?" The answer to the second question unlocks the meaning of the first.

#### The Five Major Purposes

| Purpose | Goal | Signal Characteristics |
|---------|------|----------------------|
| **To Inform** | Share facts, data, or knowledge | Neutral tone, factual language, no opinion markers, organized presentation of data |
| **To Persuade** | Change the reader's belief or behavior | Opinion language, emotional appeals, calls to action, one-sided arguments |
| **To Entertain** | Amuse, engage, or provide enjoyment | Humor, storytelling, vivid characters, plot, dialogue, suspense |
| **To Describe** | Create a sensory picture | Rich imagery, sensory details (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch), figurative language |
| **To Explain** | Clarify how or why something works | Step-by-step structure, definitions, examples, analogies, process language |

#### Detailed Breakdown of Each Purpose

**1. To Inform**

The author presents facts without trying to convince you of anything. The writing is neutral — it does not take sides.

Characteristics:
- Third-person perspective ("The department reported...")
- Statistics, dates, names, measurements
- Absence of evaluative adjectives (no "excellent," "terrible," "important")
- Balanced presentation (if multiple sides exist, both are presented)
- Source attribution ("According to...", "The study found...")

Example:
> "The Philippine Civil Service Commission administered the Career Service Examination to 450,000 applicants in March 2024. The passing rate was 32.5%, with the National Capital Region recording the highest number of passers at 28,000."

Purpose: To inform — presents verifiable data without judgment.

**2. To Persuade**

The author wants to change your mind or motivate you to act. The writing is one-sided and uses emotional or logical appeals.

Characteristics:
- First or second person ("We must...", "You should...")
- Evaluative language ("essential," "unacceptable," "critical")
- Calls to action ("We must act now," "It is time to...")
- Rhetorical questions ("Can we afford to ignore this?")
- One-sided evidence (only supporting arguments presented)
- Emotional appeals (fear, pride, guilt, hope)

Example:
> "Our public schools are overcrowded, underfunded, and failing our children. Every day we delay education reform, another generation loses its chance at a better future. We must demand that our leaders prioritize education funding — our children's futures depend on it."

Purpose: To persuade — uses emotional language, one-sided framing, and a call to action.

**3. To Entertain**

The author wants to engage the reader's imagination, create enjoyment, or tell a story.

Characteristics:
- Narrative structure (characters, setting, conflict, resolution)
- Dialogue
- Humor, irony, or wit
- Suspense or surprise
- Creative language and wordplay
- Fiction or creative nonfiction

Example:
> "Mang Tomas stared at the leaking roof, then at the bucket already overflowing on the floor. 'Well,' he said to his cat, who was perched on the only dry chair, 'at least one of us is comfortable.' The cat yawned in agreement."

Purpose: To entertain — uses humor, dialogue, character, and a light narrative tone.

**4. To Describe**

The author wants to create a vivid mental picture using sensory details.

Characteristics:
- Sensory language (what you see, hear, smell, taste, feel)
- Figurative language (similes, metaphors, personification)
- Rich adjectives and adverbs
- Focus on appearance, atmosphere, or experience
- Often present tense or vivid past tense

Example:
> "The Banaue Rice Terraces cascade down the mountainside like a giant green staircase carved by ancient hands. In the early morning mist, the terraces shimmer with dew, and the air carries the earthy scent of wet soil mixed with the faint sweetness of growing rice."

Purpose: To describe — creates a sensory picture using imagery and figurative language.

**5. To Explain**

The author wants to help the reader understand how something works or why something happens.

Characteristics:
- Process language ("First... then... finally...")
- Definitions and clarifications
- Examples and analogies
- Cause-and-effect structure
- "How-to" or "why" framing
- Technical terms followed by explanations

Example:
> "Photosynthesis is the process by which plants convert sunlight into food. The plant absorbs carbon dioxide from the air through tiny pores in its leaves called stomata. Using energy from sunlight, it combines the carbon dioxide with water absorbed through its roots to produce glucose and oxygen."

Purpose: To explain — breaks down a process step by step with definitions.

#### Overlapping Purposes

Many passages have a PRIMARY purpose and a SECONDARY purpose. The CSE asks for the primary purpose — the main reason the passage exists.

| Passage Type | Primary Purpose | Secondary Purpose |
|-------------|----------------|-------------------|
| News article | Inform | Describe |
| Editorial | Persuade | Inform |
| Novel excerpt | Entertain | Describe |
| Textbook | Explain | Inform |
| Travel brochure | Persuade | Describe |
| Government memo | Inform | Explain |

**Key rule:** When a passage both informs AND persuades, look for evaluative language and calls to action. If present → persuade. If absent → inform.


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "The formula always works the same way regardless of the problem context."

> **Why it fails:** CSE problems often present variations where the standard formula must be adapted. Blindly applying a memorized formula without checking the context leads to systematic errors.

> **Correct model:** Always read the problem to identify what type of relationship exists (direct, inverse, part-whole, etc.), then apply the appropriate formula. Verify your answer makes sense in the problem's context before selecting it.

### 4.2 Identifying Author's Purpose

Identifying purpose requires looking at the passage as a whole — not just individual sentences. Use this systematic approach:

#### The PURPOSE Method

**P** — Point: What is the main point or message?
**U** — Underlying intent: Is the author trying to change your mind, teach you, or engage you?
**R** — Reader response: What does the author want you to DO after reading? (Believe something? Know something? Feel something? Act?)
**P** — Perspective: Is the writing one-sided or balanced?
**O** — Organization: How is the information structured? (Story? Argument? Data? Process?)
**S** — Style: Is the language neutral, emotional, vivid, or technical?
**E** — Evidence type: Facts only? Opinions? Sensory details? Anecdotes?

#### Recognizing Informative Writing

Informative writing answers: "What happened?" or "What is this?"

Clues:
- Presents multiple perspectives without favoring one
- Uses hedging language ("research suggests," "data indicates")
- Attributes claims to sources
- Avoids first/second person
- No calls to action

Example:
> "The Department of Health reported 1,200 new dengue cases in Metro Manila during the third quarter. This represents a 15% increase from the same period last year. Health officials attribute the rise to above-average rainfall and delayed fogging operations in several barangays."

Analysis: Neutral reporting, attributed sources, specific data, no opinion → Purpose: To inform.

#### Recognizing Persuasive Writing

Persuasive writing answers: "What should you believe/do?"

Clues:
- Uses "should," "must," "need to," "it is time to"
- Presents only one side of an argument
- Appeals to emotions (fear, pride, guilt, hope, anger)
- Uses rhetorical questions
- Includes a call to action
- Uses loaded language (words with strong positive or negative connotations)

Example:
> "Our barangay's youth programs are woefully underfunded. While neighboring communities invest in sports facilities and scholarship programs, our young people have nowhere to go after school. We cannot afford to neglect an entire generation. The barangay council must allocate at least 20% of its budget to youth development — our community's future depends on it."

Analysis: One-sided, emotional language ("woefully," "neglect"), call to action ("must allocate"), rhetorical framing → Purpose: To persuade.

#### Recognizing Descriptive Writing

Descriptive writing answers: "What does it look/feel/sound like?"

Clues:
- Heavy use of adjectives and adverbs
- Sensory details (colors, textures, sounds, smells)
- Figurative language (similes, metaphors)
- Focus on creating atmosphere
- Often lacks a clear argument or narrative progression

#### Recognizing Explanatory Writing

Explanatory writing answers: "How does this work?" or "Why does this happen?"

Clues:
- Sequential structure (first, next, then, finally)
- Definitions of terms
- Cause-and-effect language
- Analogies ("Think of it like...")
- Diagrams or step references

#### CSE-Style Purpose Questions

Common question stems:
- "What is the author's primary purpose in writing this passage?"
- "The author wrote this passage mainly to..."
- "The passage is primarily intended to..."
- "Which best describes the author's purpose?"


> ⚠️ **Misconception:** "If my computed answer is close to one of the choices, it must be right."

> **Why it fails:** The CSE deliberately includes distractors that result from common errors — using the wrong operation, misidentifying the proportion type, or reversing the ratio. A "close" answer could be the result of a systematic mistake that the test writers anticipated.

> **Correct model:** Verify your setup before computing. Check that you've identified the correct proportion type, set up the equation properly, and solved accurately. A wrong setup with correct arithmetic still produces a wrong answer — and the CSE will include that wrong answer among the choices.

### 4.3 What Is Author's Tone?
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** When you follow this procedure, you're exploiting a mathematical invariant — something that stays constant regardless of how you manipulate the numbers. Identifying that invariant is the key to solving problems efficiently rather than memorizing steps.


**Tone** is the author's attitude toward the subject, the audience, or both. It is revealed through word choice (diction), sentence structure, and the overall approach to the material. Tone is to writing what vocal inflection is to speech — it tells you HOW the author feels about what they are saying.

#### The Core Principle

> Tone is not what the author says — it is how the author says it. Two authors can write about the same topic with completely different tones, creating completely different effects.

#### How Tone Is Conveyed

| Element | How It Reveals Tone |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Word choice (diction)** | "Discussed" vs. "argued" vs. "bickered" — same action, different attitudes |
| **Sentence length** | Short, punchy sentences = urgent, forceful. Long, flowing sentences = reflective, calm |
| **Figurative language** | Metaphors and similes reveal how the author views the subject |
| **Level of formality** | Formal = serious, authoritative. Informal = casual, friendly |
| **Punctuation** | Exclamation marks = excitement/emphasis. Ellipses = hesitation/trailing off |
| **Detail selection** | What the author chooses to include/exclude reveals attitude |

#### Common Tone Words (Organized by Category)

**Positive Tones:**
- Optimistic — hopeful about the future
- Enthusiastic — excited and energetic
- Appreciative — grateful, recognizing value
- Admiring — expressing respect or approval
- Encouraging — supportive, motivating
- Celebratory — marking achievement or success
- Compassionate — showing empathy and concern
- Respectful — showing deference and consideration

**Negative Tones:**
- Critical — finding fault, pointing out problems
- Pessimistic — expecting negative outcomes
- Sarcastic — saying the opposite of what is meant, mockingly
- Condescending — talking down to the reader
- Dismissive — treating something as unimportant
- Bitter — resentful, showing deep dissatisfaction
- Indignant — angry at perceived injustice
- Cynical — distrustful of motives, expecting the worst

**Neutral Tones:**
- Objective — presenting facts without personal judgment
- Matter-of-fact — straightforward, unemotional
- Formal — professional, following conventions
- Detached — emotionally uninvolved
- Analytical — examining systematically

**Complex Tones:**
- Ironic — meaning the opposite of what is literally stated
- Nostalgic — longing for the past
- Ambivalent — having mixed feelings
- Cautious — careful, measured, hedging
- Resigned — accepting something unpleasant without resistance
- Reflective — thoughtful, contemplative

#### Tone Analysis: Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Objective tone:**
> "The city council approved the budget allocation of ₱15 million for road repairs. The project is expected to take six months and will affect three major thoroughfares."

Analysis: Neutral language, factual reporting, no evaluative words → Tone: Objective/Matter-of-fact

**Example 2 — Critical tone:**
> "Once again, the city council has thrown money at a problem without addressing its root cause. The ₱15 million for road repairs will be wasted if the drainage system — which causes the road damage in the first place — remains neglected."

Analysis: "Thrown money," "once again" (implies repeated failure), "wasted," "neglected" → Tone: Critical

**Example 3 — Sarcastic tone:**
> "The city council has generously allocated ₱15 million to fix roads that will deteriorate again within months because, naturally, fixing the drainage system that destroys them would be far too logical."

Analysis: "Generously" (used ironically), "naturally" (mocking), "far too logical" (implying the council is illogical) → Tone: Sarcastic

**Example 4 — Optimistic tone:**
> "The city council's ₱15 million investment in road repairs signals a promising commitment to infrastructure improvement. With proper execution, this project could significantly enhance daily commutes for thousands of residents."

Analysis: "Investment" (positive framing), "promising commitment," "significantly enhance" → Tone: Optimistic

Same topic, four different tones — each created entirely through word choice and framing.

### 4.4 What Is Mood?

**Mood** is the emotional atmosphere of a passage — the feeling it creates in the reader. While tone is the author's attitude (directed outward), mood is the reader's emotional experience (received inward).

#### The Core Principle

> Tone is what the author FEELS. Mood is what the reader FEELS. The author creates the mood through tone, but they are not the same thing.

#### How Mood Is Created

| Technique | Example | Mood Created |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| **Setting details** | "Dark clouds gathered over the empty street" | Ominous, tense |
| **Pacing** | Short, rapid sentences | Urgent, anxious |
| **Imagery** | "Golden sunlight streamed through the window" | Warm, peaceful |
| **Sound words** | "The clock ticked loudly in the silence" | Tense, suspenseful |
| **Color references** | "Everything was gray — the sky, the buildings, the faces" | Gloomy, depressing |
| **Weather/nature** | "A gentle breeze carried the scent of flowers" | Calm, pleasant |

#### Common Mood Words

**Positive Moods:**
- Cheerful, joyful, lighthearted
- Hopeful, inspiring, uplifting
- Peaceful, serene, tranquil
- Warm, comforting, nostalgic
- Exciting, energetic, triumphant

**Negative Moods:**
- Gloomy, somber, melancholic
- Tense, anxious, suspenseful
- Ominous, foreboding, threatening
- Lonely, isolated, desolate
- Angry, hostile, oppressive

**Neutral/Complex Moods:**
- Reflective, contemplative, thoughtful
- Mysterious, curious, intriguing
- Bittersweet, ambivalent
- Formal, businesslike, detached

#### Mood Analysis: Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Suspenseful mood:**
> "The hallway was empty. Maria's footsteps echoed against the tile floor as she walked toward the director's office. The door was slightly open. Inside, she could hear voices — low, urgent, stopping abruptly as she approached."

Mood: Suspenseful/Tense — created through emptiness, echoing sounds, the slightly open door, and abruptly stopping voices.

**Example 2 — Hopeful mood:**
> "After months of preparation, the community garden was finally ready. Neighbors gathered with seedlings and smiles, children ran between the freshly turned rows, and for the first time in years, the vacant lot felt alive with possibility."

Mood: Hopeful/Uplifting — created through community gathering, smiles, children playing, and the transformation from vacant lot to garden.

**Example 3 — Gloomy mood:**
> "The factory had been closed for three years. Weeds pushed through cracks in the parking lot, and the windows — those that remained unbroken — stared blankly at the empty street. No one came here anymore."

Mood: Gloomy/Desolate — created through abandonment imagery, decay, emptiness, and the personification of windows "staring blankly."

### 4.5 Difference Between Tone and Mood
> 🤔 **Why does this work?** This shortcut works because it's a special case of the more general rule. By understanding the underlying principle, you can verify your answer logically even if you forget the exact formula under exam pressure.


This distinction is the single most commonly tested concept in this subtopic and the most frequently confused by examinees.

#### Side-by-Side Comparison

| Aspect | Tone | Mood |
|--------|------|------|
| **Definition** | Author's attitude toward the subject | Emotional atmosphere experienced by the reader |
| **Source** | The author (sender) | The reader (receiver) |
| **Question it answers** | "How does the author feel about this?" | "How does this passage make the reader feel?" |
| **Created by** | Word choice, sentence structure, perspective | Setting, imagery, pacing, sensory details |
| **Can change within a passage?** | Yes — tone can shift | Yes — mood can shift |
| **Relationship** | Tone CREATES mood | Mood is the RESULT of tone |

#### Key Insight: Tone and Mood Often Align — But Not Always

**When they align:**
- Author's tone: Optimistic → Reader's mood: Hopeful
- Author's tone: Critical → Reader's mood: Uncomfortable/Concerned
- Author's tone: Humorous → Reader's mood: Amused

**When they diverge:**
- Author's tone: Sarcastic → Reader's mood: Amused OR uncomfortable (depends on target)
- Author's tone: Matter-of-fact → Reader's mood: Disturbed (if the facts are disturbing)
- Author's tone: Cheerful → Reader's mood: Suspicious (if the cheerfulness seems forced or inappropriate)

#### Worked Example: Same Passage, Different Questions

**Passage:**
> "The government report noted, with characteristic understatement, that the flood response was 'somewhat delayed.' In reality, rescue teams arrived 72 hours after the first distress calls, by which time 200 families had been stranded on rooftops for three days without food or clean water."

**Tone question:** What is the author's tone?
Answer: **Ironic/Critical** — "characteristic understatement" and the contrast between "somewhat delayed" and the 72-hour reality reveal the author's critical attitude toward the government's response and reporting.

**Mood question:** What mood does the passage create?
Answer: **Indignant/Disturbing** — The reader feels anger or disturbance at the suffering described and the government's minimization of it.

Notice: The author's tone is ironic (a technique), but the mood the reader experiences is indignation (an emotion). They are related but distinct.

#### Practice: Identifying Tone vs. Mood

| Passage | Tone (Author's Attitude) | Mood (Reader's Feeling) |
|---------|-------------------------|------------------------|
| A news report about a natural disaster using neutral language | Objective, detached | Somber, concerned |
| A satirical essay mocking bureaucratic inefficiency | Sarcastic, mocking | Amused, possibly frustrated |
| A eulogy praising a community leader's service | Respectful, admiring | Reflective, inspired |
| A warning about environmental destruction with vivid imagery | Urgent, alarmed | Anxious, motivated |
| A nostalgic essay about childhood in a small town | Warm, reflective | Nostalgic, bittersweet |

### 4.6 Contextual Analysis of Purpose, Tone, and Mood

Analyzing purpose, tone, and mood requires examining multiple textual elements simultaneously. This section teaches you to perform integrated analysis.

#### Step-by-Step Contextual Analysis

**Step 1: Read the entire passage first**
Do not start analyzing after one sentence. Purpose and tone emerge from the whole text.

**Step 2: Identify the subject**
What is the passage about? This gives you context for interpreting tone.

**Step 3: Examine word choice**
Circle or mentally note words with strong connotations (positive or negative). These are your tone indicators.

**Step 4: Check sentence structure**
- Short, declarative sentences → forceful, urgent, or matter-of-fact
- Long, complex sentences → reflective, academic, or descriptive
- Questions → engaging, challenging, or rhetorical (persuasive)
- Exclamations → emotional, enthusiastic, or alarmed

**Step 5: Look for patterns**
Does the author consistently use positive language? Negative? Neutral? Mixed? The pattern reveals the overall tone.

**Step 6: Identify the purpose**
Based on tone + content + structure, determine WHY the author wrote this.

**Step 7: Assess the mood**
How does the combination of all elements make you (the reader) feel?

#### Integrated Analysis Example

**Passage:**
> "Every Filipino child deserves access to quality education — not the overcrowded classrooms and outdated textbooks that too many of our students endure today. While other nations invest billions in educational technology and teacher development, we continue to patch a crumbling system with band-aid solutions. The time for incremental change has passed. We need bold, transformative action, and we need it now."

**Analysis:**

| Element | Observation | What It Reveals |
|---------|-------------|----------------|
| Word choice | "deserves," "endure," "crumbling," "band-aid," "bold, transformative" | Strong emotional language — dissatisfaction with current state, urgency for change |
| Sentence structure | Mix of long (context) and short punchy (calls to action) | Building argument then delivering forceful conclusion |
| Perspective | "We" — inclusive, collective | Author positions themselves with the reader, creating solidarity |
| Evidence type | Comparison with other nations, characterization of current system | One-sided — only problems presented, no acknowledgment of progress |
| Call to action | "The time has passed," "we need it now" | Explicit demand for action |

**Conclusion:**
- Purpose: To persuade (calls to action, one-sided, emotional appeals)
- Tone: Urgent and critical (dissatisfied with status quo, demanding change)
- Mood: Motivated/Indignant (reader feels compelled to agree and act)

### 4.7 Diction and Word Choice

**Diction** — the author's choice of words — is the primary vehicle for tone. Understanding how word choice creates tone is the most practical skill for answering CSE questions.

#### Levels of Diction

| Level | Characteristics | Typical Tone | Example |
|-------|----------------|-------------|---------|
| **Formal** | Academic vocabulary, complex sentences, no contractions | Serious, authoritative, professional | "The committee hereby resolves to allocate additional resources." |
| **Informal** | Conversational, contractions, colloquialisms | Friendly, casual, approachable | "The team figured they'd just throw more people at the problem." |
| **Slang** | Very casual, group-specific language | Humorous, irreverent, youthful | "The whole project was a total dumpster fire." |
| **Technical** | Specialized vocabulary, precise terminology | Expert, analytical, objective | "The algorithm's time complexity is O(n log n)." |

#### Connotation vs. Denotation in Tone

Words with the same denotation (dictionary meaning) can have very different connotations (emotional associations), which create different tones:

| Neutral | Positive Connotation | Negative Connotation |
|---------|---------------------|---------------------|
| thin | slender, lean | scrawny, emaciated |
| old | vintage, classic | decrepit, obsolete |
| talk | discuss, converse | gossip, babble |
| plan | strategy, vision | scheme, plot |
| group | team, community | mob, gang |
| change | reform, progress | upheaval, disruption |
| spend | invest, allocate | waste, squander |

**CSE Application:** When a passage uses "invest" instead of "spend," the author views the expenditure positively. When it uses "squander" instead of "spend," the author views it negatively. Same action — different tone — revealed entirely through word choice.

#### Emotionally Charged vs. Neutral Language

**Neutral:** "The policy was discontinued after three years."
**Positive charge:** "The policy was retired after a successful three-year run."
**Negative charge:** "The policy was scrapped after three years of failure."

All three describe the same event. The charged versions reveal the author's attitude (tone).

#### Identifying Tone Through Diction: Practice

**Passage A:**
> "The senator's proposal addresses a genuine need in our healthcare system."

Tone words: "genuine need," "addresses" → Tone: Supportive/Approving

**Passage B:**
> "The senator's proposal throws money at a problem that requires structural reform, not funding."

Tone words: "throws money," "requires" (implying the proposal doesn't meet the requirement) → Tone: Critical/Dismissive

**Passage C:**
> "The senator introduced a healthcare funding proposal estimated at ₱2.3 billion over five years."

Tone words: None — purely factual language → Tone: Objective/Neutral

### 4.8 Figurative Language and Emotional Effect

Figurative language amplifies tone and mood by creating vivid associations that go beyond literal meaning.

#### How Literary Devices Influence Tone and Mood

**Simile** (comparison using "like" or "as")
> "The bureaucratic process moved like molasses in January."

Effect: Creates a tone of frustration/criticism by comparing the process to something painfully slow. Mood: Impatient, frustrated.

**Metaphor** (direct comparison without "like" or "as")
> "The new policy is a band-aid on a bullet wound."

Effect: Critical tone — implies the policy is woefully inadequate for the severity of the problem. Mood: Concerned, dissatisfied.

**Personification** (giving human qualities to non-human things)
> "The city groaned under the weight of its growing population."

Effect: Creates a tone of concern/sympathy by making the city seem like a suffering entity. Mood: Sympathetic, worried.

**Hyperbole** (deliberate exaggeration)
> "We've been waiting for this permit for a million years."

Effect: Humorous or frustrated tone — exaggeration emphasizes the speaker's impatience. Mood: Amused or sympathetic.

**Imagery** (vivid sensory descriptions)
> "The abandoned school sat silent, its windows dark, its playground overtaken by weeds that reached waist-high."

Effect: Creates a melancholic tone through decay imagery. Mood: Sad, reflective.

**Irony** (saying the opposite of what is meant)
> "The 'efficiency improvement' added three more forms to the application process."

Effect: Sarcastic tone — the quotation marks signal that the author does not believe the label matches reality. Mood: Amused, critical.

#### Recognizing Figurative Language as Tone Signals

On the CSE, figurative language is a strong indicator that the passage is NOT purely informative. Informative writing uses literal, precise language. When you see metaphors, similes, or irony, the author is expressing an attitude — which means the tone is NOT neutral.

**Decision rule:**
- Figurative language present → Tone is NOT objective/neutral
- Determine whether the figurative language is positive or negative
- Match to the appropriate tone word

### 4.9 Author Bias and Perspective

**Bias** is a systematic leaning toward one side of an issue. All authors have perspectives, but bias becomes problematic when it distorts the presentation of information while pretending to be neutral.

#### Subjective vs. Objective Writing

| Feature | Objective Writing | Subjective Writing |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|
| Language | Neutral, precise | Evaluative, emotional |
| Perspective | Third person, detached | First/second person, involved |
| Evidence | Multiple sides presented | One side emphasized |
| Purpose | To inform or explain | To persuade or express |
| Tone | Neutral, analytical | Passionate, critical, enthusiastic |

#### Identifying Bias

**1. Loaded language** — Words chosen for emotional impact rather than precision
> "The reckless policy" vs. "The policy" — "reckless" reveals bias against the policy.

**2. Selective evidence** — Presenting only facts that support one conclusion
> A passage about a government program that mentions only failures and ignores successes reveals negative bias.

**3. Labeling** — Using characterizing terms instead of neutral descriptions
> "Activists" vs. "troublemakers" vs. "concerned citizens" — same people, different bias.

**4. Source selection** — Quoting only critics or only supporters
> If a passage about a new law quotes three opponents and zero supporters, the author's bias is against the law.

**5. Framing** — How information is contextualized
> "The budget was cut by 10%" (negative frame) vs. "90% of the budget was preserved" (positive frame) — same fact, different bias.

#### Bias and Tone Connection

Bias reveals itself through tone. A biased author cannot maintain a truly neutral tone because their word choices will leak their perspective:

- Positive bias → Admiring, supportive, enthusiastic tone
- Negative bias → Critical, dismissive, sarcastic tone
- Neutral (no bias) → Objective, matter-of-fact tone

#### CSE Application

When a question asks about the author's perspective or attitude, look for:
1. Which side does the author seem to favor?
2. What language reveals this favoritism?
3. Is the presentation balanced or one-sided?

### 4.10 Elimination Strategies

When you cannot immediately identify the correct answer, use elimination to remove wrong choices systematically.

#### Strategy 1: Remove Tone Mismatches

If the passage uses positive language, eliminate all negative tone options (and vice versa).

**Passage uses words like:** "promising," "achievement," "progress"
**Eliminate:** critical, pessimistic, bitter, sarcastic

**Passage uses words like:** "failed," "neglected," "inadequate"
**Eliminate:** optimistic, celebratory, enthusiastic, admiring

#### Strategy 2: Identify Unsupported Interpretations

Eliminate answers that require information NOT in the passage.

If the passage does not contain humor → eliminate "humorous" and "lighthearted"
If the passage does not contain anger → eliminate "furious" and "hostile"

#### Strategy 3: Spot Extreme Answer Choices

The CSE rarely uses extreme tones as correct answers. Moderate options are usually correct.

**Extreme (usually wrong):** furious, ecstatic, devastated, enraged
**Moderate (usually correct):** critical, optimistic, concerned, disappointed

Exception: If the passage contains genuinely extreme language, the extreme answer may be correct. But this is rare.

#### Strategy 4: Narrow Contextually Correct Answers

When two options seem close, return to the passage and find the specific words that support one over the other.

**Example:** Is the tone "concerned" or "alarmed"?
- If the passage uses measured language about a problem → concerned
- If the passage uses urgent, emergency-level language → alarmed

#### Strategy 5: Match Purpose to Structure

| If the passage structure is... | The purpose is likely... |
|-------------------------------|------------------------|
| Facts, data, neutral reporting | To inform |
| Argument, call to action, emotional appeals | To persuade |
| Story, characters, dialogue, humor | To entertain |
| Sensory details, imagery, atmosphere | To describe |
| Steps, definitions, how/why | To explain |

#### The "Flip Test" for Tone

If you are unsure whether the tone is positive or negative, try flipping the key words:
- Replace "invested" with "wasted" — does the meaning change? If yes, the original word choice reveals positive tone.
- Replace "reform" with "upheaval" — does the meaning change? If yes, the original word choice reveals the author's supportive attitude.

### 4.11 Common Errors in Purpose and Tone Analysis

#### Error 1: Confusing Tone with Mood

**Wrong thinking:** "The passage makes me feel sad, so the tone is sad."
**Correct thinking:** "The passage makes me feel sad (mood), but the author's tone might be sympathetic, critical, or matter-of-fact — I need to look at the author's word choice, not my emotional response."

#### Error 2: Ignoring Word Choice

**Wrong thinking:** "The passage is about education, so the purpose is to inform."
**Correct thinking:** "The passage is about education, but the author uses words like 'must,' 'unacceptable,' and 'we need to act' — so the purpose is to persuade."

#### Error 3: Misunderstanding the Author's Intention

**Wrong thinking:** "The passage contains facts, so it must be informative."
**Correct thinking:** "The passage contains facts, but they are selectively chosen to support one argument. Facts used as evidence for a position = persuasive purpose."

#### Error 4: Making Unsupported Assumptions

**Wrong thinking:** "The author mentions problems with the healthcare system, so the author must be angry."
**Correct thinking:** "The author mentions problems using measured, analytical language — the tone is concerned or critical, not angry. Anger requires stronger language."

#### Error 5: Focusing on Personal Opinion Instead of Textual Evidence

**Wrong thinking:** "I disagree with the author, so the tone must be wrong/biased."
**Correct thinking:** "Regardless of whether I agree, what does the author's language reveal about THEIR attitude?"

#### Error 6: Choosing the First Plausible Answer

**Wrong thinking:** "Option A seems okay — I'll pick it."
**Correct thinking:** "Let me read ALL options and find the one BEST supported by the passage's language."

### Exam Strategies

#### Quick Purpose Identification (30-Second Method)

1. Read the last sentence — it often reveals purpose most clearly
2. Check for calls to action → Persuade
3. Check for sensory details → Describe
4. Check for story elements → Entertain
5. Check for process/steps → Explain
6. If none of the above → Inform

#### Quick Tone Identification (30-Second Method)

1. Find the strongest adjective or adverb in the passage
2. Is it positive, negative, or neutral?
3. Match to the closest tone word in the answer choices
4. Verify by checking 2-3 other word choices in the passage

#### Time Management

- Purpose questions: 30-45 seconds (structure-based, faster to identify)
- Tone questions: 45-60 seconds (requires careful word analysis)
- Mood questions: 30-45 seconds (overall impression, usually faster)
- Combined questions: 60-90 seconds (requires full analysis)

### Real CSE-Like Examples

#### Easy Example

**Passage:**
> "The Department of Social Welfare and Development distributed relief goods to 5,000 families affected by Typhoon Odette. Each family received rice, canned goods, water, and hygiene kits."

**Question:** What is the author's primary purpose?
**Answer:** To inform
**Explanation:** The passage presents factual information (specific numbers, specific items) without opinion, emotion, or calls to action. Pure reporting.

**Tone:** Objective/Matter-of-fact
**Mood:** Neutral/Informed

#### Medium Example

**Passage:**
> "While the government touts its infrastructure program as 'Build, Build, Build,' communities along the proposed expressway route face displacement without adequate compensation. Families who have lived on their land for generations are being told to leave with barely a month's notice. Progress, it seems, has a price — and it is always paid by those who can least afford it."

**Question:** What is the author's tone?
**Answer:** Critical/Sympathetic (toward displaced families)
**Explanation:** "Touts" (negative connotation for the government's promotion), "barely a month's notice" (emphasizing inadequacy), and the final ironic statement about progress reveal criticism of the government's approach and sympathy for affected families.

**Purpose:** To persuade (one-sided, emotional framing, implied call for better treatment)
**Mood:** Indignant/Sympathetic

#### Hard Example

**Passage:**
> "The commission's report, released quietly on a Friday afternoon — a timing choice that communications professionals will recognize as the burial slot for unfavorable news — concluded that 'procedural irregularities' had occurred but that 'no individual accountability could be established.' One might admire the linguistic craftsmanship required to acknowledge wrongdoing while simultaneously ensuring that no one is blamed for it."

**Question:** What is the author's tone?
**Answer:** Sarcastic/Ironic
**Explanation:** "Released quietly on a Friday afternoon" with the parenthetical explanation signals awareness of media manipulation. "One might admire the linguistic craftsmanship" is clearly sarcastic — the author does not actually admire it. The entire passage uses irony to criticize the commission's evasion of accountability.

**Purpose:** To persuade (through satire — the author wants the reader to see through the commission's language)
**Mood:** Cynical/Amused

### Mini Practice Set

**1.** "The city government launched a tree-planting campaign last Saturday. Over 500 volunteers participated and planted 2,000 seedlings along the riverbank."
- **Question:** What is the author's purpose?
- **Answer:** To inform — factual reporting with specific numbers, no opinion.

**2.** "We cannot stand by while our rivers are poisoned by industrial waste. Every citizen must demand stricter environmental regulations before it is too late."
- **Question:** What is the author's purpose?
- **Answer:** To persuade — emotional language ("poisoned"), call to action ("must demand"), urgency ("before it is too late").

**3.** "Lola Ising sat on her wooden bench, watching the sunset paint the sky in shades of orange and purple. The smell of cooking rice drifted from the kitchen, and somewhere a radio played an old kundiman."
- **Question:** What is the author's purpose?
- **Answer:** To describe — sensory details (sight, smell, sound), imagery, atmosphere creation.

**4.** "The manager's memo stated that 'all employees are valued team members.' This was distributed the same day that 30 positions were eliminated without notice."
- **Question:** What is the author's tone?
- **Answer:** Ironic — the juxtaposition of "valued" with mass termination reveals the author's skepticism about the memo's sincerity.

**5.** "After years of neglect, the historic church has finally been restored to its former glory. The community can once again take pride in this architectural treasure."
- **Question:** What is the author's tone?
- **Answer:** Appreciative/Celebratory — "finally," "former glory," "take pride," "treasure" all express positive regard.

**6.** "The rain fell steadily on the tin roof, a rhythmic drumming that made the small room feel even smaller. Outside, the street had become a river of brown water."
- **Question:** What mood does the passage create?
- **Answer:** Gloomy/Confined — the steady rain, small room, and flooding create a sense of being trapped and melancholic.

**7.** "Studies show that students who eat breakfast perform 20% better on standardized tests. Breakfast provides essential glucose for brain function and sustained concentration."
- **Question:** What is the author's purpose?
- **Answer:** To explain — presents a cause-effect relationship with supporting scientific reasoning.

**8.** "The senator called the opposition's plan 'innovative.' Coming from someone who has blocked every reform bill for the past decade, the compliment rings hollow."
- **Question:** What is the author's tone?
- **Answer:** Skeptical/Sarcastic — the contrast between the compliment and the senator's track record suggests the author doubts the sincerity.

**9.** "Manila, with its 13 million residents, is one of the most densely populated cities in the world. The metropolitan area covers approximately 620 square kilometers."
- **Question:** What is the author's tone?
- **Answer:** Objective/Neutral — presents verifiable statistics without evaluative language.

**10.** "The children's laughter echoed through the park as they chased each other around the fountain. Parents watched from shaded benches, smiling at the simple joy of a Sunday afternoon."
- **Question:** What mood does the passage create?
- **Answer:** Cheerful/Warm — laughter, play, smiling parents, and "simple joy" create a lighthearted atmosphere.

**11.** "It is unconscionable that in a country with abundant natural resources, millions of families still lack access to clean drinking water. Our leaders must be held accountable."
- **Question:** What is the author's tone?
- **Answer:** Indignant — "unconscionable" expresses moral outrage, and "must be held accountable" demands action.

**12.** "The old fisherman mended his net by the light of a kerosene lamp, his weathered hands moving with the certainty of fifty years' practice."
- **Question:** What is the author's purpose?
- **Answer:** To describe — focuses on creating a visual scene with sensory details and atmosphere.

**13.** "The committee met for six hours and produced a twelve-page report recommending further study. Decisive leadership, clearly, is alive and well."
- **Question:** What is the author's tone?
- **Answer:** Sarcastic — "Decisive leadership, clearly, is alive and well" means the opposite; the author is mocking the committee's inaction.

**14.** "Voter turnout in the last barangay election was 45%, down from 62% five years ago. Political analysts attribute the decline to voter fatigue and disillusionment."
- **Question:** What is the author's purpose?
- **Answer:** To inform — presents data and attributes analysis to experts without personal opinion.

**15.** "The abandoned hospital loomed at the end of the street, its broken windows like empty eye sockets staring into the darkness."
- **Question:** What mood does the passage create?
- **Answer:** Eerie/Ominous — the personification ("eye sockets staring"), darkness, and abandonment create a threatening atmosphere.

**16.** "Our barangay health workers deserve more than our gratitude — they deserve fair compensation, proper equipment, and the respect of a nation that depends on their service."
- **Question:** What is the author's purpose?
- **Answer:** To persuade — advocates for specific actions (compensation, equipment, respect) using emotional appeal.

**17.** "The quarterly report indicates a 3.2% increase in revenue and a 1.8% decrease in operating costs compared to the previous quarter."
- **Question:** What is the author's tone?
- **Answer:** Matter-of-fact — purely numerical reporting with no evaluative language.

**18.** "Mang Pedro looked at the foreclosure notice, then at the house his father had built with his own hands sixty years ago. He folded the paper carefully and placed it in his pocket without a word."
- **Question:** What mood does the passage create?
- **Answer:** Somber/Poignant — the contrast between family history and loss, combined with Pedro's silent acceptance, creates sadness.

**19.** "The new shopping mall is, without question, the ugliest building ever constructed in this city. Its concrete facade clashes with every surrounding structure."
- **Question:** What is the author's tone?
- **Answer:** Harsh/Critical — "ugliest building ever," "without question," and "clashes" express strong negative judgment.

**20.** "To understand how a bill becomes a law in the Philippines, we must first examine the legislative process. A bill begins when a member of Congress files it with the Secretary General."
- **Question:** What is the author's purpose?
- **Answer:** To explain — introduces a process with sequential structure and educational framing.

### Quick Recap

| Concept | Definition | Key Question |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| **Author's Purpose** | Why the author wrote the passage | "What does the author want to achieve?" |
| **Tone** | Author's attitude toward the subject | "How does the author feel about this?" |
| **Mood** | Emotional atmosphere experienced by reader | "How does this passage make the reader feel?" |

**Five Purposes:** Inform, Persuade, Entertain, Describe, Explain

**Tone is revealed by:** Word choice, sentence structure, figurative language, formality level, detail selection

**Mood is created by:** Setting, imagery, pacing, sensory details, atmosphere

**Critical distinction:** Tone = author's feeling (sender). Mood = reader's feeling (receiver).
